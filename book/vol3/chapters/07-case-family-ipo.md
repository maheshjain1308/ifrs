# Chapter 7: Case: Calder & Sons, a Family Firm Considers an IPO

*Illustrative case. Calder & Sons Building Supply, Inc., its owners, its advisers, its peers and all figures are fictional and were constructed for teaching. Nothing here describes a real company, and no figure should be read as market data. Dollar amounts are in millions unless stated.*

## The Situation

On the morning of Thursday, 4 November 2026, Margaret Calder opened the envelope that every chief executive of a family company eventually receives. It was a letter, three pages long, from the investment bank Lindqvist Rowe, and it said in carefully lawyered language what the bank's coverage officer had been saying over lunch for a year: the market for building-products companies was open, the public peers were trading at their highest multiples in three years, and Calder & Sons Building Supply, Inc. ("Calder") could probably complete an initial public offering ("IPO") in the first quarter of 2027 at an equity value "in the range of one billion dollars."

Margaret, 52, is Calder's chief executive and a third-generation owner. The company was founded in 1952 by her grandfather Walter Calder as a single lumberyard in the Mid-Lakes region. Today it designs and manufactures engineered millwork (doors, windows and stair systems) in two plants, and distributes those products and a broad range of third-party building materials through 41 branches. It employs about 2,900 people. It has never had an outside shareholder. Its debt is owed to a bank syndicate and to a small group of insurance companies that hold private notes. Its dividend policy, for as long as anyone can remember, has been "forty percent of earnings, paid quarterly, and never cut."

The ownership of the company's 50.0 million shares is spread over four holders who do not always agree.

- **Margaret Calder** holds 17.0 million shares (34%). She runs the business and wants capital to expand it.
- **Hugh Calder**, her father, 81, is chairman and holds 14.0 million shares (28%). He built the distribution network. He wants the family to keep control "for at least another generation," and he is also thinking about his estate, which is almost entirely Calder stock.
- **The Calder Family Trust** holds 11.0 million shares (22%) for the benefit of Margaret's children and Hugh's other grandchildren. The trustee is an independent corporate trustee, which votes the shares in consultation with Margaret.
- **The Eastbrook branch**, descendants of Walter's second son, hold 8.0 million shares (16%) through Nora and Tomas Calder-Hale. They are not involved in management. Both are in their sixties, both have said clearly that they want to convert part of their holding into cash, and Tomas has twice raised the idea of a sale of the whole company.

The board has seven members: Margaret, Hugh, Nora Calder-Hale, the chief financial officer Anil Varghese, and three independent directors, one of whom, Beatrice Okafor, a retired treasurer of a listed industrial company, is the lead independent director. At its meeting on Friday, 19 November 2026, the board must decide four things.

1. **Whether to authorise a confidential submission** of a registration statement so that an IPO could price in the first quarter of 2027, or to wait.
2. **What range of prices is defensible**, and how large the offering should be. Lindqvist Rowe has proposed $225 million in total: $150 million of new shares sold by the company (primary) and $75 million of existing shares sold by family members (secondary).
3. **Whether to adopt a dual-class structure**, with the family's shares carrying ten votes each and the shares sold to the public carrying one vote each, and if so with what protections for public investors.
4. **Whether an IPO is the right route at all**, given two alternatives that arrived in the last month: a proposal from a private equity firm to buy a minority stake, and an informal indication of interest from a strategic buyer.

There is a hard timetable behind the decision. Calder's most recent financial statements cover the period ended 30 September 2026. For purposes of this case, assume the registration rules treat interim statements as stale 135 days after the period end, which is 12 February 2027. A deal priced after that date needs audited FY2026 numbers, which the auditors expect to deliver in the first half of March. The bankers therefore describe two practical windows: a pricing in the first half of February on the September numbers (too tight, in the view of the company's counsel), or a pricing in the second half of March on audited year-end numbers. If the March window is missed, the next realistic window is after the second-quarter numbers are available, in September 2027.

Margaret's own view is that the company has outgrown private ownership. Calder's capital budget has been constrained by the family's reluctance to borrow more, and she sees acquisition targets among regional distributors that she cannot buy with the family's cash flow. Hugh's view is that public markets "reward quarter-to-quarter behavior and punish long-term thinking." The independent directors want a clear, quantified answer to three questions: what the company is worth, what the family gives up, and what is lost by waiting. Anil Varghese has been asked to prepare the analysis for the meeting. You are advising him.

### Who decides what

It helps to be explicit about who holds which levers, because the case turns as much on governance as on valuation. The company's charter requires a simple majority of the board for an IPO authorisation, but the shareholders' agreement, which dates from 1998, requires holders of 75% of the shares to approve any change in share classes or any issuance that would reduce the family's aggregate ownership below 60%. No single holder has 75%, and any holder with more than 25% (Margaret at 34% or Hugh at 28%) can block alone. Margaret and Hugh together have 62%; adding the trust gives 84%, so the Eastbrook branch cannot block if the other three are aligned. In practice, the real constraint is not legal but personal: Hugh will not support a structure that lets the family lose control, and Nora and Tomas will not support a structure that keeps their capital locked up indefinitely.

## Industry Context

Calder operates in the middle of the building-products value chain. Upstream are lumber mills, glass and hardware makers, and fabricators of engineered wood. Downstream are professional contractors and home builders, and, at the margin, retail customers. Calder's economics blend two models.

**Distribution** (about 71% of revenue) buys products from many suppliers and sells them, with delivery and credit, to professional contractors. Gross margins are in the low-to-mid twenties of revenue, working capital is heavy (receivables and inventory together exceed a quarter of sales), and capital expenditure is modest. Returns come from density: each additional branch added to a metropolitan area lowers delivery cost per order and increases the share of a contractor's purchases that Calder captures. Distribution is consolidating, and larger players pay substantial premiums for regional networks.

**Manufacturing** (about 29% of revenue) produces made-to-order millwork. Gross margins are higher, in the mid-thirties of revenue, but the business needs plants, automation and skilled labour, and it is more exposed to input prices. Calder's plants run at roughly 80% of capacity, and the company's capital plan includes automation of the stair line and the construction of a third plant within five years.

Demand for both lines follows residential repair and remodelling, new housing starts and commercial fit-out, all of which are sensitive to interest rates and consumer confidence. Revenue is seasonal: the second and third quarters are the strongest, and the first quarter is the weakest. Calder's EBITDA margin has been remarkably stable through the recent cycle, which the management team attributes to its mix of repair work and new construction and to the price discipline of the distribution branches. Investors will test that claim.

**The public peers.** Six listed companies are the natural comparables, and each differs from Calder in ways that matter for valuation. Ashgrove and Corvane are larger and more diversified; Brightwater and Eastfield are mostly distributors, with lower margins and higher leverage; Delmar is the only manufacturer of similar scale; Fallowgate sells materials to commercial builders. All six are larger than Calder by market value except Delmar, which is slightly smaller. None has a dual-class structure, although Corvane has a controlling shareholder. Over the last year the peer group's median multiple of enterprise value to next-twelve-months EBITDA has risen from about 8.1 times to about 9.0 times, as investors have become more confident that interest rates have peaked.

**The IPO market.** Three comparable companies completed IPOs during the last eighteen months (see the exhibits). The market clearly values scale, visible growth and a credible plan for using proceeds. It has been less forgiving of offerings that consist mostly of selling shareholders cashing out, and of governance structures that leave public holders with little voice. Investors in this sector tend to be long-only funds that compare a new issue to the existing peers on cash-flow yield, so the price at which the new shares can be sold is strongly anchored to where the peers trade on the day.

**A note on dual-class structures.** In a dual-class structure the company issues two kinds of common stock that differ in voting rights but share equally in dividends and liquidation proceeds. Founders and families use them to raise equity capital while keeping control. Critics argue that they separate cash-flow rights from control and weaken accountability. Public investors in some markets price this by paying less for the low-vote shares; the size of the discount varies with the circumstances and with the quality of safeguards such as sunset clauses, which convert high-vote shares to ordinary shares after a period or upon a trigger. Some index providers and proxy advisers apply special rules to multi-class companies; in a real transaction the current policies should be checked with counsel. Volume 1, Chapter 24 introduced these governance issues, and Volume 1, Chapter 13 covered the mechanics of an IPO, including underwriting spreads and underpricing. Volume 2's treatment of valuation under uncertainty is also relevant when comparing offers made at different dates.

## Complications and Exhibits

The data above are the clean version. The following facts surfaced during the first two weeks of preparation, and a good analysis has to decide how far each of them matters.

**1. "Adjusted" EBITDA.** Management presents "adjusted EBITDA" to bankers. For FY2025 it adds back $3.1 million for a one-time enterprise-system implementation cost and $2.2 million of family-related expenses (vehicles, a corporate aircraft share and the salaries of two relatives who do not work in the business), giving $114.8 million against reported EBITDA of $109.5 million. The Exhibit 1 figures are reported, not adjusted, and the forecast in Exhibit 4 assumes the family expenses continue. Lindqvist Rowe's valuation letter is based on adjusted figures. A public investor will accept the system add-back only if it is demonstrably one-off, will probably accept the family add-back only if the expenses actually stop, and will insist on deducting the costs of being a public company, which Anil estimates at $3.5 million a year (directors, audit and controls, listing and investor relations).

**2. Related-party leases.** Six branches are leased from a family partnership in which Hugh and Nora hold interests. An independent appraiser estimates that the rents are about $1.2 million a year above market. Investors will ask for the leases to be reset at the IPO. The reset would raise EBITDA by $1.2 million, but it would reduce the income of two selling shareholders.

**3. The notes.** The $85.0 million of senior notes carry a make-whole premium that Anil estimates at 4.5% of principal if prepaid, about $3.8 million. This is the reason the proposed use of proceeds repays only the term loan. The insurance companies holding the notes have said informally that they would welcome a lower-leverage Calder and would waive the premium if the notes were refinanced into a public bond later, but they have not put this in writing.

**4. Management equity.** Besides the 1.5 million existing options at $9.00, the compensation committee proposes a post-IPO equity plan of 2.4 million shares, about 4% of the post-IPO share count, to retain senior managers who will be asked to stay through a lock-up. The plan is not in the forecast cost base. Assume that, at today's pricing, the annual expense is about $2.0 million after tax.

**5. Lock-ups.** The underwriters require all existing holders to agree not to sell for 180 days after pricing. The Eastbrook branch has asked whether it can sell a further tranche earlier if the stock trades above the offer price. Lindqvist Rowe says an early release clause usually weakens the book building because investors read it as a signal that the sellers are in a hurry.

**6. The over-allotment option.** The underwriters can sell up to 15% more shares than the base deal if demand is strong and then buy shares back in the market or exercise an option to buy additional shares from the company. If exercised in full at $17.50, the company would sell another 1.286 million shares ($22.5 million) and the family would hold fewer votes. The base case assumes it is not exercised.

**7. Tax on sales.** Assume the sellers pay tax at 20% on capital gains, with a tax basis of $1.00 per share, and that the sale proceeds are net of the 6.0% spread. Hugh's advisers note that under the tax rules assumed for this case, shares held at death receive a new tax basis equal to the market value at that date, which would eliminate the gain for his heirs. This makes Hugh's secondary sale more costly in after-tax terms than it appears, and it is the reason he has suggested that the secondary be borne entirely by the Eastbrook branch.

**8. The strategic indication.** The Marlow Group indication is informal. Marlow has asked for sixty days of exclusivity and access to detailed branch-level data. Tomas Calder-Hale supports it. Margaret says that if Calder is sold she will retire, and she believes that three of the five senior managers would leave. The strategic proposal is expressed on an LTM basis; Marlow has not said whether it would assume the notes or require them to be repaid, and has not said who would bear the make-whole premium.

**9. The private equity proposal.** Redfield's price is expressed as an enterprise value of 8.0 times FY2026E EBITDA of $115.6 million. The firm says that the control rights are "standard." Margaret's counsel says that the consent right over acquisitions would stop the family from doing precisely the deals that motivate the transaction.

**10. A data conflict.** The Lindqvist Rowe letter refers to peer valuation "at about 9.5 times EBITDA." The median of the six peers in Exhibit 6 is lower than that, and the mean is lower still. The letter appears to use the highest multiples in the set, or EBITDA before certain peer-specific costs; the bank has not been asked to explain. Another discrepancy: the letter quotes a price range of $18 to $22 per share, which cannot be reproduced from the data in Exhibits 4 to 6 without ignoring an IPO discount.

**11. Timing scenarios.** The board wants to compare pricing in the second half of March 2027 (Option A) with pricing in September 2027 (Option B). Management has prepared the following assumptions. The fully diluted pre-IPO share count for valuation purposes is 51.5 million, which is 50.0 million shares plus the 1.5 million options treated as exercised with $13.5 million of exercise proceeds. The offer price in each scenario is

```
Offer price = [ (Multiple × NTM EBITDA − Net debt + 13.5) / 51.5 ] × (1 − 10%) × (1 − 3%)
```

where 10% is the IPO discount and 3% is the dual-class governance discount. If the window is closed and the deal cannot be priced as planned, the family achieves a downsized or private-placement alternative worth 80% of the price the scenario would have produced. Expected prices are discounted to today at the 10.5% cost of equity from Exhibit 5.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Build a valuation range from trading multiples and a discounted cash flow, and reconcile the methods rather than average them blindly.
2. Translate a valuation into an offer price by applying an IPO discount and a governance discount, and explain what each discount represents.
3. Measure the dilution a public offering imposes: ownership dilution, earnings-per-share dilution and the value transferred by underpricing.
4. Analyse a dual-class structure quantitatively: voting power, the sell-down path before control is lost, and the cost in valuation of weaker governance.
5. Evaluate IPO timing under uncertainty using probability-weighted outcomes, discounting and a break-even multiple.
6. Compare an IPO with a minority sale, a sale of the company and a leveraged recapitalisation, and defend a recommendation to a board that includes divided owners.

### Solution to Question 6: Dual-class design

**Voting power after the IPO.** Family shares: 45.714 million (Class B). Public shares: 12.857 million (Class A).

| Class B votes per share | Total votes (m) | Family votes (%) | Family economic ownership (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 58.571 | 78.0 | 78.0 |
| 5 | 241.43 | 94.7 | 78.0 |
| 10 | 470.00 | 97.3 | 78.0 |

At the IPO, the family holds a clear majority of the votes in all three cases. A dual-class structure adds little to control in the first year. Its value is in the future.

**Maximum sell-down.** Let s be the total number of shares the family sells (the IPO secondary plus later sales) with the primary offering of 8.571 million fixed. Family votes exceed 50% as long as r × (50 − s) > (8.571 + s), where r is the Class B vote ratio.

| Class B votes per share | Maximum total family sales (m) | Further sales beyond the IPO secondary (m) | Family economic stake at that point (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20.7 | 16.4 | 50.0 |
| 5 | 40.2 | 36.0 | 16.7 |
| 10 | 44.7 | 40.4 | 9.1 |

A simple rule of thumb: the family keeps voting control as long as its economic stake exceeds 1/(r + 1) of the shares, which is 50% for one vote, 16.7% for five votes and 9.1% for ten votes.

**Cost of the governance discount.** The family's 45.714 million retained shares are worth $829.4 million at an offer price of $18.14 (no governance discount), against $804.6 million at 3%, $788.0 million at 5% and $763.1 million at 8%. The cost is therefore **$24.8 million, $41.4 million and $66.3 million** respectively. Since the discount depends on the strength of the safeguards, the structure should be designed with the safeguards that minimise the discount.

**Recommendation.** Class B should carry five votes, not ten. Five votes keep control until the family's stake falls to 16.7%, well beyond the sell-down the family contemplates, and the structure is easier to explain and defend; ten votes buy a further 7.6 points of cushion that the family does not expect to need and invite a larger discount. Exhibit 11 should be strengthened with: a **time-based sunset** (conversion of Class B to Class A twelve years after the IPO, extendable by a vote of Class A holders); an **ownership-based sunset** (automatic conversion when Class B shares fall below 15% of total shares outstanding); **conversion on transfer** outside a defined family group (already in the draft); an **equal-treatment clause** so that Class A holders receive the same per-share consideration in any sale of the company; and a **nominating committee of independent directors**. Each of these costs the family little today and reduces the probability that investors demand a larger discount.

**Is the family one bloc?** Not legally. The dual-class structure gives control to the Class B holders as a group but says nothing about how they decide among themselves. The analysis therefore points to a **voting agreement or family governance charter** among the Class B holders covering: how the board nominees are chosen (for example by a majority of Class B votes), a right of first refusal when a family member sells, a rule on which Class B transfers are permitted, procedures to resolve a deadlock between Margaret and Hugh, and what happens when Hugh dies and his shares pass to several heirs. Without such an agreement a split among the family would hand the decisive vote to the independent directors or to the public holders, which is the outcome Hugh fears most.

### Solution to Question 8: Use of proceeds and capital structure

**Net primary proceeds** = 150.0 − 9.0 (spread) − 5.5 (expenses) = **$135.5 million**. Of this, $110.0 million repays the term loan (leaving $10.0 million of term loan and $85.0 million of notes, or $95.0 million of debt) and $25.5 million is added to cash.

| | Before IPO (end-2026E) | After IPO |
|---|---|---|
| Debt | 205.0 | 95.0 |
| Cash | 31.0 | 56.5 |
| Net debt | 174.0 | 38.5 |
| Net debt / FY2026E EBITDA | 1.51x | 0.33x |
| Peer median net debt / EBITDA | 1.80x | 1.80x |

**Is Calder under-levered?** Yes, on paper. At 0.33x it sits far below the peer median of 1.80x and the 20% debt-to-value target in the WACC. But the $25.5 million is earmarked for automation and acquisitions; spending it, and using debt for further acquisitions, would take leverage back toward 1.0x to 1.5x. The company should be explicit about this in the prospectus, since a public investor who sees an unlevered balance sheet will ask why the company sold more equity than it needed.

**Leveraged recapitalisation.** Raising net debt to a multiple of FY2026E EBITDA (115.6) and holding cash at 31.0:

| Target net debt/EBITDA | Net debt | Incremental borrowing | Gross debt | Interest at 7.0% | FY2027E EBITDA / interest | Covenant headroom (EBITDA fall before 3.0x) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2.0x | 231.2 | 57.2 | 262.2 | 18.4 | 6.9x | 33.3% |
| 2.5x | 289.0 | 115.0 | 320.0 | 22.4 | 5.6x | 16.7% |

A recapitalisation to 2.5x could fund a $115.0 million dividend, which exceeds the $75.0 million secondary and costs no underwriting spread, and the family keeps 100% of the votes. It would also increase interest expense by 22.4 − 13.3 = $9.1 million before tax and leave headroom of only 16.7% of EBITDA against the 3.0x covenant, in a cyclical business. It gives no growth capital, no acquisition currency and no price discovery, and the make-whole premium on the notes (about $3.8 million) would arise if the notes were refinanced. The recapitalisation is a real alternative for liquidity but not for growth.

### Solution to Question 9: Timing

**Offer prices.** Offer price = [(multiple × EBITDA − net debt + 13.5) / 51.5] × 0.90 × 0.97.

| Option | Multiple | Probability | Offer price ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (March 2027) | 9.0x | 50% | 16.53 |
| A | 8.0x | 30% | 14.39 |
| A | 10.0x | 20% | 18.67 |
| B (September 2027) | 9.0x | 45% | 17.68 |
| B | 7.8x | 35% | 15.01 |
| B | 10.2x | 20% | 20.35 |

Worked example, A at 9.0x: 9.0 × 126.2 = 1,135.8; less 174.0 = 961.8; plus 13.5 = 975.3; divided by 51.5 = 18.94; times 0.873 = 16.53.

**Expected values.** A: 0.5 × 16.53 + 0.3 × 14.39 + 0.2 × 18.67 = **$16.32**. Allowing for an 8% chance of a closed window with outcome worth 80%: 16.32 × (0.92 + 0.08 × 0.80) = 16.32 × 0.984 = **$16.06**. Present value over 0.375 years at 10.5%: 16.06 / 1.105^0.375 = **$15.47**.

B: 0.45 × 17.68 + 0.35 × 15.01 + 0.20 × 20.35 = **$17.28**. With a 15% closed-window risk: 17.28 × (0.85 + 0.15 × 0.80) = 17.28 × 0.97 = **$16.76**. Present value over 0.875 years: 16.76 / 1.105^0.875 = **$15.36**.

**Conclusion.** Option A is better by $0.11 per share (0.7%), a small margin. The break-even multiple at which Option B equals Option A in present value is **8.88x**, only 0.15x below today's 9.03x peer median. Waiting is therefore only worth it if one believes multiples will be at least roughly flat. The weight of risk (a longer exposure to a window that may close, and more time for a bad quarter at Calder) tilts the decision toward March. **Weaknesses:** the scenario probabilities are subjective; the 80% fallback is arbitrary; the cost of equity is applied to a single cash flow when the family's real alternative is to continue to own the shares; the analysis ignores the option value of using the window to complete one of the acquisitions first; and the multiples in the two options are not independent of each other (a bad March makes a bad September more likely). The right use of the calculation is to show that the case for waiting is weak, not to claim a precise answer.

The comparison of an IPO at $17.50 with a sale at $19.02 is not one of price alone: in the IPO the family keeps 78% of a company whose value is expected to rise (the intrinsic value after the IPO is $19.54) and defers tax on the retained stake; in the sale it receives a certain amount now and ends its role in the business. For a board whose chief executive says she will retire on a sale, and whose chairman wants control, the strategic route has the lowest value to the people who must decide it, but should be kept alive as a price check.

### Sensitivity discussion

The offer price is most sensitive to the market multiple. One turn of EV/EBITDA on FY2027E EBITDA of $126.2 million is worth 126.2 / 51.5 = $2.45 per share before discounts and $2.14 after the 10% and 3% discounts, which is why Question 9 turns on a 0.15x gap. Each percentage point of IPO discount is worth about $0.20 per share (20.16 × 0.01 × 0.97), and the 3% governance discount is worth about $0.54 per share at the mid-point price before the IPO discount, or $24.8 million on the family's retained shares. The $3.5 million public-company cost is worth $0.61 per share before discounts. The DCF is sensitive mainly to WACC: moving from 8.8% to 9.3% lowers the value by $1.83 per share at 2.5% growth. Taken together, no plausible single change moves the offer price outside a $15 to $20 band, but the combination of a weaker market (8.0x), a 15% IPO discount and a 5% governance discount produces a price of about $13.31, a fall of 24% from the base case. At that price the company would have to issue about 11.3 million shares instead of 8.6 million to raise the same $150 million, an increase of 31%.

### Common student errors

1. **Averaging methods without weighting or reconciling them**, or using the peer maximum multiple, as the bank letter does.
2. **Applying the EV multiple to equity**, or forgetting to subtract net debt (and to use the pre-IPO net debt in a pre-money valuation).
3. **Ignoring the options** in the share count or counting all 1.5 million as new shares without exercise proceeds.
4. **Confusing the IPO discount with the dual-class discount**, or applying both to the wrong base.
5. **Treating the primary and secondary the same way** in the dilution analysis; only primary shares add to the share count and raise cash for the company.
6. **Computing votes as if Class B shares were sold at ten votes** to the public (shares sold by the family convert to Class A).
7. **Stopping at the spread** when estimating the cost of the IPO and omitting underpricing.
8. **Describing dual-class as a pure governance evil or pure benefit**, instead of quantifying the control it buys and its cost.
9. **Discounting the expected price at the WACC** in the timing analysis (the cash flow is to equity holders, so the cost of equity is appropriate).
10. **Mixing LTM and NTM** multiples between the Marlow offer and the peer multiples.

### Alternative defensible answers

*Single-class structure.* The family holds 78% after the IPO and could sell 16.4 million more shares before losing a majority. A single class avoids the governance discount ($25 to $41 million), the proxy-adviser and index issues, and the symbolism. Its drawback is that the sell-down the Eastbrook branch wants, plus acquisitions, would put control at risk within a few years. A board that expects the family to stay above 50% can defensibly choose it.

*Delay.* A board that values the March window less than the opportunity to complete the first acquisitions before listing, and so present a larger, more diversified company, could choose September. The numbers do not strongly prefer either date.

*Sale to Marlow.* If Margaret were willing to leave and Hugh to give up control, a firm offer near 10.0 times LTM EBITDA is above the IPO price per share and certain. This is a rational choice for owners whose objective is wealth rather than continuity.

*Smaller IPO.* Eastbrook alone sells (3.0 million shares, $52.5 million) and Hugh does not sell, for tax reasons, producing a $202.5 million offering with a free float of 19.8% of shares. The risk is thin trading.
