## The Situation

On the evening of Tuesday, 3 November 2026, Victor Aldana, the chief executive of Sterling Allied Industries, read a nine-page letter from Redwater Partners for the third time. The letter had arrived that afternoon by courier and by email, addressed to the full board. It opened politely and closed bluntly. Redwater, which had quietly accumulated 6.2 percent of Sterling Allied's shares over fourteen months, argued that the company was "a collection of four good-to-excellent businesses held together by habit and a corporate center that no longer earns its keep." It demanded that the board announce, within sixty days, a plan to separate the company into four independent, listed companies within twelve months. If the board declined, Redwater would nominate four of the eleven directors at the annual meeting. The nomination window closed on Friday, 15 January 2027.

Sterling Allied is a diversified industrial company headquartered in Pittsburgh with fiscal 2026 revenue of roughly $9.5 billion. It was assembled over six decades by acquisition. Its four divisions have little in common except a shared name, a shared balance sheet and a shared head office.

**Halvard Precision** (Aerospace and Defense Components) makes machined and forged components, actuators and sealing systems for commercial and military aircraft. It is the crown jewel: high margin, high return on capital, growing at 7 percent a year on a long order backlog, and valued by the public market at the highest multiples in the group's peer sets.

**Kestrel Flow Systems** (Industrial Flow) manufactures pumps, valves and flow-control systems for water, energy and process industries. It is a steady, mid-cycle business with a large installed base and a profitable aftermarket service stream. Its returns are adequate and its growth is moderate.

**Marlowe Specialty Materials** produces engineered resins, coatings and specialty chemicals. It is cyclical, capital intensive and exposed to commodity feedstock prices. It earns healthy EBITDA margins but, because of the capital it ties up, returns that have been below its cost of capital for four consecutive years.

**Hearthstone Living** (Consumer Home Products) sells kitchen and bath fixtures, small appliances and home storage through home-improvement chains and a growing direct-to-consumer channel. It is the group's only consumer business, has a different customer base, a different seasonality, a different working-capital profile and, by common agreement inside the company, the weakest strategic fit.

Aldana, who has led Sterling Allied for six years, had not been surprised by the letter, only by its timing. The board had discussed "portfolio simplification" at its September retreat and had asked the finance team for a preliminary analysis. That work had been overtaken by events in the last three weeks. On 14 October, Colleen Odom, the chief executive of Larkin Brands, a consumer products group, had telephoned Aldana and followed up with a non-binding proposal to acquire Hearthstone Living for $2.9 billion in cash. And the investment bank advising the board, Brandt Weiss & Co., had begun sounding out the market on whether a partial initial public offering of Halvard Precision could achieve a premium valuation.

So four distinct paths were suddenly on the table, each championed by a different constituency:

1. Redwater's path: a full four-way separation, presumably through a sequence of spin-offs and sales.
2. A spin-off of Hearthstone Living alone, tax-free to shareholders, favored by two of the independent directors and by a faction of senior executives who wanted to keep the industrial businesses together.
3. A cash sale of Hearthstone to Larkin Brands, favored by the chief financial officer, Priya Natarajan, who wanted the cash to reduce debt.
4. A carve-out initial public offering of a minority stake in Halvard Precision, favored by the investment bankers and by Halvard's own management.

Helen Marchetti, the lead independent director, had asked Aldana and Natarajan to present a joint recommendation, with full supporting analysis, at a special board meeting on Thursday, 19 November 2026. Marchetti's instruction was specific. She did not want a sermon on strategy. She wanted to know, per Sterling Allied share, what each path was worth after dis-synergies, taxes and costs; how confident the finance team was in that number; what happened if the market did not re-rate the stock the way Redwater said it would; and what the board should say to Redwater in the meantime.

Natarajan's team had prepared the data in this chapter. It is imperfect, as such data always is. You are asked to act as the finance team and advise the board.

### The people

**Victor Aldana**, chief executive since 2020, a former head of the Kestrel division. He is skeptical that the discount is real and believes Redwater is "pricing in a world without friction." His own compensation is tied to relative total shareholder return over three years and to the group's return on invested capital.

**Priya Natarajan**, chief financial officer since 2022. She favors the sale of Hearthstone because it deleverages the company, but is uneasy about the tax cost and about the risk of a buyer walking away.

**Helen Marchetti**, lead independent director, a retired banker. She chairs the strategy committee and is the board's point of contact with Redwater.

**Jonas Treadwell**, founder of Redwater Partners. His letter cites the divisions' stand-alone peer multiples and an estimate that a separation would release "no less than 35 percent" of equity value. Redwater's holding period at similar companies has historically been two to three years.

**The Sterling family trust** holds 11 percent of the shares and has historically supported management. Its trustee has told Marchetti that the trust "would support any plan that raises the value of our shares and does not break the company in a way we cannot undo."

**Colleen Odom**, chief executive of Larkin Brands, the potential buyer of Hearthstone. Her proposal is non-binding and conditional on financing, due diligence and regulatory clearance. She has asked for an answer, and for exclusivity, within three weeks.

### The decision

By 19 November the board must decide (a) which separation path, if any, to pursue and in what order, (b) whether to open exclusive talks with Larkin, (c) how to respond to Redwater, and (d) how to describe the plan to the market. A public announcement is tentatively planned for the first week of December, so that it precedes the January nomination deadline and gives shareholders time to assess it.

## Industry Context

Diversified industrial groups have been out of fashion for a generation. The investment case for them rests on three ideas, each with a long academic and practical history: that a corporate center can allocate capital more efficiently than external markets, that a diversified group enjoys lower financing costs because its earnings are less volatile, and that shared services and purchasing yield cost savings. The case against rests on three mirror-image ideas: that headquarters allocates capital poorly and cross-subsidizes weak businesses with the cash flow of strong ones, that the market cannot see clearly into a complex group and so applies a "conglomerate discount," and that managers of a portfolio of businesses they only partly understand destroy value through unfocused strategy and bureaucratic reporting.

The empirical literature is mixed on how large the discount is and, more importantly, on why it exists. Some studies find that diversified firms trade at a discount to a portfolio of stand-alone peers; others show that much of the measured discount reflects the characteristics of firms that choose to diversify (they were already poor performers) rather than diversification itself; and still others find that the discount narrows or reverses in some periods. Students of Volume 1, Chapter 22 will recognize the same selection problem that complicates the measurement of acquisition premia. The practical lesson for a board is that a sum-of-the-parts valuation is a starting point for analysis, not a forecast of what shareholders will receive.

Separations in the industrial sector over the past several years have tended to follow a recognizable script. A diversified parent announces a plan to separate one or more businesses. The market reaction on announcement is on average positive but modest. The separated entities then trade on the multiples of focused peers, though usually with some initial pressure: spin-offs are often sold by index funds and by shareholders who do not want the new entity's stock, so the early trading of a newly spun company can be weak. Over the following year, operational improvements (focus, targeted incentives, cleaner capital allocation) are believed to contribute more to value than the multiple re-rating itself. A frequent disappointment is the stranded cost problem: a parent that sheds a division usually finds that a substantial share of the head-office cost allocated to that division does not leave with it.

Three structural routes exist for separating a business, and each has distinct economics:

* **A spin-off** distributes shares of the subsidiary to the parent's shareholders pro rata. It raises no cash for the parent beyond any pre-spin financing the subsidiary borrows and upstreams. Under the stylized tax regime in this case, a qualifying spin-off is tax-free at both the corporate and shareholder level, provided that the business meets an active-business test, that neither entity is acquired in a defined window after the spin, and that the spin is not a device for distributing earnings.
* **A sale** to a strategic or financial buyer raises cash and crystallizes value at a negotiated price that can include a share of the buyer's synergies. It triggers corporate-level tax on the gain over the tax basis, and it removes the business and its risks from the seller permanently.
* **A carve-out** (a minority initial public offering) sells a stake, typically 15 to 30 percent, to public investors while the parent retains control. It raises cash, creates a public price for the subsidiary and a currency for acquisitions and employee equity, and leaves the option of a later full spin-off or sale. It also leaves the parent holding a listed stake, with all the governance conflicts of controlling a partly owned subsidiary, and the stake may itself attract a holding-company discount.

Activist investors have become the principal catalyst for these transactions. A typical campaign begins with a stake of 1 to 10 percent, a published valuation of the parts, a request for board seats, and a threat of a proxy contest. Boards that respond with a credible, well-analyzed alternative generally fare better, in the sense of retaining control of the process, than boards that refuse to engage. But a credible alternative needs a rigorous counter-valuation, which is what this case asks you to build.

Finally, a note on the sectors involved. Aerospace components trade at premium multiples because of long program lives, aftermarket revenue and high barriers to entry. Industrial flow businesses trade at mid-range multiples with a premium for aftermarket mix. Specialty materials trade at a discount because of cyclicality and capital intensity. Branded consumer home products trade in between, with a premium for brands and direct-to-consumer exposure and a discount for retailer concentration. Diversified industrial groups that remain in the market trade at a discount to the weighted average of their parts' sectors, which is the empirical fact on which Redwater's thesis is built.

**Exhibit D: debt capacity.** The agencies' guidance and market evidence suggest that the sustainable leverage of each division as a stand-alone company, measured as net debt to its stand-alone EBITDA, is 2.5x for Halvard, 3.0x for Kestrel, 2.5x for Marlowe and 2.5x for Hearthstone. The diversified group at 3.0x is at the high end of its own investment-grade capacity because its earnings are diversified. Debt in excess of stand-alone capacity must be repaid, and reducing debt that is assumed to be permanent costs the 25 percent interest tax shield on the amount repaid (treat the lost tax shield as permanent debt: its value is the tax rate multiplied by the debt repaid).

**Exhibit E: pension and indemnities.** The $520 million pension deficit belongs to a legacy plan covering Marlowe and Kestrel employees. In every scenario except the four-way breakup it stays with the remaining group. In the four-way breakup it is carried as a debt-like claim of $390 million after tax, with no change in total value.

**Exhibit F: the five-year active-business test.** Hearthstone includes a kitchen-fixtures business, Brightline, acquired in a taxable purchase in March 2023 and now contributing about 25 percent of Hearthstone's EBITDA. Tax counsel advises that Brightline might not satisfy the five-year active-business requirement, but that the remainder of Hearthstone would. Counsel's view is that there is about a 10 percent chance that the spin-off would be treated as taxable at the corporate level. In that event Sterling would owe 25 percent of the excess of SpinCo's fair value over its $1,150 million tax basis.

**Exhibit G: the shared plant.** A plant in Ohio produces components for both Kestrel and Hearthstone. After any separation of Hearthstone, a ten-year supply agreement would apply, which the team has incorporated in the purchasing-synergy loss in Exhibit A. A buyer such as Larkin may be unwilling to take on that contract on identical terms.

**Exhibit H: Redwater's presentation.** Redwater's letter values each division at the median multiples in Table 7 and capitalizes the corporate costs, but ignores stand-alone costs, lost purchasing synergies, separation costs, debt capacity and taxes. It claims upside of at least 35 percent of the share price. It also states that the market applies a conglomerate discount of 28 percent. Check these claims.

**Exhibit I: the diversified peers.** Table 7 shows that the three listed diversified industrial peers trade at a median of 7.6x EBITDA, below Sterling Allied's 8.0x. Redwater's presentation does not mention this.

**Exhibit J: possible delay and failure of the Larkin deal.** If the Larkin sale does not complete (probability 30 percent), the board's best fallback would be a Hearthstone spin-off, delayed by about six months. The finance team estimates that a six-month delay, combined with the cost of an announced but failed sale process, would reduce the per-share value of the fallback by $0.40.

**Exhibit K: the residual discount.** The key uncertainty is how much of a conglomerate discount remains in the shares of the surviving company after a separation. For all single-division separation scenarios (spin, sale, carve-out) the finance team's base case assumes that the surviving, still-diversified group continues to trade at a discount to the sum of its parts of 12 percent of enterprise value, and that the new stand-alone company (the SpinCo or the newly listed Halvard) trades at its fair value with no discount, other than the 10 percent IPO discount that unwinds shortly after listing. For the carve-out, the parent's retained stake in Halvard is assumed to trade at a holding-company discount of 10 percent to the value of the stake. In the four-way breakup the base case assumes no residual discount. Treat these as assumptions to be tested.

**Exhibit L: operational detail on Marlowe.** Marlowe's return on capital has been below its cost of capital for four years. Management has a plan to improve margins by 150 basis points over three years through plant closures costing $90 million. The plan is not in the forecasts of Table 4. Some directors privately say that the real value-creating move is to sell or close Marlowe rather than to spin Hearthstone.

**Exhibit M: timing.** The announcement window and the Redwater nomination deadline compress the timetable. A spin-off of Hearthstone would take about 12 months, so no spin-off could be completed before the 2027 annual meeting. A signed sale agreement or a launched IPO could be announced earlier.

## Assignment Questions

1. **Segment economics.** For each division compute the EBITDA margin, EBIT, after-tax operating profit (NOPAT at 25 percent), return on capital employed, the spread over the division's cost of capital, and the ratio of imputed enterprise value (median multiple) to capital employed. Which divisions create value and which destroy it? What does this suggest about which businesses benefit from being inside the group?
2. **Sum of the parts.** Value each division at the median multiple and at the low and high ends of the selected ranges. Capitalize the corporate costs at the blended multiple. Bridge enterprise value to equity value and to a per-share value, and compute the conglomerate discount on both an enterprise-value and an equity-value basis. Test Redwater's claims (a 28 percent discount; at least 35 percent upside).
3. **Diagnosing the discount.** Compare Sterling Allied's multiple with the diversified peers. Discuss whether the discount is "real," how much of it a separation can capture, and the possible causes (capital allocation, complexity, investor clientele, leverage, return on capital). Perform a discounted cash flow cross-check of Hearthstone Living at WACC of 8.0, 8.5 and 9.0 percent to test the multiple used.
4. **The full breakup.** Estimate the value per share of Redwater's four-way breakup after stand-alone costs, lost purchasing synergies, one-time costs, stand-alone debt capacity and the loss of the interest tax shield. Test sensitivity to a residual 5 percent and 10 percent discount in the post-breakup values.
5. **Capital structure and credit.** Compute the leverage of the group after (a) a Hearthstone spin-off, (b) a sale of Hearthstone to Larkin, and (c) a Halvard carve-out. Evaluate rating, covenant and put-at-101 issues. What should the board do about the SpinCo debt allocation?
6. **Spin-off of Hearthstone.** Value SpinCo and RemainCo, compute the value per Sterling share after costs under the base case, and show how the answer depends on the residual discount that RemainCo suffers and on a possible discount for SpinCo. Incorporate the risk that the spin-off fails the tax test. What residual discount makes the board indifferent to doing nothing?
7. **Sale of Hearthstone to Larkin.** Compute after-tax proceeds, the value per Sterling share under the base case, and the sale price at which the sale is no better than the spin. ## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Build a sum-of-the-parts valuation from segment data and trading comparables, including the treatment of corporate costs, net debt and debt-like claims, and distinguish enterprise-value from equity-value measures of a conglomerate discount.
2. Decompose a gross "break-up value" into the parts that are real (stand-alone costs, lost purchasing scale, lost debt capacity, one-time costs, taxes) and the part that is capturable through a change in how the market prices the shares.
3. Compare a spin-off, a sale and a carve-out on after-tax value per share, speed, reversibility, risk and strategic effect, and explain why the three routes differ in taxation and in who captures the synergies.
4. Analyze the capital-structure and credit consequences of a separation, including debt allocation, agency leverage thresholds and the effect of stranded costs.
5. Use probability weighting, sensitivity analysis and break-even analysis to convert a point estimate into a decision under uncertainty, and communicate that decision to a board.
6. Evaluate an activist's presentation critically, identifying what it omits, and design a response that engages the shareholder without conceding the analysis.

#### Question 3: Diagnosing the discount

Peers. The diversified industrial peers trade at a median of 7.6x EBITDA; Sterling Allied trades at 8.0x. Sterling is therefore not cheap relative to its true comparables; the discount is relative to a hypothetical portfolio of focused companies, and it is also embedded in the sector. A board that takes the diversified-peer multiple as the right benchmark could conclude that the market treats Sterling no worse than any other conglomerate. The relevant question is whether a separation turns each part into a focused company and moves it from the 7.6x set to the focused set. Sterling's implied multiple on the SOTP basis is 14,184.2 / 1,440 = 9.85x, against a market multiple of 8.0x.

Possible causes of the discount, and how much each can be addressed:

* Cross-subsidy and capital allocation: Marlowe's -3.5 point spread is the clearest evidence. Fix by operational improvement, exit or restructuring; separation of the other divisions does not address it.
* Complexity and information: four segments, a corporate center, a shared balance sheet. A spin-off or sale simplifies this and is capturable.
* Investor clientele: aerospace and consumer investors differ; a single stock does not fit anyone's mandate. Partly capturable.
* Leverage and the pension: 3.0x leverage plus a 520 pension deficit limit rerating. Only partly addressed by separation (and may worsen on a spin, see Question 5).
* Real synergies: shared overhead and purchasing power exist; the market is correct to reflect their value, so the portion of the discount corresponding to them is not an inefficiency (see Question 4).

#### Question 5: Capital structure and credit

Starting point: net debt 4,320 / EBITDA 1,440 = 3.0x; with the pension deficit added, (4,320 + 520) / 1,440 = 3.36x. The agency trigger in Table 5 is net debt above 3.5x for two consecutive quarters.

(a) Spin-off. SpinCo borrows 700 and pays it to Sterling, so group net debt falls to 3,620. RemainCo EBITDA is 1,154 (derived in Question 6). RemainCo leverage = 3,620 / 1,154 = 3.14x, up from 3.0x, because the EBITDA that leaves (240 after stand-alone costs) is a larger share of the group than the debt that leaves (700 is 16 percent of debt, while the EBITDA lost is 20 percent). Pension-adjusted: (3,620 + 520) / 1,154 = 3.59x, above the 3.5x level. SpinCo itself starts at 700 / 240 = 2.92x, above the 2.5x capacity in Exhibit D (capacity is 600). The two entities are both too levered; the spin transfers a leverage problem to each, and no allocation of the 4,320 solves it, because total capacity falls (RemainCo at 3.0x supports 3,462, SpinCo at 2.5x supports 600, a total of 4,062 against the group's 4,320 less the pension effect). Board options: suspend discretionary repurchases and slow the dividend; assign debt so that RemainCo is closest to 3.0x (a SpinCo debt of 858 would be needed, 3.6x for SpinCo and not financeable), or use a third source such as Marlowe plant-closure savings or asset sales.

(b) Sale to Larkin. Net proceeds of 2,428.75 (Question 7) retire debt; net debt falls to 1,891.25. Leverage is 1,891.25 / 1,154 = 1.64x. There is headroom to re-lever to 2.5x (net debt of 2,885, extra capacity of 993.75) or to 3.0x (3,462). The sale is the only route that improves credit quality.

(c) Carve-out. Halvard raises 957.5 (2.5x) and upstreams it; Sterling nets 723.7 of IPO proceeds; consolidated net debt = 4,320 - 723.7 = 3,596.3 on consolidated EBITDA of 1,409 (Halvard 383 + Kestrel, Marlowe and Hearthstone 1,128 less 102 of corporate cost), a leverage of 2.55x. Because Halvard's debt is consolidated, the group improves modestly; parent-only net debt is 4,320 - 957.5 - 723.7 = 2,638.8.

Put at 101. The 2031 notes can be put only on a change of control combined with a downgrade to sub-investment grade. A spin-off or a sale of part of the assets is not a change of control as normally defined, so the put is not triggered directly, but indentures also restrict the sale of substantially all assets and require legal review; and a downgrade driven by the spin would raise refinancing costs on the 2028 term loan and the commercial paper program. Illustration: a 75 basis point repricing on 4,900 of debt costs about 36.8 pre-tax a year.

Recommendation: do not set the SpinCo debt at 700 without agency pre-clearance; pre-clear a ratings view and target RemainCo net debt / EBITDA of 3.0x or below by the date of the spin.

What the carve-out does and does not do. It raises $724 million of net cash, establishes a public price for the most valuable asset, and gives Halvard its own currency; it also takes about $96 million of value (10 percent of 957.5) from the parent's shareholders in the IPO discount, though the discount reverses into the retained stake. It does nothing about the structure of the conglomerate: the parent still contains four divisions in effect, still holds a listed subsidiary at a holding-company discount, and has simply added minority shareholders, a governance conflict, and stand-alone costs. For Redwater, whose argument is that the discount arises from bundling, a carve-out is the weakest response; it creates a price for the best asset while leaving the rest as it was. It may be a useful first step toward a full spin of Halvard, but it should not be presented as the answer.

#### Question 9: Ranking and alternatives

Ranking. On expected value, the sale ranks first, the breakup second at zero residual discount (but it is the most fragile to the residual discount), the spin third and the carve-out fourth. On risk-adjusted value, the sale with a spin fallback dominates: it has the highest expected value, the fastest timing and the best credit outcome. The breakup's value is entirely dependent on an assumption that no discount remains, and its breakeven residual is only 6.7 percent. The spin is a good fallback. The carve-out is a poor stand-alone response but a possible step later.

Strategic effect. The sale and spin both simplify the group and focus it on three industrial businesses (the strongest case for a focused RemainCo). The carve-out leaves complexity unchanged. The breakup eliminates the diversified structure but destroys the shared services and purchasing scale and increases leverage constraints.

Marlowe alternative. Marlowe earns 6.0 percent against 9.5 percent and destroys $76 million of economic profit a year. The restructuring plan in Exhibit L would add 150 basis points of margin on revenue of 1,700, or 25.5 of EBITDA; at the 8.0x peer multiple that is 204 of enterprise value, against after-tax plant-closure costs of 90 x 0.75 = 67.5, a net gain of 136.5 or $0.43 per share. That is modest compared with the Hearthstone transactions ($1.57 per share of difference between sale and spin, $3.99 of gain from the sale versus the price) and it does not address the strategic issue. A sale of Marlowe would be defensible only if the tax basis and a buyer's price justify it, and the case supplies neither; students who recommend it must say what data they would need (tax basis, buyer synergies, cyclical timing). The strongest answer treats the Marlowe plan as a complementary action alongside the Hearthstone exit, not a substitute.

Combinations. A dual-track process (negotiate with Larkin while preparing the registration for a spin-off) captures the sale's higher price while retaining a credible fallback and negotiating leverage. Preparing the spin in parallel costs perhaps $20 to $30 million of preparatory work (not in the case) but reduces the 30 percent failure risk's cost. A staged plan (exit Hearthstone in 2027, revisit Halvard in 2028 when Halvard has a longer record as a stand-alone division, and set a ROIC target for Marlowe) is a natural sequence.

#### Question 10: Recommendation and engagement with Redwater

The recommendation memo appears at the end of this note. The key elements are summarized here for discussion.

1. Pursue the sale of Hearthstone to Larkin as the first track and prepare the spin-off as the second track, with a decision point at signing or at 31 January 2027.
2. Do not pursue a four-way breakup or a Halvard carve-out now.
3. Announce a credit-supportive plan: use the proceeds to reduce net debt to no more than 2.5x EBITDA and review capital returns thereafter.

5. Engage Redwater in a private meeting with the lead independent director before announcement; share the analysis (not the Larkin terms); offer a board refreshment discussion for two new independent directors with portfolio-management experience, chosen with the family trust's input; do not concede the four-way breakup.
6. Define the evidence that would change the recommendation: Larkin's price below $2,230 million (7.15x), the existence of a bid for Halvard or Kestrel at a significant premium, evidence that peer conglomerates are rerating after separations, or a tax opinion that the spin would be taxable.
