## The Situation

On Monday 2 November 2026, at 7:40 in the morning, the board secretary of Harbor & Pine Holdings, Inc. received a letter by courier and by email. It came from Granite Ridge Partners, a private equity sponsor, and it proposed to acquire every share of Harbor & Pine for **$9.40 per share in cash**, a premium of 38.2 percent to the unaffected closing price of $6.80 on 30 October. The letter expires on Friday 20 November 2026 at 17:00. It is conditional on due diligence and on committed financing of $400 million of new term debt and a $60 million asset-based revolver, which the sponsor says it holds in writing. It does not mention what the sponsor intends to do with the company's real estate.

Harbor & Pine is a specialty retailer of home furnishings, garden and outdoor-living products. It operates 214 stores in 31 states, 192 of them leased and 22 owned, plus a leased headquarters, a company-owned distribution center and an e-commerce site that now accounts for 18 percent of sales. Founded 61 years ago by a family nursery business, it listed 19 years ago and has been run since by a succession of merchants. Net sales peaked at $1.81 billion in fiscal 2024. Since then comparable-store sales have fallen three years running, closures have outpaced openings, the stock has fallen by roughly 60 percent from its high, and the company has funded a share repurchase and a dividend with borrowings.

The people involved:

**Dana Whitcombe (chief executive officer)** joined seven months ago from a national home-goods chain. She has a turnaround plan, "Anchor 2030", which closes 28 weak stores over two years, remodels 60 more, rebuilds the e-commerce platform and takes $55 million of cost out of the business. She believes the stock is worth well above $9.40 if she is given three years.

**Raj Venkataraman (chief financial officer)** has been with the company for nine years. He prepared the normalized earnings schedule that the committee is using and is uneasy about three of its adjustments.

**Margaret Oyelaran (lead independent director)** chairs the special committee formed on 2 November to evaluate the proposal. A former retail chief financial officer, she asks one question of every number: "Is that before rent or after rent?"

**Tom Ferreira (head of real estate)** knows that the owned stores and the distribution center were appraised in the spring at $354.0 million against a book value of $238.0 million, and that the headquarters lease and 41 other leases expire within three years.

**Calloway Value Fund**, which holds 9.6 percent of the shares, has written to the board that it will not support a sale below $11.00.

**The decision.** The special committee must decide by 20 November whether to (a) accept the proposal, (b) reject it and continue with Anchor 2030, or (c) engage with Granite Ridge and counter at a higher price. It needs a defensible view of what Harbor & Pine is worth. That means normalizing a messy earnings year, putting leased and owned properties on a comparable footing with peers that report under a different lease standard, valuing four turnaround outcomes rather than one, and understanding what a sponsor can afford to pay. You are an analyst at the committee's financial adviser.

## Industry Context

**Specialty home and outdoor retail.** Demand for furniture, décor and garden products follows housing turnover, household formation and consumer confidence, and is discretionary and seasonal. Merchants compete on assortment and price against large-format and online rivals, and stores serve as showrooms and fulfillment points for the online channel. Store productivity (sales per square foot), markdown discipline and inventory turns drive the gross margin; occupancy, labor and marketing absorb most of it. A fleet of aging stores in weaker locations produces a long tail of stores that earn less than their rent and a recurring program of closures.

**Leases are quasi-debt.** A retailer with 200 stores signs 200 long-term obligations. Rent is a contractual fixed charge that does not flex when sales fall. Closing a store does not end the obligation: the landlord must be bought out, or the lease subleased, often at a discount to remaining rent. Credit analysts therefore treat lease obligations as debt. Under US GAAP (ASC 842) an operating lease creates a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the balance sheet, but the income statement still shows a single straight-line lease cost within operating expenses, so EBITDA is stated after rent. Under IFRS 16 nearly all leases are treated like financed purchases: the income statement shows depreciation of the right-of-use asset and interest on the liability, so EBITDA is stated before most rent and EBIT is higher. Two otherwise identical retailers report very different EBITDA and EV/EBITDA depending on the standard. Valuation work must put them on the same footing, either by adding rent back and lease liabilities to enterprise value (EBITDAR and lease-adjusted EV), or by treating rent as an operating cost for everyone.

**Rules of thumb.** Practitioners sometimes capitalize annual rent at a multiple (a figure of eight times is common) to estimate lease debt. The balance-sheet liability, which discounts the committed payments at the lessee's borrowing rate, is more precise for the committed term but ignores renewals and variable rent. The two measures can differ by tens of percent.

**Turnarounds are distributions, not points.** A retailer in decline can recover, stall or fail. Value depends on how many weak stores close and at what cost, whether remodels lift sales, whether the margin rebuilds, and whether lenders and landlords remain patient. Practitioners model discrete scenarios, attach probabilities and test how sensitive the weighted value is to those probabilities, because the single "management case" is rarely the expected case.

**Real estate and the financing toolkit.** Retailers that own property can sell it to an investor and lease it back, raising cash at a capitalization rate that often implies a far higher multiple than the stock market pays for the retailer's earnings. A sale-leaseback converts owned property into a lease obligation, raises rent and lease-adjusted debt, and triggers tax on any gain. Sponsors acquiring retailers often underwrite a sale-leaseback as part of the financing.

**Take-privates.** A sponsor values a target by what return it earns on its equity, given the debt it can raise, the cash flows it can extract and the price at which it can exit. A bid is therefore bounded by the sponsor's required return, and the committee can estimate that bound.

**Modeling conventions for all scenarios.**

* The FY2026 base is the normalized 52-week year: sales of $1,621.8 million (reported $1,653.0 million less the $31.2 million 53rd week), and EBITDAR of $322.9 million, 19.9 percent of sales. Scenario EBITDAR margins apply to each year's sales.
* Sales in year t equal prior-year sales x (1 + comp growth), less closures x $5.0 million x 70 percent (closed stores average $5.0 million of sales and 30 percent transfers to nearby stores and online), plus openings x $3.0 million.
* Fixed rent in FY2026 is $184.0 million: $171.2 million on the 192 leased stores and $12.8 million for headquarters and distribution. Closing a leased store saves $1.00 million of rent (the weakest stores carry high rents), a new store adds $0.60 million, and all rent rises 2.0 percent a year. Closures are assumed to happen at the start of the fiscal year. The 22 owned stores pay no rent and no market rent is charged for them.
* EBITDA equals EBITDAR less fixed rent. Depreciation is 3.1 percent of sales. Closure costs are expensed and tax-deductible. Maintenance capex is 1.8 percent of sales; remodel and IT capex are added. Working capital is 12.0 percent of sales (so a sales decline releases cash). Tax is 25 percent of positive EBIT.
* Enterprise value is the present value of free cash flow to the firm at the WACC plus a terminal value at the FY2031 exit multiple, discounted five years. Equity value is enterprise value less net financial debt of $275.5 million. Per-share values use 59.8 million shares and are floored at zero because equity is limited liability. Operating leases are not deducted again, because rent is already in EBITDA.

## Complications and Exhibits

**Exhibit 1: Management's normalization.** Mr. Venkataraman is uneasy about three items in Table 4.7. (a) Stock-based compensation is a real cost of retaining staff. (b) Store closures have cost $5.1 million, $7.9 million and $14.2 million in the last three years, so some closure cost recurs; he judges $6.0 million a year to be normal. (c) The inventory write-down of $18.0 million exceeds a normal year, which he puts at $5.0 million. Table 4.7 also omits items that cut the other way: $3.5 million of vendor rebates that were pulled forward from FY2027, the extra 53rd week (which contributed about $2.2 million of EBITDA in a low-volume January week), $4.0 million of annual rent step-ups on 18 stores that renew at market in FY2027, and $5.0 million of marketing spend deferred in the second half that must be restored.

**Exhibit 2: Leases and closures.** Forty-one leases, including the headquarters, expire within three years. The 28 stores in the Anchor 2030 closure list average $5.0 million of sales and $1.00 million of annual rent, with 4.3 years remaining. A landlord buyout averages $1.9 million a store, and severance and liquidation add $0.4 million, for $2.3 million per closure ($3.0 million in distress, where landlords have less reason to negotiate). Under US GAAP the lease liability for a closed store stays on the balance sheet until the lease is exited.

**Exhibit 3: Owned real estate.** The 22 owned stores and the distribution center were appraised in spring 2026 at $354.0 million against a book value of $238.0 million. A sale-leaseback of the whole portfolio would be priced at a 7.0 percent capitalization rate, producing initial rent of $24.8 million (rising 2.0 percent a year) on a 20-year lease, transaction costs of $3.0 million and tax at 25 percent on the $116.0 million gain. The management projections charge no rent on these properties. Granite Ridge's letter does not mention a sale-leaseback.

**Exhibit 4: Debt terms.** The $265.0 million term loan matures on 15 September 2028, within 22 months. A change of control would trigger repayment. The revolver's springing fixed-charge coverage covenant of 1.10x applies when availability falls below 12.5 percent of the borrowing base. Fixed-charge coverage here is defined as EBITDAR divided by fixed rent plus cash interest.

**Exhibit 5: Inventory.** Inventory days have risen from 108 to 130 while sales fell. Merchandise older than twelve months totals $31.0 million, reserved at 25 percent. The auditors have suggested 40 percent. The additional $4.65 million exposure (31.0 x 15 percent) is not in any normalization above.

**Exhibit 6: Peer set.** Willow Row Stores is small and highly leveraged, and some analysts would exclude it. Nordhaven Home and Alder Outdoor Group report under IFRS 16, so their EBITDA is before most rent. Raw EV/EBITDA multiples taken straight from a data terminal mix the two standards and are not comparable.

**Exhibit 7: Trading and shareholders.** Third-quarter comparable-store sales were (3.1 percent), better than the (4.4 percent) of the first half. Ms. Whitcombe attributes this to new assortments; Mr. Venkataraman notes that part reflects customers pulling forward outdoor-furniture purchases ahead of a vendor price increase. Calloway Value Fund has said it will not support a sale below $11.00 and has asked for a market check. The committee's fairness opinion will be based on the work you do now.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning Objectives

1. Normalize a distorted earnings year by classifying adjustments (non-recurring, timing, forward-looking, judgment) and defend a range rather than a point.
2. Convert operating leases into lease-adjusted debt, EBITDAR and leverage, and restate US GAAP results as if under IFRS 16.
3. Compare peers reporting under different lease standards on a common basis, and keep numerators and denominators consistent (EV with leases over EBITDAR; EV without leases over EBITDA).
4. Value a turnaround with explicit scenarios, probabilities, and an equity floor, and test how much of the value sits in the terminal value.
5. Recognize hidden real-estate value and how a sale-leaseback changes value, leverage and coverage.
6. Estimate a sponsor's ability to pay from its required return, and use it to frame a negotiation.

**IFRS 16 restatement (normalized).** EBITDA becomes EBITDA plus fixed rent: 138.9 + 184.0 = $322.9M. #### Question 4: Cost of capital

Cost of equity = 4.3% + 1.45 x 5.5% = 12.275%. After-tax cost of term debt = 9.0% x (1 - 0.25) = 6.75%.

**Excluding operating leases from debt.** Weights use the unaffected market value of equity (406.6) and net financial debt (275.5), total 682.1: equity 59.6 percent, debt 40.4 percent. WACC = 0.596 x 12.275% + 0.404 x 6.75% = 7.32% + 2.73% = **10.04%, rounded to 10.0%**.

**Including operating leases as debt.** Total capital is 406.6 + 275.5 + 1,083.1 = 1,765.2: equity 23.0 percent, financial debt 15.6 percent, leases 61.4 percent. After-tax lease cost = 6.2% x 0.75 = 4.65%. WACC = 0.230 x 12.275% + 0.156 x 6.75% + 0.614 x 4.65% = 2.83% + 1.05% + 2.85% = **6.73%**.

The two numbers are both correct for different cash flows. The 10.0 percent WACC discounts cash flows after rent, which belong to financial debt and equity only. The 6.7 percent WACC discounts cash flows before rent (EBITDAR-based), and then requires explicit investment in replacement leases and subtraction of lease liabilities. Mixing them is the common error. If the after-rent Plan cash flows and terminal value are discounted at 6.73%, enterprise value becomes 1,117.7 instead of 972.2 and the value per share becomes (1,117.7 - 275.5) / 59.8 = **$14.08 instead of $11.65**, an overstatement of $2.43 (21 percent). This chapter uses the after-rent approach throughout.
