# Chapter 4: Case: Harbor & Pine, a Retail Turnaround

## The Situation

On Monday 2 November 2026, at 7:40 in the morning, the board secretary of Harbor & Pine Holdings, Inc. received a letter by courier and by email. It came from Granite Ridge Partners, a private equity sponsor, and it proposed to acquire every share of Harbor & Pine for **$9.40 per share in cash**, a premium of 38.2 percent to the unaffected closing price of $6.80 on 30 October. The letter expires on Friday 20 November 2026 at 17:00. It is conditional on due diligence and on committed financing of $400 million of new term debt and a $60 million asset-based revolver, which the sponsor says it holds in writing. It does not mention what the sponsor intends to do with the company's real estate.

Harbor & Pine is a specialty retailer of home furnishings, garden and outdoor-living products. It operates 214 stores in 31 states, 192 of them leased and 22 owned, plus a leased headquarters, a company-owned distribution center and an e-commerce site that now accounts for 18 percent of sales. Founded 61 years ago by a family nursery business, it listed 19 years ago and has been run since by a succession of merchants. Net sales peaked at $1.81 billion in fiscal 2024. Since then comparable-store sales have fallen three years running, closures have outpaced openings, the stock has fallen by roughly 60 percent from its high, and the company has funded a share repurchase and a dividend with borrowings.

The people involved:

**Dana Whitcombe (chief executive officer)** joined seven months ago from a national home-goods chain. She has a turnaround plan, "Anchor 2030", which closes 28 weak stores over two years, remodels 60 more, rebuilds the e-commerce platform and takes $55 million of cost out of the business. She believes the stock is worth well above $9.40 if she is given three years.

**Raj Venkataraman (chief financial officer)** has been with the company for nine years. He prepared the normalized earnings schedule that the committee is using and is uneasy about three of its adjustments.

**Margaret Oyelaran (lead independent director)** chairs the special committee formed on 2 November to evaluate the proposal. A former retail chief financial officer, she asks one question of every number: "Is that before rent or after rent?"

**Tom Ferreira (head of real estate)** knows that the owned stores and the distribution center were appraised in the spring at $354.0 million against a book value of $238.0 million, and that the headquarters lease and 41 other leases expire within three years.

**Calloway Value Fund**, which holds 9.6 percent of the shares, has written to the board that it will not support a sale below $11.00.

**The decision.** The special committee must decide by 20 November whether to (a) accept the proposal, (b) reject it and continue with Anchor 2030, or (c) engage with Granite Ridge and counter at a higher price. It needs a defensible view of what Harbor & Pine is worth. That means normalizing a messy earnings year, putting leased and owned properties on a comparable footing with peers that report under a different lease standard, valuing four turnaround outcomes rather than one, and understanding what a sponsor can afford to pay. You are an analyst at the committee's financial adviser.

## Industry Context

**Specialty home and outdoor retail.** Demand for furniture, décor and garden products follows housing turnover, household formation and consumer confidence, and is discretionary and seasonal. Merchants compete on assortment and price against large-format and online rivals, and stores serve as showrooms and fulfillment points for the online channel. Store productivity (sales per square foot), markdown discipline and inventory turns drive the gross margin; occupancy, labor and marketing absorb most of it. A fleet of aging stores in weaker locations produces a long tail of stores that earn less than their rent and a recurring program of closures.

**Leases are quasi-debt.** A retailer with 200 stores signs 200 long-term obligations. Rent is a contractual fixed charge that does not flex when sales fall. Closing a store does not end the obligation: the landlord must be bought out, or the lease subleased, often at a discount to remaining rent. Credit analysts therefore treat lease obligations as debt. Under US GAAP (ASC 842) an operating lease creates a right-of-use asset and a lease liability on the balance sheet, but the income statement still shows a single straight-line lease cost within operating expenses, so EBITDA is stated after rent. Under IFRS 16 nearly all leases are treated like financed purchases: the income statement shows depreciation of the right-of-use asset and interest on the liability, so EBITDA is stated before most rent and EBIT is higher. Two otherwise identical retailers report very different EBITDA and EV/EBITDA depending on the standard. Valuation work must put them on the same footing, either by adding rent back and lease liabilities to enterprise value (EBITDAR and lease-adjusted EV), or by treating rent as an operating cost for everyone.

**Rules of thumb.** Practitioners sometimes capitalize annual rent at a multiple (a figure of eight times is common) to estimate lease debt. The balance-sheet liability, which discounts the committed payments at the lessee's borrowing rate, is more precise for the committed term but ignores renewals and variable rent. The two measures can differ by tens of percent.

**Turnarounds are distributions, not points.** A retailer in decline can recover, stall or fail. Value depends on how many weak stores close and at what cost, whether remodels lift sales, whether the margin rebuilds, and whether lenders and landlords remain patient. Practitioners model discrete scenarios, attach probabilities and test how sensitive the weighted value is to those probabilities, because the single "management case" is rarely the expected case.

**Real estate and the financing toolkit.** Retailers that own property can sell it to an investor and lease it back, raising cash at a capitalization rate that often implies a far higher multiple than the stock market pays for the retailer's earnings. A sale-leaseback converts owned property into a lease obligation, raises rent and lease-adjusted debt, and triggers tax on any gain. Sponsors acquiring retailers often underwrite a sale-leaseback as part of the financing.

**Take-privates.** A sponsor values a target by what return it earns on its equity, given the debt it can raise, the cash flows it can extract and the price at which it can exit. A bid is therefore bounded by the sponsor's required return, and the committee can estimate that bound.
