# Chapter 12: Case: Tri-County and Lakeshore, a Regional Bank Merger

*All companies, people, and figures in this case are fictional and illustrative. Any resemblance to real institutions is coincidental. The numbers are constructed for teaching and are not forecasts or statistics about any real market.*

## The Situation

At 7:15 on the morning of Wednesday, November 4, 2026, Maria Delgado, chief executive officer of Tri-County Bancorp, sat at the head of the boardroom table on the fourteenth floor of the bank's headquarters and listened to her chief financial officer, Samuel Okafor, read out a number she did not like. "On our own base case," he said, "tangible book value per share drops about six and a half percent the day we close. We earn it back in four and a half years on the simple method. On the crossover method, it is seven."

Tri-County Bancorp ("Tri-County" or "TCB") is a $6.2 billion bank holding company serving three adjoining counties and the small cities around them. It lends to local businesses, commercial real estate investors, and homeowners, and it funds itself mainly with deposits gathered through 61 branches. It has been consistently profitable, well capitalized, and publicly listed for two decades. Its board has long said that it wants to be "the bank that the next bank has to beat," which, in practice, means scale: spreading the fixed cost of technology, compliance, and risk management across a larger balance sheet.

Lakeshore Financial Corporation ("Lakeshore" or "LFC") is a $2.1 billion bank holding company headquartered on the opposite side of the lake in the next county. It has 24 branches, a loyal deposit base, a good small-business franchise, and a chief executive, Walter Haines, who is 66 and has told friends that he would like to hand the bank to a strong partner rather than a financial buyer. Nine of Lakeshore's branches sit within a few miles of a Tri-County branch. Lakeshore's shares trade at a modest premium to book value and a discount to Tri-County's valuation multiples.

In late October, after a series of conversations between Maria and Walter, Tri-County's advisor, Fairhaven Partners, delivered a draft proposal: Tri-County would acquire Lakeshore for 0.75 Tri-County shares plus $5.00 in cash for each Lakeshore share. At Tri-County's current stock price of $21.50, that is worth $21.125 per Lakeshore share, a 25.7 percent premium to Lakeshore's $16.80 closing price, and a total of about $295.8 million.

### The people

**Maria Delgado**, 55, CEO of Tri-County, who led two small acquisitions earlier in her tenure and wants this one to be the transformational one.

**Samuel Okafor**, 49, CFO of Tri-County, who built the merger model and is cautious about tangible book value dilution.

**Helen Prescott**, 62, chair of Tri-County's board, a retired industrial executive who asks one question of every deal: "Who pays for this if we are wrong?"

**Raj Menon**, Tri-County's chief risk officer, who has reviewed Lakeshore's loan files and is concerned about its investor commercial real estate concentration.

**Walter Haines**, 66, CEO of Lakeshore, a sympathetic seller who nonetheless owes fiduciary duties to his shareholders and has made clear that his board will entertain other offers.

**Fairhaven Partners**, Tri-County's investment banking advisor, which prepared the valuation and synergy analysis.

### The decision

Tri-County's board meets on Thursday, November 12, 2026. Lakeshore's board has said it will respond to a formal proposal by Thursday, November 19. Maria must recommend whether the board should approve the proposal as drafted, approve it with changes, or decline to proceed. If the board approves, the companies expect to announce the transaction on Monday, November 23, and to close in the second quarter of 2027, subject to regulatory approvals and shareholder votes.

The decision turns on five questions that this case asks students to answer in the order in which a bank board would raise them. Is the exchange ratio fair, and what does the offer imply on standard bank valuation measures? What accounting does the acquisition create, and how does it affect earnings per share and tangible book value per share? Are the synergies large and certain enough to justify the premium? Will the combined company remain comfortably capitalized, and for how long are its capital ratios compressed? And what could go wrong, because banks that stumble in mergers usually stumble on credit quality, deposit retention, or systems conversion rather than on the arithmetic of the exchange ratio.

## Industry Context

### Why regional banks merge

Regional banks earn most of their income from the spread between what they charge on loans and securities and what they pay on deposits, measured by net interest margin, plus fee income from services such as treasury management, wealth management, and card processing. Their largest cost is people and branches, and a growing share is technology and regulatory compliance. Many of those costs are semi-fixed: a $2 billion bank needs a risk management function, a cybersecurity team, a core processing system, and a regulatory reporting department that are not proportionally smaller than those of a $6 billion bank. Combining two banks therefore allows overlapping functions to be consolidated, branches within a few miles of each other to be merged, and systems to be run once rather than twice. These cost savings, usually expressed as a percentage of the target's non-interest expense, are the primary source of value in a bank merger. Revenue synergies are real but harder to deliver, and many boards exclude them from the numbers they rely upon.

### How bank mergers are valued

Banks are valued on earnings and on book value, and especially tangible book value, which removes goodwill and other intangibles. The standard measures are price to earnings, price to tangible book value, and, for the deposit franchise, the core deposit premium: the amount paid above tangible book value, divided by core deposits. Buyers and sellers compare the offer to precedent transactions and to the buyer's own multiples. Two measures dominate the buyer's analysis. The first is earnings per share accretion or dilution, usually shown on an operating basis that excludes one-time merger costs and on a GAAP basis that includes them. The second is tangible book value per share dilution and the number of years of earnings accretion required to recover it, the earnback period. Many bank boards use informal limits on both: for example, operating EPS accretion by the second year and an earnback of no more than five years.

### Purchase accounting

In an acquisition, the buyer records the target's assets and liabilities at fair value. Loans acquired at a discount to par (reflecting both credit risk and interest rates that are below current market rates) are written down, and the discount is accreted into interest income over the life of the loans. Securities with unrealized losses are likewise marked down and accreted. The buyer records a core deposit intangible, an asset representing the value of the low-cost deposit base, and amortizes it. Deferred taxes are recorded on the differences between book and tax values. The difference between the purchase price and the fair value of net identifiable assets is goodwill, which is not amortized but is deducted when computing tangible book value and regulatory capital. Under the current expected credit loss framework, the buyer also establishes an allowance for loan losses on the acquired loans through a charge to earnings at closing, the so-called Day 2 provision. Students who have worked through Volume 1, Chapter 22 (mergers and acquisitions) will recognize the structure as the standard acquirer-versus-target value-creation framework, applied with bank-specific accounting.

### Capital

A bank's ability to absorb losses and keep growing depends on its regulatory capital ratios. Common equity tier 1 (CET1) capital, essentially common equity less goodwill and most intangibles, divided by risk-weighted assets, is the most closely watched. Banks also report total capital (adding eligible subordinated debt and a limited amount of the allowance for loan losses), the tier 1 leverage ratio (capital divided by average total assets less deducted intangibles), and tangible common equity to tangible assets. Regulators set minimum levels and, above them, thresholds for a "well-capitalized" designation. In the fictional world of this case, those thresholds are 6.5 percent for CET1, 8.0 percent for tier 1 capital, 10.0 percent for total capital, and 5.0 percent for the leverage ratio, and Tri-County's board has adopted tighter internal limits that appear in the exhibits. A bank merger almost always reduces capital ratios on the closing date, because the buyer creates goodwill and intangibles, pays one-time costs, and takes on the target's risk-weighted assets, and then rebuilds them through retained earnings. Regulators approving a merger look closely at how fast the rebuilding happens.

### Integration risk

The synergies on a spreadsheet are earned only if the banks are actually combined. The largest risks are customer and deposit attrition when branches close or the systems conversion goes poorly, departure of the target's lenders who carry client relationships, and unexpected credit losses in portfolios the buyer did not originate. These risks are asymmetric: merger accretion builds slowly and loss events arrive suddenly, which is why seasoned acquirers insist on conservative marks, prudent assumptions, and a structure that shares risk with the seller.
