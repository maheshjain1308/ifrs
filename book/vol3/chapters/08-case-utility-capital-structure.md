# Chapter 8: Case: Meridian Power, Capital Structure for a Regulated Utility

*Illustrative case. Meridian Power Company, its regulator, its rating agency, its peers and all figures are fictional and were constructed for teaching. Nothing here describes a real utility, commission or agency, and no figure should be read as market data. Dollar amounts are in millions unless stated.*

## The Situation

Samuel Okonkwo, chief financial officer of Meridian Power Company ("Meridian"), has a number on his whiteboard that he does not like: **$7.5 billion**. It is the capital expenditure the company intends to make between 2027 and 2031 to modernise its distribution grid, add transmission lines, build owned solar and storage capacity and convert an older coal plant to gas. It is also 72% of the $10.45 billion of rate base the company expects to have at the end of 2026. Meridian has never spent money at this pace, and the cash flow from operations (FFO) covers only about three-quarters of the cost before any dividend is paid, and about half of it after.

Meridian is an electric utility serving 1.3 million customers across the northern part of the fictional state of Platte. It owns generation, a transmission network and a distribution system, and it is regulated by the Platte Public Utilities Commission ("the Commission"). Its common stock (180.0 million shares, currently $42.00) is held mainly by income-oriented funds and a large base of retail shareholders who have received a dividend every year since 1957. Its senior unsecured debt is rated BBB by Keystone Ratings ("Keystone"), one notch above the lowest investment-grade level but two below a threshold that the treasurer regards as comfortable.

The people around the table are these.

- **Elena Marchetti**, chief executive, who inherited the capital plan from her predecessor and believes in it. She wants to protect the dividend and the rating.
- **Samuel Okonkwo**, chief financial officer, who must present the financing plan.
- **Priya Raman**, treasurer, who runs the debt programme and the relationship with Keystone, and who is certain that the rating agency's published thresholds are binding in practice.
- **Walter Lindgren**, chair of the board's finance committee and a former commissioner of another state. He knows how commissions think and warns that "a commission will not pay us to be careless about our balance sheet."
- **Dr. Hannah Osei**, the company's regulatory economist, who is preparing the cost-of-capital testimony for the rate case.

The finance committee meets on **Tuesday, 8 December 2026**. It must decide three things.

1. **The 2027 to 2031 financing plan**: how much of the funding gap to cover with debt, common equity, hybrid securities and retained cash, and in what sequence, subject to a rating constraint that has become binding.
2. **The return Meridian will request in its general rate case**, to be filed on 15 March 2027: the requested return on equity ("ROE") and the equity ratio. The present authorised return is 9.6% on a 50% equity ratio, set in the last case.
3. **The dividend policy** to adopt for the plan period, given the tension between a 62% payout ratio that investors treat as sacrosanct and a balance sheet that is losing its cushion.

The decision has a clock behind it. The commission's statutory timetable gives it about eleven months from filing to issue an order, so new rates cannot take effect before early 2028. During the first year of the plan Meridian will spend $1.35 billion against rates that were set using a rate base that is already out of date. Keystone has told Priya privately that it will review Meridian's rating after the rate case is filed and that its downgrade threshold for BBB is a ratio of funds from operations ("FFO") to debt below 15% on a sustained basis.

Elena's own summary of the dilemma, in the company's board paper, is that regulation gives Meridian a promise (an opportunity to earn a stated return on every dollar it invests prudently) but not a guarantee, and that the promise is only worth what the capital markets believe it is worth. A utility that is seen as stretching its balance sheet pays more for money, and eventually its customers pay more as well.

### How regulation shapes the question

Regulation of a utility turns the usual corporate-finance problem on its head. An unregulated company chooses a capital structure to maximise value given its operating risk. A regulated company is told, in effect, what it may earn: the commission sets a **revenue requirement** equal to operating costs, depreciation and taxes plus a **return on rate base**. Rate base is the net, depreciated cost of the plant that is used and useful in serving customers, less the deferred taxes that customers have already advanced. The allowed return is the weighted cost of the capital that the commission treats as financing that rate base: an authorised equity ratio times an allowed ROE, plus the debt weight times the embedded cost of debt. The company's actual financing mix does not set the revenue requirement. If Meridian finances more than the authorised proportion with debt it keeps the extra margin between the allowed return on equity and the cost of debt, which raises its earned ROE and, simultaneously, its risk. If the commission becomes concerned it can impute a hypothetical capital structure that is more heavily weighted to equity than the actual one.

This setting makes three features of the case especially important. First, **regulatory lag**: because rates are set on a historical rate base, a company in the middle of a heavy capital programme earns less than its allowed return until rates catch up. Second, **credit metrics, not value maximisation, are the binding constraint**: the rating thresholds that the agency applies to utilities decide how much debt can be used. Third, the **spread between the allowed return and the market cost of capital** determines whether the capital programme creates or destroys shareholder value, which is why the choice of the requested ROE matters as much as the financing mix.

## Industry Context

**Capital intensity.** Regulated electric utilities invest continuously in networks whose economic life runs to decades. A utility's earnings growth is mostly rate base growth: if rate base grows at 7.5% a year and the allowed return stays constant, then earnings grow at about the same rate, provided the financing is done at the authorised structure and costs. This has made the utility sector a favourite of income investors, who want a stable and growing dividend, but it also makes the sector a heavy and regular issuer of both debt and equity.

**The credit framework.** Rating agencies assess utilities on business risk (regulatory support, the predictability of cost recovery, the fuel mix and the physical exposure to storms or other hazards) and financial risk, measured through cash-flow ratios. The most widely used is FFO divided by adjusted debt. Keystone's published guidance, which the case treats as illustrative, is summarised below.

| Rating | FFO / debt | Debt / EBITDA | Interpretation for a utility of Meridian's business profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| A- | 22% or more | 3.8x or less | Strong; requires a materially lower payout or much more equity |
| BBB+ | 18% to 22% | 3.8x to 4.3x | Comfortable investment grade |
| BBB | 15% to 18% | 4.3x to 5.0x | Meridian's current level; downgrade threshold at 15% sustained |
| BBB- | 12% to 15% | 5.0x to 5.8x | Lowest investment grade; commercial paper and swap markets become harder |
| BB+ | below 12% | above 5.8x | Non-investment grade; many institutional buyers are excluded |

Keystone considers a ratio sustained if it is below the threshold for two consecutive years on its own forecast. It treats a hybrid security with the required features (long maturity, deep subordination and the ability to defer interest) as 50% debt and 50% equity for the calculation of FFO to debt.

**The regulatory environment.** The Commission has a reputation for being constructive on cost recovery but sceptical on returns. In recent years it has awarded ROEs a little below the average of its peers. Its staff routinely recommend lower returns and lower equity ratios than utilities request, and commissions commonly settle somewhere between the staff's and the company's positions. A commission that sees a utility using a thin equity layer tends to respond by imputing a thicker one.

**The capital markets.** Utility equity is valued on dividend yield, earnings growth and the regulatory environment; the price-to-book ratio of a utility exceeds one if investors believe that the allowed return exceeds the market cost of equity. Utility investors generally dislike equity issuance at a discount to the market price but accept it if it is regular, small relative to market value and clearly linked to a funded rate base. The usual instruments are an at-the-market ("ATM") programme, in which shares are sold gradually into the market through an agent; forward sale agreements, in which the company fixes the price today and delivers shares later; block offerings; and a dividend reinvestment plan. On the debt side, first-mortgage bonds and senior notes are issued across the maturity curve, and junior subordinated hybrids supply equity credit at a higher coupon. Volume 1, Chapter 11 (WACC), Chapter 14 (debt) and Chapter 15 (capital structure) set out the relevant theory; Volume 1, Chapter 13 covers equity issuance.

**Peers.** Meridian is compared with six regional utilities; their authorised returns, equity ratios, credit metrics and valuation multiples are shown in the next section. The peers' credit metrics are strongly related to their equity ratios and ratings. Meridian is at the lower end of the group on equity ratio and in the middle on FFO to debt.

## Complications and Exhibits

The model in the exhibits is deliberately clean. The following facts complicate it, and an adviser must decide which ones to build into the answer.

**1. Keystone's adjusted debt.** Priya has learned that Keystone does not use the reported debt. It adds $310.0 million for pension deficits and operating leases. Applying the adjustment to 2026E, the ratio of FFO to adjusted debt is 923.9 / (5,500.0 + 310.0) = 15.9%, not 16.8%. The published 15% downgrade threshold is stated in terms of the adjusted ratio. This means that Meridian has less than one percentage point of cushion today, not 1.8 points. Priya has not shared the adjustment with the rest of the finance committee because she believes the agency may soften it if Meridian files a credible plan. (Keystone has made no such statement.)

**2. The rate case outcome is uncertain.** The company's draft request is 10.25% on a 52% equity ratio. Staff will probably argue for 9.0% on 48%. The final order will be somewhere in between, and might also change the test year. Dr. Osei believes that the Commission would find a request of more than 10% hard to justify given the peer average, and that a request that was seen as aggressive could damage credibility on the other issues, including the recovery of the renewable investments. The model assumes the current 9.6% and 50% throughout; the true outcome affects every ratio.

**3. Partly forward-looking rates.** A bill before the legislature would allow utilities to use a partly forward-looking test year, so that the return on investments made during the rate-effective year is recognised in rates. If passed, the effect would be to bring revenues closer to the current year's rate base. You may treat the current model as having no forward-looking element (weight 0), and test the effect of a 50% and a 100% weight.

**4. A renewable tracker.** The company is negotiating a tracker that would allow it to recover the cost of owned solar and storage capacity as it is placed in service, without waiting for a rate case. The Commission has not agreed. The tracker would cover about half of the $1.9 billion renewable programme. It would have a similar (but smaller) effect to Complication 3.

**5. The shareholder base.** About half of the shareholders are retail investors who hold the stock for the dividend, and Meridian has not cut the dividend since 1957. A cut in the payout would reduce the DPS below the current $1.53 in 2027 for any payout below about 58% (given the 2027 earnings in the model) and would probably be read by income investors as a signal of distress, even if the earnings continue to grow. Equity issuance is also sensitive: some large holders have said that they will tolerate "modest, regular" issuance but not a surprise block.

**6. The share price.** The share price of $42.00 has been stable for six months. A fall of 15% would increase the number of shares required for any given amount of equity, and utility shares are sensitive to changes in long-term interest rates. The financing plan should therefore be tested at $36.00 and $48.00.

**7. Interest rates.** The model assumes new senior notes at 5.3%. A rise of one percentage point would raise the cost of new debt to 6.3% and reduce every coverage ratio. The company could hedge part of the 2027 issuance with forward-starting swaps, at a cost that you need not model.

**8. Legacy maturities.** A $600.0 million series of notes with a 3.6% coupon matures in 2028. The model assumes it is refinanced at the embedded cost of 4.8%, whereas the likely cost is 5.3%; the effect is an extra $3.0 million of annual interest before tax (600.0 × 0.5%). It is small but it is an example of why the embedded cost of debt will drift above 4.8%.

**9. Storm costs.** The Commission has permitted a regulatory asset of $650.0 million (including storm costs from last year) which earns no return until it is recovered. The model leaves this in the rate base; the company believes that the treatment of this asset may be contested in the case.

**10. Data ambiguity.** Exhibit 6 shows a Meridian P/B of 1.53x and an allowed ROE of 9.6%, yet the CAPM cost of equity (Exhibit 7) is below the allowed ROE, which means that investors believe the regulatory promise is worth more than the cost of capital. The dividend-discount estimate and the bond-yield-plus estimate give higher costs of equity than the CAPM. A student must decide which estimates to trust, and the case does not say.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After this case, students should be able to:

1. Explain how an allowed return is built from an authorised capital structure and why the actual financing mix does not set the revenue requirement.
2. Estimate a utility's cost of equity by three methods, compute a market WACC, and compare it with the regulatory return to judge whether a capital programme creates value.
3. Treat credit-rating thresholds as the binding constraint on debt capacity, and build a multi-year financing model that tests FFO-to-debt and leverage ratios.
4. Size the external equity needed to protect a rating, and compare common equity, hybrids, a payout change and a thicker equity ratio.
5. Analyse the effect of the rate case outcome, regulatory lag and rating-agency adjustments on the financing plan.
6. Make and defend a financing and regulatory recommendation that balances the rating, the dividend, shareholder dilution and customer costs.

### Solution to Question 1: Allowed return and regulatory lag

**Allowed returns.** After-tax: 0.50 × 9.6% + 0.50 × 4.8% = 4.80% + 2.40% = **7.20%**. Pre-tax: debt interest is deductible, so only the equity return needs grossing up for tax: 0.50 × 4.8% + 0.50 × 9.6% / (1 − 0.24) = 2.400% + 6.316% = **8.716%**.

**EBIT.** 8.716% × 9,730.0 (end-2025 rate base) = **$848.0 million**, which equals Exhibit 1.

**Earned ROE.** Net income 443.9 / equity 4,950.0 = **8.97%** (9.0%), compared with 9.6% allowed: a shortfall of 0.63 percentage points, worth 9.6% × 4,950.0 − 443.9 = **$31.3 million** of net income, or 7.1% of earnings.

**Decomposition.** If rates were set on the end-2026 rate base, EBIT would be 8.716% × 10,450.0 = 910.8 and net income (910.8 − 264.0) × 0.76 = 491.6, an earned ROE of 9.93%. Two effects separate the 9.0% from 9.6%: lag costs 9.93% − 8.97% = **0.96 points**, while the company's heavier use of debt than the authorised structure adds 9.93% − 9.60% = **0.33 points**. At the authorised structure (equity 5,225.0, debt 5,225.0, interest 250.8) the no-lag net income would be (910.8 − 250.8) × 0.76 = 501.6 and the ROE exactly 9.60%.

**Leverage.** Replacing equity with debt at a given rate base raises net income by the after-tax interest saved relative to the equity return it displaces. A utility that holds 52.6% debt against an authorised 50% earns a higher ROE on book equity, but its allowed revenue does not change: the extra margin is compensation for risk that the agency prices through the rating. This is the reason the financing plan, not the allowed return, becomes the binding choice.

### Solution to Question 8: The cost of a downgrade

**Direct cost.** A 45 basis point widening of the spread on new debt raises annual interest by 0.45% × cumulative new debt. In the all-debt plan, cumulative new debt in 2031 is $3,571.7 million, so the annual cost is **$16.1 million before tax, $12.2 million after tax**. If about $550 million of legacy debt is refinanced each year, a further $2,750 million is repriced by 2031, adding $12.4 million before tax. The total is $28.5 million pre-tax or **$21.7 million after tax**.

**Cost of avoiding it.** The $988.3 million of equity in Plan A replaces debt costing 5.3% before tax, so net income is lower by 988.3 × 5.3% × 0.76 = **$39.8 million** a year by 2031, plus $24.7 million of one-time issuance costs.

**Break-even spread.** The equity cost equals the downgrade cost when the spread widening is 39.8 / 0.76 / (3,571.7 + 2,750) = **0.83%, or 83 basis points**. The direct cost of a one-notch downgrade is therefore smaller than the earnings cost of the equity needed to avoid it.

**Why protect the rating anyway?** (i) Regulated debt costs are generally recovered through rates, so the economic burden of a spread increase falls mainly on customers, after a lag, which is a political cost that the company would like to avoid. (ii) The cost of the downgrade is not linear: one notch below BBB- takes the company out of the investment-grade universe, and Meridian would be one adverse rate order from that edge. (iii) A BBB- company has less access to commercial paper, collateral thresholds under swaps and power contracts are tighter, and refinancing in a crisis is harder. (iv) Equity is not a pure cost: the equity portion of rate base earns the allowed ROE, and equity issued at $42.00 against book value of $27.50 per share is accretive to book value. A reasonable answer is to **protect the rating at the margin but not over-insure it**: choose a target (16% reported) that leaves a modest cushion over the adjusted threshold, and use the hybrid or slower dividend growth as contingent tools.

### Solution to Question 9: Execution

**Choice of instrument.** On the $988.3 million programme, the cost of issuance is 2.5% for an ATM ($24.7 million), 3.0% for forward sales ($29.6 million), and, for a block, 4.0% of proceeds plus a price about 4% below the market: the effective loss is 1 − 0.96 × 0.96 = 7.8%, or **$77.5 million** if the whole amount were raised in blocks. The ATM is the cheapest and best suited to a steady, back-loaded programme; forward sales are useful to fix the price for a known need (for example the 2028 requirement) and to align settlement with the date of the order; a block is justified only for a sudden need and is the form that large holders have said they dislike. A mixture is appropriate: ATM as the main channel, with a forward for part of the 2028 and 2029 needs, and the dividend reinvestment plan as a small supplement.

### Solution to Question 10: Recommendation

The recommendation is in the board memorandum at the end of this section. The structure of a good answer is: (i) state the plan, with amounts by year; (ii) state the requested ROE and equity ratio and say why; (iii) state the dividend policy; (iv) identify the conditions under which the plan changes. The best answers rank the risks: the Keystone adjustment first, the rate case second, the share price third.

### Sensitivity discussion

The model is most sensitive to the allowed return and the test year, then to the credit-agency definition, and least to the issue price. In rough terms: **0.1 point of allowed ROE is worth $46 to $47 million of equity; 2 points of authorised equity ratio about $110 million; the adjusted-debt definition about $236 million (at a 16% target); moving to a forward-looking test year worth $190 million to $390 million; and a 100 basis point change in the cost of new debt about $106 million.** The sum of the adverse cases (staff outcome, adjusted debt basis and higher rates) could push the equity requirement above $1.6 billion, while the favourable cases (company ask and a forward-looking year) could bring it well below $0.6 billion. Because the rate case outcome will be known in early 2028, the plan should be organised so that most of the equity is issued after the order, which in the model is true of 2028 onwards. The 2027 requirement ($45 million) is small, which is the key reason a 2027 financing can be done without taking a position on the outcome of the case.

### Common student errors

1. **Using the market capital structure for the regulatory return** and the authorised structure for the market WACC; the two serve different purposes.
2. **Forgetting to gross up the equity return for tax** when computing the pre-tax allowed return.
3. **Using end-of-year rate base for EBIT in the same year**, thereby removing regulatory lag.
4. **Treating equity issuance as value destroying** because it dilutes EPS, without noting that equity issued above book value is accretive to book value and earns the allowed return.
5. **Ignoring the interest on the year's own new debt** or counting it for a full year when only half a year applies.
6. **Using reported debt only**, thereby missing the agency's adjustment.
7. **Setting the payout to 55% without computing the DPS in the first year**, which is a cut.
8. **Concluding that an earned ROE above the allowed ROE is a success**, when it reflects leverage above the authorised structure and invites a hypothetical-structure adjustment.
9. **Forgetting issuance costs** (net proceeds are 97.5% of gross for the ATM).
10. **Computing the downgrade cost on the full debt balance immediately**, rather than on new issuance and refinancing as it occurs.

### Alternative defensible answers

*Accept the downgrade risk.* Question 8 shows that the direct cost of a one-notch downgrade is below the earnings cost of the equity that avoids it. A board with a strong view that the Commission will treat debt costs as pass-through could choose a target of 15.0% and issue only $0.5 billion to $0.8 billion of equity. This answer requires a clear view of the non-linear risks (the next notch is junk) and an acceptance of the dividend and the plan being stressed by a bad order.

*Use hybrids more heavily.* If management believes that Keystone's 50% equity credit will persist, then a larger hybrid programme would minimise dilution. The risk is concentration of exposure to a single agency's methodology and the heavier fixed charges.

*Slow the programme.* If the rate case is unfavourable, a deferral of the 2030 and 2031 capital expenditure (the Other category rises sharply in those years) would relieve the funding need by hundreds of millions of dollars at a cost to the growth in rate base.

*Seek a minority investor in a subsidiary.* A utility could sell a minority interest in the transmission business to an infrastructure fund, raising equity-like capital without issuing common stock. The case does not present this option, but it is common in practice and could be raised in discussion.
