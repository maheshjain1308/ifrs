# Chapter 9: Case: Quantra Systems, Dividend or Buyback?

*Quantra Systems, its executives, its shareholders, its competitors and every number in this case are fictional and illustrative. Any resemblance to real companies or people is coincidental. The tax rates are stylized so that the arithmetic is transparent; they are not a statement of any actual tax code. This chapter applies the payout theory of Volume 1, Chapter 16 (dividends, repurchases, signaling and clienteles), the capital structure analysis of Volume 1, Chapter 15, and the market-efficiency and behavioral material of Volume 1, Chapter 12.*

## The Situation

On Monday, 9 November 2026, Priya Raman-Duval, Chief Financial Officer of Quantra Systems, opened a calendar entry that had been sitting on her screen for three weeks: "Board Capital Committee, Wednesday 9 December, 10:00. Item 1: Use of excess cash." The committee would decide how to return a large pile of idle cash to shareholders, in what form, and on what timetable. Whatever it chose would be announced alongside fourth-quarter results on 28 January 2027, three days before the window opened in which shareholders could nominate directors for the spring annual meeting.

Quantra Systems is a mid-cap company, headquartered in a mid-sized inland city, that sells analytics software bundled with specialized edge-computing appliances to manufacturers, utilities and logistics operators. Roughly 58 percent of FY2026 revenue is recurring subscription and support; the remainder is appliance sales and implementation services, which are cyclical because customers buy hardware in refresh waves every four to five years. The company was founded in 1998 by Edmund Quantrell, a former equipment engineer, who still chairs the board emeritus and whose family trust holds 14 percent of the shares. The chief executive since 2019 is Dr. Mirela Okonkwo-Hart, a former head of product, who is respected internally for a disciplined product roadmap and criticized externally for an unwillingness to "do something" with the balance sheet.

The balance sheet is the problem, or the opportunity. At the end of FY2024 Quantra held $1.64 billion of cash and short-term investments; at the end of FY2025, $2.05 billion; the FY2026 forecast is $2.60 billion. The company earns about 4.8 percent pre-tax on that cash, far below the 7.5 percent earnings yield the market currently assigns to the equity (EPS of $3.60 on a share price of $48.00). It pays a regular quarterly dividend of $0.20 per share and buys back roughly $200 million of stock per year, which retires about 4 million shares a year against roughly 1.2 million shares issued under employee equity plans (the diluted share count has drifted down from 206.0 million to 200.0 million in two years). Net of those two programs, cash builds by more than half a billion dollars each year.

### The people

- **Priya Raman-Duval, CFO.** Eight years at Quantra after a career in corporate banking. Believes in a dividend policy that "management can defend in a downturn." She has to present the recommendation.
- **Dr. Mirela Okonkwo-Hart, CEO.** Prefers repurchases because she believes the shares are worth well above the market price: her internal DCF puts intrinsic value at about $58 per share. She also wants to keep dry powder for acquisitions.
- **Harold Weiss-Banerjee, Chair of the Capital Committee.** A retired industrial finance executive and the board's most skeptical voice about buybacks: "Show me why paying $55 for something we say is worth $58 is a good use of money, and show me why it is better than giving the cash back evenly."
- **Edmund Quantrell, Chair Emeritus.** His family trust has a tax basis of $1.50 per share. He dislikes dividends ("a dividend is the government's cut of my cash flow") but also dislikes selling, because the trust's long-term plan is to hold the stock until estate planning events.
- **Tomas Eklund, Treasurer.** Responsible for liquidity, the rating agency relationship and the execution plan, whichever instrument is chosen.
- **Meridian Capital Partners, activist fund.** On 14 October 2026 Meridian disclosed a 6.0 percent position (12.0 million shares, bought during the second and third quarters at an average of $41 per share). Its letter demands a $2.5 billion buyback within six months, funded by the cash plus $1.0 billion of new debt, and two board seats. It has said it is prepared to nominate directors at the 2027 annual meeting.

### The decision

The Capital Committee has framed four candidate actions and asked the CFO to evaluate them on a common basis, with a recommendation that can be defended to long-term holders, to Meridian, to the rating agencies and to the employees whose bonuses depend on EPS:

1. **Special dividend.** A one-time distribution of $7.50 per share, $1,500 million in total, with no change to the regular dividend.
2. **Open-market repurchase.** A $1,500 million authorization executed over about five months at an expected average price of $52.80 (a 10 percent premium to today's price, reflecting the price pressure of the program and the possibility of a positive announcement effect).
3. **Tender offer.** A modified Dutch auction for $1,500 million at prices between $52 and $58, completed in the first quarter of 2027.
4. **Meridian's proposal.** A $2,500 million tender offer at $56, funded with $1,500 million of cash and $1,000 million of new ten-year senior notes at 6.4 percent.

Underneath those four choices sit the questions that Volume 1, Chapter 16 teaches us to ask. Is the payout a pure financial transaction, or does it carry information? Who among Quantra's shareholders is made better off after tax, and who is made worse off? What does EPS accretion really measure? And how much of the cash is truly "excess" once acquisitions, contingencies, offshore cash and the credit rating are taken seriously?

### The deadline

The committee meets on 9 December, with materials due to directors on 2 December. Because the fiscal-year close on 31 December begins the blackout, open-market purchases cannot start before the 28 January earnings release, and a tender launched before then would have to be filed in December. Ms. Raman-Duval has about three weeks to produce a recommendation.

## Industry Context

Quantra operates in the industrial analytics and edge-computing segment, which sits between enterprise software and industrial equipment. Three structural features of the industry shape the payout decision.

**Cyclicality with a recurring core.** Subscription and support revenue is sticky, with gross retention in the low nineties, and carries gross margins above 80 percent. Appliance and services revenue is lumpy: customers defer refresh cycles when capital budgets tighten, and a pause in the cycle can shave 10 to 15 percent off total revenue in a single year while operating costs, particularly research and development, are largely fixed. This asymmetry is why Ms. Raman-Duval insists on testing every payout commitment against a downside year, not merely a base case. It also explains why Quantra's EBIT margin has ranged from roughly 9 percent in a trough year to above 15 percent in a peak year.

**Low capital intensity, high cash accumulation.** Capital expenditure runs at about 4.7 percent of revenue, and amortization of acquired intangibles, rather than physical depreciation, is a large non-cash charge. Free cash flow therefore exceeds net income by a comfortable margin, and cash builds quickly unless it is deployed. The industry's historical answer has been acquisitions of small analytics firms, but integration risk and price discipline have left many peers with idle cash and a growing set of activist shareholders.

**Peer payout practice has diverged.** Among the five listed peers shown later in this chapter, one pays no dividend and relies exclusively on repurchases, two combine a low-yield dividend with continuous buybacks, and two pay a high-yield dividend with little or no repurchase. There is no single industry norm. Investors in the sector therefore sort themselves into clienteles: growth-oriented funds that prefer reinvestment and buybacks, income-oriented funds and retail holders that prefer dividends, and event-driven investors who want large, lumpy returns of capital.

**Regulatory and tax environment (stylized).** For the purposes of this case, the jurisdiction taxes qualified dividends received by taxable individuals at 24 percent and long-term capital gains at 20 percent. Tax-exempt institutions and employee retirement plans pay neither tax. Foreign institutions suffer a 15 percent withholding tax on dividends and are not taxed on capital gains. Mutual funds and ETFs are tax-transparent, and their end-investors, a mix of taxable and tax-deferred accounts, face blended effective rates of 12 percent on dividends and 8 percent on gains. The corporate tax rate is 21 percent. The jurisdiction also levies a 1 percent excise tax on the value of net share repurchases, payable by the company, and a special-purpose "offshore cash" regime under which cash moved from foreign subsidiaries to the parent is subject to a 5 percent leakage cost. Dividends and repurchases are both permitted from retained earnings, and the company has ample distributable reserves.

### Further complications

1. **Offshore cash.** Of the $2,600 million of cash, $900 million sits in foreign subsidiaries. The domestic requirement for a $1,500 million distribution is the distribution itself plus the $300 million Verdana payment, the $400 million domestic operating minimum and the $100 million domestic buffer: $2,300 million in total against $1,700 million of domestic cash. About $600 million must therefore be repatriated, at a 5 percent leakage cost. The Treasurer's excess-cash figure does not reflect this $30 million cost.

2. **The March 2027 maturity.** If the $400 million of 4.6 percent notes are repaid from cash instead of refinanced, gross debt and cash both fall by $400 million, net debt is unchanged, but the Treasurer's "excess" would be recomputed as $1,100 million on a gross basis. Some directors read the Treasurer's 1,500 as a net figure; others as gross. The treatment of this item is ambiguous in the committee papers.

3. **Verdana Analytics.** The $300 million acquisition has a purchase price adjustment clause that could add up to $80 million if Verdana meets earn-out targets in 2027 and 2028. The earn-out is not in the Treasurer's table.

4. **Litigation.** A patent claim (the Brandt matter) has a reasonably possible loss range of $60 million to $150 million, unreserved. The contingency buffer of $200 million is meant to cover it among other things, but the General Counsel advises that a loss could arrive in the same quarter as a cyclical downturn.

5. **Excise tax on repurchases.** The 1 percent excise tax applies to the open-market program and the tender (net of any issuance by the company during the year). It does not apply to dividends. The Treasurer's EPS analysis ignores it. Employees' share issuance of about 1.2 million shares a year (worth roughly $58 million) would offset a small portion of the base.

6. **Price pressure and execution.** Average daily volume is 1.9 million shares. Limiting the company to 15 percent of daily volume implies the open-market program would take about 100 trading days, so the assumed 10 percent premium is a judgment, not a measurement.

8. **Dividend history.** The company has raised its dividend every year since 2012, by 4 to 6 percent annually, and never cut it. A "dividend growth" investor mandate that two large fund holders follow requires uninterrupted increases.

9. **Basis data.** The register's average tax bases come from a custodian survey and are rough. In particular, the 14 percent held by the founders' trust has a basis so low that almost the whole proceeds of any sale would be gain; the trust also has a stated intention to hold until an estate-planning event, at which point (under the stylized code) gains on shares held at death are not taxed.

10. **Disclosure.** An authorization to repurchase shares is not an obligation. In the last three years Quantra completed 90 percent of its announced authorization on average, but the committee papers show that two of its peers completed under half of theirs.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Distinguish genuinely excess cash from idle cash, using a cash-need schedule that includes operating minimums, committed acquisitions, contingencies and repatriation frictions.
2. State the Modigliani-Miller payout irrelevance proposition and identify which assumption each of taxes, information, agency and transaction costs relaxes (Volume 1, Chapter 16).
3. Compute EPS accretion, derive the break-even repurchase price, and explain why accretion is an accounting outcome and not a value outcome.
4. Quantify the value transfer between selling and continuing shareholders, and analyze investor clienteles by after-tax cash.
5. Calibrate a regular dividend with the Lintner model, compare signals by the cost of mimicry, and design a tender offer.
6. Recognize the agency problem in EPS-based pay, stress-test a leveraged proposal against covenants, and write a board recommendation that is robust to uncertainty about intrinsic value.

### Solution to Question 1: Excess cash and capacity

**(a) Reconstructing and adjusting the Treasurer's figure.** The Treasurer's figure follows from Exhibit 9:

```
Cash and short-term investments            2,600
less minimum operating cash (10% x 6,000)   (600)
less Verdana acquisition                    (300)
less contingency buffer                     (200)
Indicated excess cash                       1,500
```

Three adjustments are needed.

*Offshore leakage.* A $1,500 million distribution must come from the parent. The parent's domestic need is 1,500 (distribution) + 300 (Verdana) + 400 (domestic operating minimum) + 100 (domestic buffer) = 2,300. Domestic cash is 1,700, so 2,300 less 1,700 = 600 must be repatriated, at 5 percent leakage = 30.

*Verdana earn-out.* The contingent consideration of up to 80 is a real claim on cash within 24 months and belongs in the cash-need schedule.

*Circularity.* Because the leakage depends on the distribution D, the exact solution is found from the identity 2,600 = D + 300 + 80 + 600 + 200 + 0.05 x (D less 820), where the repatriation need is D + 880 - 1,700 = D - 820:

```
2,600 = D + 1,180 + 0.05D - 41
1.05 D = 1,461
D = 1,391.4
```

The first-order shortcut, 1,500 - 30 - 80 = 1,390, is accurate to within $2 million. The adjusted excess cash is therefore about **$1,390 million** on the refinancing reading.

*March 2027 maturity.* If the $400 million notes are repaid from cash, the Treasurer's gross excess falls to 1,100 before the other adjustments, and to about 990 after leakage and the earn-out. This is not a computational difference but a choice among claimants: $400 million either goes to shareholders (and the debt is refinanced) or goes to bondholders (and shareholders get less). The comparison:

| Reading | Distribution to shareholders | Gross debt after | Cash after Verdana | Net debt after |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: refinance the notes | 1,390 | 1,200 | 880 | 320 |
| B: repay the notes | 990 | 800 | 880 | (80) |

Reading B leaves Quantra with net cash; reading A leaves a small net debt. The difference matters for the rating but is slight, given a 6.0 percent refinancing cost against 4.8 percent earned on cash; reading A costs about 0.012 x 400 x 0.79 = $4 million a year after tax relative to reading B in carry. The committee should adopt reading A and clearly record that the figure is *net of* the March 2027 refinancing.

**(b) Covenants and rating guardrail.** After distributing 1,390 and paying leakage of 30, cash at the 31 December 2026 balance date is 2,600 - 1,390 - 30 = 1,180, falling to 880 after the Verdana payment in Q1 2027.

| Test | FY2026E basis | FY2027E base | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gross debt / EBITDA | 1,200 / 1,160 = 1.03x | 1,200 / 1,270 = 0.94x | 2.5x (rating review) |
| Net debt / EBITDA (after Verdana) | 320 / 1,160 = 0.28x | 320 / 1,270 = 0.25x | 3.0x (covenant) |
| EBIT / interest expense | 860 / 60 = 14.3x | 950 / 60 = 15.8x | 4.0x (covenant) |
| Gross debt capacity at 2.5x | 2,900 (headroom 1,700) | 3,175 (headroom 1,975) | |

All tests pass with very large margins in the base case. The right lesson is that the base case is not the binding case; Question 9 repeats the test in the downside.

**(c) Cumulative cash available over FY2027E and FY2028E.** Start with cash of 2,600 and add free cash flow before distributions of 986.7 for FY2027E and 1,067.1 for FY2028E (Exhibit 4). Subtract Verdana (300) and the earn-out (80). The ending floor at 31 December 2028 is 10 percent of FY2028E revenue (683.7) plus the 200 buffer = 883.7.

```
2,600.0 + 986.7 + 1,067.1 - 300 - 80 - 883.7 = 3,390.1
```

Quantra can therefore distribute about **$3,390 million** over the two years with no new debt, before leakage and ignoring the interest lost on distributed cash. Allowing gross debt of 1.0x FY2028E EBITDA (1,379) adds 1,379 - 1,200 = 179 of new debt, for **$3,569 million**. Because the regular dividend (about $160 million a year) and the continuing $200 million program are inside this total, incremental capacity above the current policy is about 3,390 - 2 x 360 = $2,670 million. The question for the committee is therefore one of timing and form, not of whether the capacity exists. The interest lost on distributed cash is modest: each $1,000 million distributed reduces annual net income by about $38 million (1,000 x 4.8% x 0.79).

### Solution to Question 2: The Modigliani-Miller benchmark

**(a) Wealth equivalence for 1,000 shares.** Before any action, equity value is 200.0 x 48 = 9,600, and 1,000 shares are worth $48,000.

*Special dividend.* The company pays 7.50 per share, $1,500 million in total. The ex-dividend equity value is 9,600 - 1,500 = 8,100, so the ex-dividend price is 8,100 / 200 = $40.50. The holder has 1,000 x 7.50 = $7,500 in cash and 1,000 x 40.50 = $40,500 in stock: **$48,000**.

*Repurchase at $48.* The company buys 1,500 / 48 = 31.25 million shares, leaving 168.75 million. Equity value is 8,100 and the price remains 8,100 / 168.75 = $48.00. The company bought 31.25 / 200 = 15.625 percent of each holder's shares if the repurchase is pro rata. The holder sells 156.25 shares for $7,500 and keeps 843.75 shares worth 843.75 x 48 = $40,500: **$48,000**. A holder who does not tender holds 1,000 shares worth $48,000 and owns a larger fraction of the company (1,000 / 168.75 million versus 1,000 / 200 million), but the same wealth.

**(b) Prices.** Ex-dividend price: $40.50 (a fall of exactly $7.50). Post-repurchase price: $48.00 (unchanged). The distinction is crucial: the *per-share price drop* is a feature of the dividend, not a loss of value. A student who reads "the price falls 15.6 percent" as a loss has confused price with wealth.

**(c) What the benchmark omits.** Taxes (dividends are taxed immediately, gains only on sale and only on the gain portion, Question 5); information (a repurchase at a price that differs from intrinsic value transfers wealth, Question 4, and may signal, Question 6); agency (cash in managers' hands is subject to wasteful spending, which supports distribution of excess cash in either form, but compensation tied to EPS biases managers toward repurchases, Question 8); and transaction costs (a holder who wants cash from a repurchase-only policy must sell and pay trading costs, while a holder who wants no cash under a dividend policy must reinvest and pay costs, which supports clienteles).

### Solution to Question 3: EPS accretion

**(a) Pro forma FY2026E EPS.** Baseline: net income 720, 200.0 million shares, EPS $3.60. Using $1,500 million of cash costs foregone interest of 1,500 x 4.8% = $72.0 million pre-tax, or 72.0 x (1 - 0.21) = **$56.88 million after tax**. Pro forma net income is 720 - 56.88 = 663.12 in every option.

| Option | Shares repurchased (m) | Shares after (m) | Net income | EPS (US$) | Accretion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Special dividend $7.50 | 0 | 200.00 | 663.12 | 3.32 | -7.9% |
| Repurchase at $48.00 | 31.250 | 168.75 | 663.12 | 3.93 | +9.2% |
| Repurchase at $52.80 | 28.409 | 171.59 | 663.12 | 3.86 | +7.3% |
| Repurchase at $54.00 | 27.778 | 172.22 | 663.12 | 3.85 | +6.9% |
| Repurchase at $52.80 with 1% excise tax | 28.409 | 171.59 | 662.55 | 3.86 | +7.3% |

For the excise-tax row the extra cash cost is 15 (1 percent of 1,500), which costs a further 15 x 4.8% x 0.79 = 0.57 of net income. Excise tax is therefore a real cash cost of $15 million but a negligible EPS effect (about 0.1 percent of EPS). The dividend is dilutive to EPS because the lost interest reduces earnings but the share count is unchanged. Arithmetic example: 663.12 / 171.59 = 3.8645; 3.8645 / 3.60 - 1 = 7.35 percent.

**(b) Break-even price.** Let c be the after-tax yield on cash (0.048 x 0.79 = 0.03792), E net income, N shares, and X dollars used. Pro forma EPS exceeds current EPS when

```
(E - cX) / (N - X/P) > E / N
N(E - cX) > E(N - X/P)
-N c X > -E X / P
E / (N P) > c
```

so accretion requires that the repurchase **earnings yield** EPS / P exceed the after-tax cash yield, and the break-even price is P* = EPS / c = 3.60 / 0.03792 = **$94.94**. At the current price the earnings yield is 3.60 / 48.00 = 7.50 percent, nearly double the 3.79 percent after-tax yield on cash, so every price below $94.94 is accretive. The break-even tells us the test is nearly meaningless: Quantra could repurchase at $90 and call it accretive. Accretion measures only the spread between the earnings yield and the after-tax yield on cash; it says nothing about whether $90 is a good price.

**(c) The constant P/E fallacy.** A naive analyst multiplies the old P/E of 48.00 / 3.60 = 13.33 by the new EPS of 3.8645 to forecast a price of $51.53, a 7.4 percent gain. The error: the old P/E blended the market's valuation of operations and of cash, with cash valued at about $1 per dollar but earning only 3.79 percent after tax (a P/E of 26 on cash earnings). Removing low-yielding cash *lowers* the blended multiple. The value-consistent calculation is: equity value after the repurchase is 9,600 - 1,500 = 8,100; shares are 171.59; price = 8,100 / 171.59 = **$47.21** (if purchased at $52.80), P/E = 47.21 / 3.8645 = **12.2x**. If purchased at $48, the price is exactly $48.00 and the P/E is 48.00 / 3.9296 = 12.2x. A buyback re-rates the stock only if it changes the market's view of cash flows, risk or governance, not by arithmetic.

**(d) Meridian's debt-financed tender.** Meridian proposes $2,500 million at $56: shares repurchased = 2,500 / 56 = 44.64 million, leaving 155.36 million.

```
Lost interest income on 1,500 of cash, after tax:   1,500 x 4.8% x 0.79 = 56.88
Interest on 1,000 new notes, after tax:             1,000 x 6.4% x 0.79 = 50.56
Pro forma net income: 720 - 56.88 - 50.56 = 612.56
EPS = 612.56 / 155.36 = 3.94, accretion +9.5%
```

Meridian's "roughly 10 percent" is right. But compare with a cash-only tender of $1,500 million at $56 (26.79 million shares, EPS 663.12 / 173.21 = 3.83, +6.3 percent): the extra $1,000 million of debt adds only 3.2 percentage points of accretion. The spread is thin: the incremental earnings yield is 3.60 / 56 = 6.43 percent against an after-tax debt cost of 6.4 x 0.79 = 5.06 percent, a margin of 1.4 points on $1,000 million, about $14 million of earnings a year, bought with permanent exposure to the downside. Credit metrics: gross debt 2,200 / FY2026E EBITDA 1,160 = 1.90x (1.73x on FY2027E); net debt (2,200 - 1,100) / 1,160 = 0.95x; EBIT / interest expense = 860 / 124 = 6.9x. These are acceptable in the base case, but the guardrail at 2.5x gross has a cushion of only 0.6x, which is eliminated by a 24 percent fall in EBITDA. Question 9 shows that the downside case breaches.

### Solution to Question 4: Value transfer

**(a) Derivation.** Let V be intrinsic value per share, N = 200.0 million, X = $1,500 million, P the price paid, and n = X / P shares retired. Intrinsic equity value before is V x N. After paying X in cash for n shares, it is V x N - X, spread over N - n shares:

```
V' = (V x N - X) / (N - n)
Gain per continuing share = V' - V = n (V - P) / (N - n)
```

The gain is positive only when P < V. The total transfer to continuing holders is n(V - P), and sellers lose exactly that amount relative to holding. A repurchase is a zero-sum bet between sellers and stayers.

**(b) Grid.** Values of V' (percentage change from V in parentheses):

| V | P = $48.00 | P = $52.80 | P = $54.00 | P = $55.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $44 | 43.26 (-1.7%) | 42.54 (-3.3%) | 42.39 (-3.7%) | 42.26 (-4.0%) |
| $48 | 48.00 (0.0%) | 47.21 (-1.7%) | 47.03 (-2.0%) | 46.89 (-2.3%) |
| $52 | 52.74 (+1.4%) | 51.87 (-0.2%) | 51.68 (-0.6%) | 51.53 (-0.9%) |
| $58 | 59.85 (+3.2%) | 58.86 (+1.5%) | 58.65 (+1.1%) | 58.47 (+0.8%) |
| $64 | 66.96 (+4.6%) | 65.85 (+2.9%) | 65.61 (+2.5%) | 65.42 (+2.2%) |

Worked cell (V = $58, P = $52.80): n = 28.409; V' = (11,600 - 1,500) / 171.591 = 58.86; gain = 28.409 x 5.20 / 171.591 = $0.86.

The break-even is P = V. At the CEO's own estimate of $58, a repurchase at $55 creates only $0.47 per continuing share (n(V - P) = 27.27 x 3 = $81.8 million, divided by 172.73 million shares). A one percent change in the purchase price moves value by about as much as the whole gain.

**(c) Probability-weighted.** With weights 25/45/30 on V = 44, 52, 64, expected V = 0.25 x 44 + 0.45 x 52 + 0.30 x 64 = $53.60. At P = $52.80 the expected V' = 0.25 x 42.54 + 0.45 x 51.87 + 0.30 x 65.85 = $53.73, a gain of $0.13 (0.2 percent). At P = $55.00 expected V' = $53.38, a loss of $0.22. The expected value to stayers is effectively zero, so the case for repurchase cannot rest on a bet that the market is wrong. It must rest on tax efficiency, flexibility, signaling or a lack of better uses for the cash.

### Solution to Question 5: Taxes and clienteles

**(a) After-tax cash per share held.** A pro rata tender at $54 buys 1,500 / 54 = 27.78 million shares, or 13.889 percent of each holder's position; proceeds are 0.13889 x 54 = $7.50 per share held, matching the dividend. Tax on the tender is the capital gains rate times (54 - basis) x 0.13889.

| Clientele | Dividend tax | After-tax dividend | Tender tax | After-tax tender |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Founders' trust | 1.800 | 5.700 | 1.458 | 6.042 |
| Retail, taxable | 1.800 | 5.700 | 0.500 | 7.000 |
| Tax-exempt and employee plans | 0.000 | 7.500 | 0.000 | 7.500 |
| Mutual funds and ETFs | 0.900 | 6.600 | 0.156 | 7.344 |
| Foreign institutions | 1.125 | 6.375 | 0.000 | 7.500 |
| Meridian | 1.800 | 5.700 | 0.361 | 7.139 |

Sample calculations: founders, dividend 0.24 x 7.50 = 1.80; tender 0.20 x (54 - 1.50) x 0.13889 = 0.20 x 7.292 = 1.458. Retail, tender 0.20 x (54 - 36) x 0.13889 = 0.20 x 2.50 = 0.50.

**(b) Aggregate tax.** Dividend: 28.0 x 1.80 + 30.0 x 1.80 + 48.0 x 0.90 + 18.0 x 1.125 + 12.0 x 1.80 = 50.4 + 54.0 + 43.2 + 20.25 + 21.6 = **$189.45 million** (12.6 percent of the distribution). Tender: 28.0 x 1.458 + 30.0 x 0.50 + 48.0 x 0.1556 + 12.0 x 0.361 = 40.83 + 15.0 + 7.47 + 4.33 = **$67.63 million** (4.5 percent). The difference of $121.8 million is a deadweight transfer to the tax authority under the dividend, borne entirely by taxable holders and by foreign holders through withholding. In a real tender, high-basis, tax-exempt and foreign holders would tender disproportionately and the founders would not, so the actual tax on the tender would be lower still.

**(c) Ex-dividend price drop.** If the marginal investor is a taxable individual, a price drop equals the dividend scaled by (1 - 0.24) / (1 - 0.20) = 0.95, so the price falls $7.125 to about $40.875, not $7.50 to $40.50. The stock falls less than the dividend because holders value a dollar of price at $0.80 after tax but a dollar of dividend at $0.76.

**(d) Winners and losers.** The founders, who hold 14 percent with basis $1.50, are hurt most by a dividend: $1.80 of tax per share held. If they never sell and the stylized code forgives gains at death, the effective tax cost of a buyback to the family is zero, so they strongly prefer a repurchase, and an even larger one raises their stake from 14.0 to 16.2 percent. Tax-exempt holders (32 percent) are indifferent. Foreign institutions prefer the repurchase because the withholding is avoided. Income-seeking retail holders may prefer the dividend for convenience, but the tax difference ($1.80 vs $0.50) more than covers the cost of selling 0.156 share, which at a 10 basis point trading cost is about $0.01 per share held. A dividend-growth mandate would value an uninterrupted regular dividend, so the regular dividend should not be sacrificed.

### Solution to Question 6: Signaling and dividend policy

**(a) Lintner.** FY2027E EPS is $3.96 (Exhibit 4). Target dividend = 0.35 x 3.96 = $1.386. New dividend = old + speed x (target - old) = 0.80 + 0.30 x (1.386 - 0.80) = **$0.976**, or about $0.244 a quarter. Rounding to $0.25 a quarter ($1.00 a year) is a 25 percent increase and a payout of 1.00 / 3.96 = 25.3 percent, up from 22.2 percent today (0.80 / 3.60). The increase is far above the 4 to 6 percent pace of recent years and is itself a statement.

**(b) What each action signals.**

| Action | What it says | Cost of mimicry | Credibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Special dividend | Cash is temporarily abundant | Low: any firm with cash can pay it | Weak signal; no commitment |
| Regular dividend increase | Earnings are durable | High: cuts are punished (dividend-growth mandates, ratchets) | Strong |
| Open-market authorization | Shares are cheap | Moderate: an overvalued firm loses (V = 44, P = 52.80 loses 3.3 percent for stayers); but it is not an obligation | Moderate |
| Tender offer | Shares are cheap, at a premium | Higher: binding, and priced above market (V = 44, P = 55 loses 4.0 percent) | Strong |

The tender separates a firm worth $58 (+0.8 percent for stayers) from one worth $44 (-4.0 percent), a gap of 4.8 points, about the premium itself. Costly signaling works only where a bad type finds mimicry costly.

**(c) Bayesian update.** The prior that Quantra is worth $58 (and not $44) must be consistent with a price of $48: p x 58 + (1 - p) x 44 = 48 gives p = 4/14 = 28.6 percent. If an overvalued firm mimics with probability q, the posterior is p' = p / (p + (1 - p) q), and the price is 44 + 14 p'.

| Mimic probability q | Posterior p' | Price (US$) | Change vs $48 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 100.0% | 58.00 | +20.8% |
| 0.1 | 80.0% | 55.20 | +15.0% |
| 0.3 | 57.1% | 52.00 | +8.3% |
| 0.6 | 40.0% | 49.60 | +3.3% |
| 1.0 | 28.6% | 48.00 | 0.0% |

The more costly it is for weak firms to mimic, the greater the price reaction. This is why the form of commitment (fixed-price tender over open-market authorization) matters more than the amount.

### Solution to Question 7: Tender offer design

**(a) Clearing price.** For each price p, the company can afford 1,500 / p million shares. The offer clears at the lowest price at which cumulative shares offered covers that quantity.

| Price | Shares offered (m) | Shares affordable (m) | Fully subscribed? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $52 | 12 | 28.85 | No |
| $53 | 19 | 28.30 | No |
| $54 | 26 | 27.78 | No |
| $55 | 31 | 27.27 | Yes |

The clearing price is **$55**; the company buys 1,500 / 55 = 27.273 million shares. Pro-ration = 27.273 / 31 = **87.98 percent**: every holder who offered at or below $55 sells 87.98 percent of its tendered shares at $55.

**(b) Ownership.** After the tender: 200 - 27.273 = 172.727 million shares. Meridian, which tendered its 12.0 million at $52, sells 12.0 x 0.8798 = 10.557 million for $580.6 million (a gain of about $147.8 million over its $41 basis) and retains 1.443 million shares, or 0.84 percent. The founders (not tendering) hold 28.0 / 172.727 = **16.21 percent**. Under open-market buying at $52.80 (28.409 million shares, 171.591 million remaining), the founders hold 28.0 / 171.591 = 16.32 percent and Meridian, which is not forced to sell, holds 12.0 / 171.591 = 6.99 percent.

**(c) Comparison.**

| Feature | Open market | Fixed-price tender | Dutch auction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premium paid (illustrative) | 4.80 x 28.41 = $136 million | 6.00 x 27.78 = $167 million at $54 | 7.00 x 27.27 = $191 million at $55 |
| Certainty of size | Low (non-binding) | High | High |
| Speed | About 100 trading days | About 20 business days | About 20 business days |
| Price discovery | None | None | Reveals holders' reservation prices |
| Effect on activism | Meridian stays and stays vocal | Meridian can exit | Meridian can exit |

The tender pays a higher premium for certainty and speed, and it gives Meridian a profitable exit that reduces the pressure of a proxy contest. In expected value terms the open market is cheaper, but it is the weaker signal and leaves the activist in place.

### Solution to Question 8: Compensation and agency

**(a) FY2027E pro forma EPS and bonus.** FY2027E status quo net income is 791.7 (Exhibit 4). Each $1,500 million action costs 56.88 of after-tax interest; assume execution at the start of the year.

| Action | Net income | Shares (m) | EPS (US$) | Payout vs target | CEO bonus |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Status quo | 791.7 | 200.00 | 3.96 | 85% | $2.04m |
| Special dividend | 734.8 | 200.00 | 3.67 | 0% | $0.00 |
| Open market at $52.80 | 734.8 | 171.59 | 4.28 | 200% (capped) | $4.80m |
| Tender at $55 | 734.8 | 172.73 | 4.25 | 200% (capped) | $4.80m |

Payout for status quo: 25 + (3.96 - 3.80) / 0.20 x 75 = 85 percent. If the open-market program runs evenly from February to June, the average weighted shares are 200 - 28.41 x (8.5 / 12) = 179.88 and lost interest is 56.88 x 0.7083 = 40.29, so EPS = 751.4 / 179.88 = $4.18 and the payout is about 189 percent, or $4.53 million. The conclusion survives.

**(b) The problem.** Two actions with identical shareholder value (Question 2) differ by $4.8 million in executive pay. The plan rewards EPS that is created by shrinking the share count, which is exactly what Question 3(b) showed to be unrelated to value. A director who wants a dividend is asking executives to forgo their entire bonus.

**(c) Neutralization.** Add back the after-tax interest on cash used and restore repurchased shares at the weighted-average timing, so EPS is measured on the pre-distribution capital base: (734.8 + 56.88) / (171.59 + 28.41) = 791.7 / 200 = **$3.96** in every option. Alternatives: pay on free cash flow per share with a capital charge, or on return on invested capital (which is unaffected by distributions). The Compensation Committee should adopt neutralization before the board announces the decision, or the choice will look self-interested.

### Solution to Question 9: Stress test and policy flexibility

**(a) Cases.** Hybrid program: $1,200 million tender at $55 (21.82 million shares retired, 178.18 million remaining) plus a regular dividend of $1.00 per share ($178.2 million). Lost interest on $1,200 million is 45.50 after tax. Operating cases: base (revenue 6,420, margin 14.8 percent); downside (revenue 5,520, a fall of 8 percent from FY2026E, margin 9 percent, working capital release of 30); severe (revenue 5,100, margin 6 percent, release of 60).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 950.2 | 746.2 | 961.2 | 1,270.2 | 5.4x | 2.5x |
| Downside | 496.8 | 388.0 | 678.0 | 816.8 | 3.8x | 1.8x |
| Severe | 306.0 | 237.3 | 557.3 | 626.0 | 3.1x | 1.5x |

Net income base: (950.2 + 112 - 60) x 0.79 - 45.50 = 746.2. FCF = net income + 320 + 220 - 280 + working capital effect (for the base case the increase of 45 cancels with the 1.2 of... we use the Exhibit 4 convention of -45 in the base case). The dividend is covered more than three times in every case, so the hybrid is robust, and there is room to maintain a smaller flexible buyback.

**(b) Meridian's program.** Gross debt 2,200, interest expense 60 + 64 = 124.

| Case | Gross debt / EBITDA (limit 2.5x guardrail) | EBIT / interest (covenant 4.0x) | Net debt / EBITDA (covenant 3.0x) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 2,200 / 1,270.2 = 1.73x | 950.2 / 124 = 7.7x | 0.9x |
| Downside | 2,200 / 816.8 = **2.69x (breach)** | 496.8 / 124 = 4.01x (0.2 percent headroom) | 1.3x |
| Severe | 2,200 / 626.0 = **3.51x (breach)** | 306.0 / 124 = **2.47x (covenant breach)** | 1.8x |

(Net debt uses cash of 1,100 for simplicity.) Meridian's proposal leaves no margin for a refresh pause of the kind the industry has experienced and could trigger a rating review in the downside and an event of default in the severe case. The leverage in Quantra's current structure (1,200 of debt) would have gross leverage of 1.5x and 1.9x in those same cases and interest cover of 8.3x and 5.1x.

**(c) Flexible framework.** (i) A regular dividend at 25 percent of normalized EPS, grown with earnings, sized so that it is covered at least three times by severe-case FCF. (ii) Repurchases as the residual: each year, 60 percent of FCF after dividends and committed acquisitions (for FY2027E base: (961 - 178) x 60 percent = about $470 million), reduced when the share price is above 90 percent of the intrinsic value range. (iii) A gross-debt-to-EBITDA range of 0.8x to 1.5x through the cycle, tested at downside-case EBITDA. (iv) Special dividends only when cash exceeds the target for two successive years.

### Solution to Question 10: Recommendation

The recommended structure (the hybrid, run as a package): (1) a **$1,200 million modified Dutch auction tender offer** at $52 to $58, launched immediately after the 28 January earnings release; (2) a **regular dividend increase to $0.25 per quarter**; (3) retention of the balance of the adjusted excess, about $175 million, as a flexible reserve for opportunistic repurchases below $52 and for the Verdana earn-out; (4) a committed **gross leverage range of 0.8x to 1.5x**; and (5) neutralized EPS in the bonus plan.

**Why this form.** Tender: tax efficiency (Question 5), credible signal (Question 6), certainty and speed, and it gives Meridian an exit. The $1,200 million size: it leaves the adjusted excess intact after the $12 million excise tax and about $16 million of repatriation leakage (repatriation of 1,200 x 1.01 + 300 + 400 + 100 - 1,700 = 312, times 5 percent). It also respects the sell-side median of $54, so the expected gain is nearly zero without a premium for the $58 case. At V = $58 and P = $55, stayers gain (58 x 200 - 1,200) / 178.18 = $58.37, or 0.6 percent; at V = $44 they lose 3.1 percent; the package is robust to the main uncertainty because the dividend increase and the leverage range carry the benefit that does not depend on valuation. FY2027E pro forma EPS is 746.2 / 178.18 = $4.19, 5.8 percent above the 3.96 status quo.

### Common student errors

1. Calling the dividend "dilutive" and the buyback "accretive" as if EPS were value, or applying the old P/E to the new EPS.
2. Ignoring foregone interest, so that accretion appears to be 15.6 percent (the share count reduction) rather than 7.3 percent.
3. Treating the stock price drop on the ex-dividend date as a loss.
4. Forgetting that the repurchase price premium is paid by stayers, and sizing the program at the market price.
5. Taking Treasurer's excess cash at face value without the offshore, earn-out and maturity adjustments.
6. Comparing taxes at the statutory rates only, forgetting that the capital gains tax is on the gain, not the proceeds.
7. Reading the Dutch auction as a pay-as-bid auction: all accepted shares get the uniform clearing price.
8. Testing leverage only in the base case.
9. Assuming a signal exists without asking who finds it costly to mimic.
10. Using intrinsic value of $58 as a fact.

### Alternative defensible answers

*A pure buyback ($1,500 million).* Defensible if the committee holds firm to V = $58 and accepts a thinner dividend story; it maximizes tax efficiency and flexibility. The weakness: the premium paid, the commitment problem, and the bonus problem.

*A special dividend.* Defensible if the committee believes the shares are fairly priced or believes the buyback would mainly benefit an activist who bought at $41; it avoids the value-transfer risk. The weakness: a $122 million higher tax bill, and the founders' strong opposition.

*Meridian's proposal.* Defensible only for a company with far less cyclicality. A staged version (a 1.5x ceiling, with half deployed in 2027 and half in 2028 subject to the downside test) is a reasonable compromise: debt of 1,740 gives downside leverage of 1,740 / 816.8 = 2.13x and interest cover of 496.8 / 94.6 = 5.3x.

*Hold the cash for acquisitions.* Defensible only with a specific pipeline; the cash earns 3.79 percent after tax against a 7.5 percent earnings yield, which is a visible drag, and the board's record on acquisitions is limited.
