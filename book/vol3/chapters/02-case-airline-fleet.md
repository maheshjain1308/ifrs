# Chapter 2: Case: Norwind Air, a Fleet Renewal Decision

*Illustrative case. Norwind Air Group, its people, Aerovance Industries, Lakeshore Aircraft Leasing and every number in this chapter are fictional and were constructed for teaching. Amounts are in $M (millions of US dollars) unless stated. The case draws on Volume 1, Chapters 7, 8, 11, 14, 19 and 20, and on Volume 2 for options and lease analysis. Norwind reports under IFRS, so IFRS 16 applies to leases.*

## The Situation

Tobias Renner, chief financial officer of Norwind Air Group, had a decision on his desk with a hard date attached. Aerovance Industries, the manufacturer of the NX-200 narrowbody, would hold twelve delivery slots for Norwind in 2027 only if Norwind signed by 15 December 2026. The board would meet on 17 November 2026. Renner had to tell it whether to replace twelve ageing aircraft with twelve NX-200s, how to pay for them, whether to hedge the fuel the new fleet would burn, and how much of the order to commit now.

Norwind is a regional and short-haul carrier based at Skarholm, flying 54 aircraft to about 70 destinations. It carries leisure and business passengers, with a heavy summer peak, and its traffic moves with the business cycle. Its fleet is described in Table 1. The twelve Series 700 aircraft, an average of 19 years old, seat 174 passengers, burn 2.95 tonnes of fuel per block hour and need two expensive heavy maintenance checks, in Years 3 and 9. The NX-200 seats 186 and burns 2.35 tonnes per block hour, 20% less. Norwind would sell the old aircraft to a freight-conversion buyer for $5.0M each.

### The people

Ingrid Halvorsen, the chief executive, wanted the whole order: "A modern fleet is the only fuel hedge that cannot be cancelled." Renner was less sure. Norwind had been close to its leverage covenant in the last downturn, and he distrusted a deal that looked good only if demand stayed strong. Captain Amara Osei, head of fleet planning, valued the commonality of one type across the short-haul network. Wei Lindqvist, the treasurer, managed fuel hedging and the banks, and had been asked to design a hedging policy for the new fleet. Professor Marcus Eddington, chair of the risk committee, asked a question that framed the case: "If we wait, what do we lose? If we go now, what do we risk?"

### The decision

The board faced five linked questions: (1) is the renewal worth doing at all; (2) should Norwind buy the aircraft with secured debt or lease them from Lakeshore, which had offered a 12-year operating lease; (3) how much fuel price risk should be hedged, and with what; (4) given that Norwind's demand is cyclical and its covenant is tight, how much of the order should be committed now; and (5) should Norwind use Aerovance's contractual rights to defer six deliveries and to buy six more aircraft at a fixed price?

"Year 0" is delivery, March 2027. Later cash flows occur at year end, and the analysis horizon is twelve years.

## Industry Context

**Cyclicality.** Airline earnings move with GDP, corporate travel budgets and leisure confidence. Capacity is added in booms and cannot be removed quickly in busts, because aircraft are bought or leased on long contracts. The result is a sequence of profitable and unprofitable years, and fleet decisions made in a boom are tested in the next bust. Many airlines have gone out of business with modern fleets and heavy leverage.

**Fuel and carbon.** Fuel is typically the largest or second-largest cost, about 15% of Norwind's revenue. Airlines recover some fuel price changes through fares, but with a lag and incompletely. New-generation aircraft use roughly a fifth less fuel per seat. Carbon regulation adds a second price on every tonne burned: Norwind's routes fall under an emissions trading scheme, and the carbon price is expected to rise.

**Aircraft values and leasing.** Aircraft are long-lived, mobile, standardised assets with active resale markets, which makes them easy to finance with secured debt and attractive to lessors. A lessor earns its return from rentals and from the residual value of the aircraft; a lessee that leases passes the residual risk to the lessor. Residual values depend on the model's popularity, the age of the aircraft at disposal and the state of the cycle, and appraisers' estimates are wide.

**Manufacturers' contracts.** Manufacturers allocate delivery slots years ahead and often sell flexibility: rights to defer deliveries for a fee, to convert orders from one model to another, or to buy additional aircraft at a pre-agreed price. These rights are options and have value, though their prices rarely reflect it explicitly.

**IFRS 16.** Since 2019 lessees under IFRS recognise most leases on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability. EBITDA no longer bears the rent, which is replaced by depreciation of the asset and interest on the liability. Operating leases therefore no longer hide leverage, but they still shift residual-value risk to the lessor, and covenant definitions determine how they count.

## Complications and Exhibits

### Exhibit A: Residual value is uncertain

Three appraisers gave 12-year resale values for the NX-200 of 35%, 52% and 60% of price. Norwind's base case uses 52%. A lessee that leases does not bear this risk; a buyer does.

### Exhibit B: Carbon price

The $65 per tonne starting price and 7% growth are a planning assumption. Policy could soften (carbon price near zero) or tighten. Renner wants to know how much of the case rests on it.

### Exhibit C: How rents are really paid

Lakeshore's monthly rent is payable monthly in advance. The model treats rent as an annual payment in arrears, which understates the cost slightly, and for IFRS 16 the lease liability is the present value of payments at Norwind's incremental borrowing rate of 6.5%.

### Exhibit D: Margin calls and liquidity

A 60% swap that is 30% out of the money loses about $66M a year in mark-to-market value, payable in cash if the counterparty requires collateral. Norwind has cash of $410M and an undrawn revolver of $300M.

### Exhibit E: Pre-delivery payments

Aerovance requires 20% of the price in four instalments before delivery. Norwind would fund PDPs on its revolver at 7.5%. Under a lease, Lakeshore would pay them, with an arrangement fee not modelled.

### Exhibit F: Items excluded

Crew training for the new type, spares provisioning and the commonality benefits of a single type are not quantified; Osei believes they roughly offset. The 12 Series 700 aircraft would otherwise be parked, not sold, in a bust, which is a further reason to prefer keeping Aerovance deliveries flexible.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Case synopsis

Norwind Air looks like a lease-versus-buy case and is really a case about risk and flexibility. The operating renewal has a thin positive NPV. The financing choice is close and turns on the residual value. Fuel is a risk that the new aircraft partly hedges and that treasury can hedge further. Demand is cyclical and the covenant is tight, so the binding question is how much to commit now. Aerovance's contractual rights are options worth more than they cost. The case suits a module on capital budgeting, leasing and risk management after Volume 1, Chapters 7, 8, 14, 19 and 20. All figures come from one Python model, checked by hand on selected years; year-end flows, the 9.5% WACC from Question 1 and Table 4 inputs are used throughout, and results are per aircraft unless stated.

### Learning objectives

Students should be able to (1) compute an airline's cost of capital from comparables; (2) build incremental after-tax cash flows for fleet replacement and decompose NPV; (3) analyse lease versus buy by the net advantage to leasing and explain the role of residual value; (4) apply IFRS 16 and test a leverage covenant under normal and bust conditions; (5) estimate a minimum-variance fuel hedge ratio and compare swaps and collars including liquidity risk; (6) treat fuel and carbon as project risks; (7) model a cyclical demand process; (8) value deferral and purchase options; and (9) write a recommendation that combines value, risk and constraints.

### Question 1 solution: Cost of capital

**Unlevering.** With zero debt beta, βU = βL / [1 + (1 − t) × D/E].

| Airline (illustrative) | Levered beta | Net debt incl. leases / equity | Tax rate | 1 + (1 − t) × D/E | Unlevered beta |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Corvid Airways | 1.88 | 1.10 | 22% | 1.8580 | 1.0118 |
| Skyreach Air | 1.64 | 0.80 | 25% | 1.6000 | 1.0250 |
| Halcyon Airlines | 1.40 | 0.60 | 20% | 1.4800 | 0.9459 |
| Brightfeather Air | 1.98 | 1.30 | 26% | 1.9620 | 1.0092 |
| Oriel Airlines | 1.53 | 0.70 | 24% | 1.5320 | 0.9987 |

The average unlevered beta is 0.998. The target net debt ratio of 40% gives D/E = 0.40 / 0.60 = 0.6667.

```
βL  = 0.998 × [1 + 0.76 × 0.6667] = 0.998 × 1.5067 = 1.504
ke  = 4.2% + 1.504 × 5.5% = 12.47%
kd  = 6.5% × (1 − 0.24) = 4.94%
WACC = 0.60 × 12.47% + 0.40 × 4.94% = 7.48% + 1.98% = 9.46%, rounded to 9.5%
```

**Why the WACC.** The renewal's operating cash flows (fuel, maintenance and seat contributions) carry the business risk of an airline, which falls on both lenders and shareholders, so they are discounted at the blended WACC, not at the cost of debt. The financing decision is a separate matter and uses the after-tax cost of debt (Question 3), because lease payments are contractual and nearly as safe as debt service (Volume 1, Chapter 14).

### Question 2 solution: Operating NPV of renewal

**Year 0.** Norwind pays $52.00M, sells the old aircraft for $5.00M and pays tax on the $3.00M gain (24% × 3.00 = $0.72M): −52.00 + 5.00 − 0.72 = **−$47.72M**. Sunk or irrelevant costs (the old aircraft's book value, past depreciation) do not enter, and the old aircraft's sale price is a relevant inflow.

**Worked Year 1.** Fuel saved per year is (2.95 − 2.35) × 3,300 = 1,980 tonnes. Year 1 fuel is 820 × 1.02 = $836.4 per tonne and carbon is 3.16 × 65 × 1.07 = $219.8 per tonne, so the saving is 1,980 × 1,056.2 = $2.091M at normal utilisation, or $2.05M with the expected utilisation index of 0.98. Maintenance saving is 3.1 × 1.03 − 1.0 × 1.03 = $2.16M. The extra-seat contribution is 0.90 × 1.02 × 0.9654 (the expected utilisation-times-yield index) = $0.89M. Incremental EBITDA is $5.10M. Tax depreciation is 52 × 0.90 / 15 = $3.12M a year, so tax is 0.24 × (5.10 − 3.12) = $0.47M and the Year 1 cash flow is **$4.62M**.

**Year 12 sale.** Book value is 52 − 12 × 3.12 = $14.56M. The expected sale price is 0.52 × 52 = $27.04M, the gain is $12.48M, tax is $3.00M and net proceeds are $24.04M.

### Question 3 solution: Lease or buy

**Method.** Leasing is a financing alternative to buying, so its cash flows are discounted at the after-tax cost of secured debt, 6.5% × 0.76 = 4.94%. Annuity factor for 12 years is 8.8933 and the 12-year discount factor is 0.5607. The annual rent is 0.80% × 12 × 52.0 = $4.99M, or $3.79M after tax.

| Item (per aircraft, $M) | Amount |
| :--- | ---: |
| Purchase price avoided (Year 0) | 52.00 |
| PV of after-tax rent (12 years at 4.94%) | (33.74) |
| PV of depreciation tax shield lost | (6.66) |
| PV of after-tax residual value lost | (13.48) |
| Net advantage to leasing (NAL) | −1.88 |

Each aircraft costs $1.88M more to lease than to buy, **−$22.6M for twelve**, on the base residual of 52%. Operating NPV plus NAL (the lease path) is 2.38 − 1.88 = $0.50M per aircraft.

**Implicit rate and break-evens.** The rate at which the lessor's flows (−52.0, rent of 4.99 for 12 years, and a 27.04 residual) have zero NPV is **6.90%**, above Norwind's 6.5% cost of debt; leasing is a higher-priced source of finance if the lessee expects the residual to hold. The break-even annual rent is $4.71M ($0.393M a month, 0.755% of price), compared with the offered $4.99M (0.80%). The break-even residual is 43.5% of price.

| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 35% | 18.20 | 1.89 | 22.6 |

**Residual matters.** Leasing becomes attractive if the residual falls below 43.5%. The three appraisals averaged 49%, giving a NAL of −$1.22M, still negative. The buyer holds the residual-value risk and the upside; a lessee holds none of it and the lease contains an implicit put on the aircraft's value. Students should note that the NAL uses expected residual and ignores the value of that put, so the true benefit of leasing is higher than the table suggests. They should also note two other effects: leasing preserves debt capacity (Question 4) and avoids the PDPs (Question 9), and Exhibit C's monthly-in-advance payment makes true rent costlier than modelled, which strengthens the case for buying. A defensible answer buys when the board has confidence in the residual and needs the ownership, and leases when covenant headroom is scarce.

### Question 4 solution: IFRS 16 and the covenant

**Lease liability at inception.** The present value of 12 rent payments of $4.992M at 6.5% is 4.992 × 8.1536 = **$40.73M per aircraft, $489M for twelve**; the right-of-use asset is the same at inception. Schedule for the first three years:

| Year | Opening liability | Interest 6.5% | Rent paid | Closing liability | ROU depreciation |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 40.73 | 2.65 | 4.99 | 38.38 | 3.39 |
| 2 | 38.38 | 2.49 | 4.99 | 35.89 | 3.39 |
| 3 | 35.89 | 2.33 | 4.99 | 33.23 | 3.39 |

Year 1 expense under IFRS 16 is interest $2.65M plus depreciation $3.39M = $6.04M, against a cash rent of $4.99M: expenses are front-loaded. EBITDA excludes the rent, so it is $4.99M per aircraft higher than under the old rules.

**Leverage.** Pro forma net debt includes the old aircraft's $60M sale proceeds. EBITDA adds the renewal's operating benefit: $61.2M in a normal year and $50.6M in a bust. The base EBITDA is $520M normal and $395M in a bust.

| Plan | Pro forma net debt incl. leases | EBITDA, normal year | Net debt/EBITDA | EBITDA, bust year | Net debt/EBITDA |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| No renewal (today) | 1,110 | 520 | 2.13x | 395 | 2.81x |
| Buy 12 (debt-funded) | 1,674 | 581 | 2.88x | 446 | 3.76x |
| Lease 12 | 1,539 | 581 | 2.65x | 446 | 3.45x |
| Buy 6, lease 6 | 1,606 | 581 | 2.76x | 446 | 3.60x |

**Interpretation.** In a normal year every plan complies (2.65x to 2.88x). In a bust, buying all twelve breaches the 3.5x covenant (3.76x) and a six-and-six split also breaches (3.60x); only leasing all twelve complies (3.45x), and with just 0.05x of headroom. Leasing produces lower measured leverage because the lease liability (489) is lower than the debt needed to buy (about $564M net of sale proceeds): the lessor keeps the residual and the lease liability excludes it. **Compliance in the bust state, not NPV, is the binding constraint on the size of the order.** With no renewal, a bust leaves Norwind at 2.81x. The maximum net debt at 3.5x in a bust is $1,560M, below the $1,606M of the six-and-six plan.

### Question 5 solution: Fuel hedging

**(a) Hedge ratio.** If a fraction (1 − θ) of a fuel price change is not recovered through fares, the EBITDA change is −(1 − θ) × fuel bill × (fuel price change), and the minimum-variance hedge covers that fraction of consumption. Regress ΔEBITDA on ΔFuel (as fractions): mean fuel change 3.89%, mean EBITDA change $0.20M, Σ(x − x̄)² = 0.6001, Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) = -140.5228.

```
slope = −140.52 / 0.6001 = −$234.2M per 100% change (−$2.34M per 1%)
h*    = 234.2 / 369 = 0.635, about 63% of consumption (≈ 285,600 tonnes)
R²    = 0.92, standard error of slope = $23.7M
95% interval for h* (t = 2.306, 8 d.f.) = 49% to 78%
```

The implied pass-through through fares is about 36%. The interval is wide because there are ten observations.

**(b) Risk reduction.** The standard deviation of the annual EBITDA change is √[((slope + h × 369)² × σF²) + σε²], with σF = 25.8% and a residual standard deviation of $18.3M:

| Hedge ratio | Std dev of EBITDA change ($M) |
| :--- | ---: |
| 0% | 63.2 |
| 60% | 18.6 |
| 63% (minimum variance) | 18.3 |
| 100% | 39.4 |

A 60% hedge removes about 70% of the volatility, and a 100% hedge is worse than 60%, since it makes Norwind net long fuel. The remaining $18M is demand noise that a fuel hedge cannot touch.

**(c) Swap versus collar** (60% of consumption; the collar buys a call at $900 and sells a put at $740, at zero cost; no-hedge EBITDA assumes 38% pass-through and base $520M).

| Fuel price ($/t) | Change | EBITDA no hedge | EBITDA, 100% swap | EBITDA, 60% swap | EBITDA, 60% collar |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 574 | -30% | 589 | 478 | 522 | 544 |
| 656 | -20% | 566 | 492 | 521 | 543 |
| 738 | -10% | 543 | 506 | 521 | 542 |
| 820 | +0% | 520 | 520 | 520 | 520 |
| 902 | +10% | 497 | 534 | 519 | 498 |
| 984 | +20% | 474 | 548 | 519 | 497 |
| 1,066 | +30% | 451 | 562 | 518 | 496 |
| 1,230 | +50% | 406 | 590 | 516 | 495 |

The swap flattens EBITDA but gives up the benefit of falling prices (−30% gives 522 against 589 unhedged). The collar keeps most of the benefit down to $740, at the cost of exposure above $900. A 100% swap makes EBITDA fall as prices fall. A swap or collar is a hedge of EBITDA, not a source of value, since fair-priced derivatives have zero expected value (Volume 1, Chapter 19); the benefit is lower distress cost and covenant protection.

**(d) Covenant and liquidity.** For the buy-12 plan in a normal year with fuel +50%:

| Hedge policy | EBITDA at fuel +50% (buy plan, normal demand) | Net debt/EBITDA |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| No hedge | 467 | 3.59x |
| 60% swap | 577 | 2.90x |
| 100% swap | 651 | 2.57x |

Without hedging, a fuel spike alone takes leverage to 3.59x, a breach. In the worst combination, a bust with fuel +50%:

| Hedge policy | EBITDA in bust with fuel +50% | Buy 12 | Lease 12 |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| No hedge | 331 | 5.05x | 4.65x |
| 60% swap | 442 | 3.79x | 3.48x |

Even a 60% hedge leaves buy-12 in breach (3.79x), while lease-12 hedged just complies (3.48x). The cost of hedging is margin liquidity: a 60% swap with fuel down 30% loses $-66.4M of mark-to-market in a year, which Norwind could need to post as cash, against cash of $410M and a $300M undrawn revolver. Collars lose less on the downside, and a policy that hedges 50% to 65% of net consumption, uses collars for part, and agrees collateral thresholds with counterparties is defensible.

### Question 6 solution: Fuel and carbon as project risks

| Fuel price level vs base | Year-0 price ($/t) | NPV per aircraft | NPV, 12 aircraft | IRR |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 70% | 574 | −0.44 | −5.3 | 9.3% |
| 85% | 697 | 0.97 | 11.6 | 9.8% |
| 100% | 820 | 2.38 | 28.5 | 10.3% |
| 115% | 943 | 3.78 | 45.4 | 10.8% |
| 130% | 1,066 | 5.19 | 62.3 | 11.3% |
| 150% | 1,230 | 7.07 | 84.8 | 11.9% |

Each 10% change in fuel price moves NPV by $0.94M per aircraft ($11.3M for twelve). The break-even fuel price is **74.7% of base, about $612 per tonne**. A fall of a quarter in fuel, plausible after a spike, erases the operating NPV.

| Carbon price Year 0 ($/t CO2) | NPV per aircraft | NPV, 12 aircraft |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | −0.75 | −9.0 |

If carbon pricing is abandoned, NPV is −$0.75M per aircraft (−$9.0M for twelve). The case therefore rests on a policy variable as much as on a commodity.

**Natural hedge.** The renewal's benefit rises when fuel rises: it reduces Norwind's exposure by 12 × 1,980 = 23,760 tonnes a year, 5.3% of consumption. The firm as a whole is hurt by high fuel; the renewal is worth more in exactly that state. Hedging policy should therefore apply the hedge ratio to post-renewal net consumption (426,240 tonnes, so about 268,531 tonnes at 63%) and not to the pre-renewal figure. The cost of this logic is that low fuel prices hurt twice, on the renewal's NPV and on the swap, while the airline's base operations gain; the board should view the three together.

### Question 7 solution: Cyclical demand

**NPV by state** (the state fixed for twelve years):

| Demand state (every year) | Utilisation index | Yield index | NPV per aircraft | NPV, 12 aircraft | IRR |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Boom | 1.08 | 1.35 | 6.38 | 76.6 | 11.7% |
| Normal | 1.00 | 1.00 | 2.91 | 34.9 | 10.5% |
| Bust | 0.82 | 0.45 | −2.76 | −33.1 | 8.5% |
| Markov expectation (start Normal) | – | – | 2.38 | 28.5 | 10.3% |

The Bust state turns the project into a loser (−$2.76M per aircraft, IRR 8.5%) and the Boom state gives $6.38M. The deviations from Normal are asymmetric: a boom adds $3.47M and a bust removes $5.67M, because the yield index falls further below one in a bust (0.45) than it rises in a boom (1.35).

**State probabilities.** From the Normal start, P(t) = P(0) × Pᵗ:

| Year | P(Boom) | P(Normal) | P(Bust) | E[utilisation] |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | 20.0% | 60.0% | 20.0% | 0.980 |
| 2 | 24.0% | 52.0% | 24.0% | 0.976 |
| 3 | 24.8% | 50.4% | 24.8% | 0.975 |
| 6 | 25.0% | 50.0% | 25.0% | 0.975 |
| 12 | 25.0% | 50.0% | 25.0% | 0.975 |

The stationary distribution solves π = πP and is 25% / 50% / 25% (Boom / Normal / Bust). The chain mixes quickly; by Year 3 the probabilities are within half a point of stationary. Expected utilisation settles at 0.975, below 1.00 because the bust (0.82) outweighs the boom (1.08) in the expectation. The cycle costs $0.54M of NPV per aircraft relative to a permanently Normal economy (2.91 against 2.38).

**Simulation.** Over 20,000 simulated 12-year paths, mean NPV is $2.39M per aircraft (matching the analytic 2.38), the standard deviation is $1.38M, the 5th percentile is $0.01M and the 95th $4.53M; the probability of a negative NPV is 5%. Fleet-wide, mean $28.7M and standard deviation $16.5M. **NPV is a poor measure of this risk:** the cycle averages out over twelve years and the project looks safe on a lifetime basis. The risk is the bust year in which leverage breaches, which is why the covenant, not the NPV, governed Question 4.

### Question 8 solution: Option to defer six deliveries

**Setup.** S = PV of operating benefits of a delivered aircraft = $50.10M; K = net outlay = 52.00 − 5.00 + 0.72 = $47.72M; immediate NPV = $2.38M. With σ = 25%, Δt = 1 year, risk-free rate 4.2% and yield forgone δ = 10%:

```
u = e^0.25 = 1.2840     d = 1/u = 0.7788
q = [e^(0.042 − 0.10) − d] / (u − d) = (0.9436 − 0.7788) / 0.5052 = 0.3263
Strike at year t = 47.72 × 1.04^t + 0.8 (t ≥ 1)
```

Early exercise is allowed, and because δ exceeds the risk-free rate, waiting costs cash flow, so exercise at an in-the-money node can be optimal.

| Year | Node | Project value S | Strike incl. escalation and fee | Exercise value | Option value | Best action |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0 | start | 50.10 | 47.72 | 2.38 | 4.35 | wait |
| 1 | u | 64.33 | 50.43 | 13.90 | 13.90 | exercise |
| 1 | d | 39.02 | 50.43 | 0.00 | 0.00 | wait |
| 2 | uu | 82.60 | 52.41 | 30.18 | 30.18 | exercise |
| 2 | ud | 50.10 | 52.41 | 0.00 | 0.00 | lapse |
| 2 | dd | 30.39 | 52.41 | 0.00 | 0.00 | lapse |

At Year 2: uu 30.18; others lapse. At the up-node in Year 1, exercising yields 13.90, while waiting is worth e^(−0.042) × 0.3263 × 30.18 = 9.44, so exercise. At the down-node, the project is out of the money and waiting costs nothing. At the start, immediate exercise gives 2.38 and waiting gives e^(−0.042) × 0.3263 × 13.90 = **$4.35M**. The deferral right is worth $1.97M more per aircraft than buying now, **$11.8M for six aircraft** ($26.1M against $14.3M).

Volatility drives the value:

| Volatility | Option value per aircraft | Premium over immediate NPV | Six aircraft |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 15% | 2.38 | 0.00 | 14.3 |
| 20% | 3.20 | 0.82 | 19.2 |
| 25% | 4.35 | 1.97 | 26.1 |
| 30% | 5.49 | 3.11 | 32.9 |
| 35% | 6.63 | 4.25 | 39.8 |

At about 16% volatility the right is worth no more than immediate exercise; below that, buy now. The fee matters little:

| Deferral fee per aircraft ($M) | Option value per aircraft | Six aircraft |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| 0.00 | 4.60 | 27.6 |
| 0.80 | 4.35 | 26.1 |
| 1.50 | 4.13 | 24.8 |
| 2.40 | 3.85 | 23.1 |
| 3.50 | 3.50 | 21.0 |

**Interpretation and limits.** A positive-NPV project can still be better deferred when uncertainty is high and the cost of waiting is moderate. The result depends on δ: waiting forgoes $5M a year of benefits per aircraft, and at 10% the leakage is large, yet the option is still worth $2M extra. The model ignores that aircraft not delivered must be replaced by costly maintenance on the old fleet, which is already inside δ, and that Aerovance's slots may disappear.

### Question 9 solution: Purchase rights and PDPs

**(a) Black–Scholes with a yield.** C = S e^(−δT) N(d1) − K e^(−rT) N(d2), with S = 50.10, K = 47.72, T = 3, r = 4.2%, δ = 10%, σ = 25%.

```
S × e^(−0.30)   = 37.11        K × e^(−0.126) = 42.07
d1 = [ln(50.10 / 47.72) + (0.042 − 0.10 + 0.5 × 0.0625) × 3] / (0.25 × √3) = -0.0731
d2 = d1 − 0.4330 = -0.5061     N(d1) = 0.4709     N(d2) = 0.3064
C = 17.48 − 12.89 = $4.58M per right
```

Six rights are worth **$27.5M** against deposits of $6.0M, a net gain of $21.5M.

| Volatility | Right value per aircraft ($M) | Six rights ($M) |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| 15% | 2.08 | 12.5 |
| 20% | 3.31 | 19.9 |
| 25% | 4.58 | 27.5 |
| 30% | 5.87 | 35.2 |
| 35% | 7.15 | 42.9 |

With δ = 6% the right is worth $7.08M, so the value is sensitive to the yield assumption. The rights are cheap because the manufacturer values slot flexibility, and they should be bought only if Norwind may grow beyond a replacement programme, since aircraft purchased under them add capacity, not replace old aircraft, and the operating benefits assumed in S would differ.

**(b) PDP carrying cost.** Each 5% instalment on twelve aircraft is 0.05 × 624 = $31.2M. Interest at 7.5% for 2.0, 1.5, 1.0 and 0.5 years is 31.2 × 0.075 × 5.0 = **$11.7M** pre-tax, $8.9M after tax; total PDPs are $124.8M. Including the carrying cost reduces the fleet NPV from $28.5M to $19.6M, and under a lease the lessor funds PDPs, but its fee is not modelled.

### Question 10 solution: Recommendation

**Comparing structures** (per the base model; leverage is net debt including leases over EBITDA in a bust year; the covenant is 3.5x).

| Structure | Value ($M) | Bust-year leverage | Comment |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | :--- |
| No renewal | 0 | 2.81x | Keeps maintenance burden and fuel exposure |
| Buy all 12 now | 28.5 | 3.76x | Breaches covenant in a bust |
| Lease all 12 now | 5.9 (NPV plus NAL) | 3.45x | Passes with 0.05x of headroom; costs $22.6M of NAL |
| Buy 6 now, defer 6 with option | 40.3 (6 × 2.38 plus 6 × 4.35) | 3.31x | Passes; option value captured |
| Lease 6 now, defer 6 with option | 6 × (2.38 − 1.88) plus 26.1 = 29.1 | 3.15x | Passes; lower value than buying |

**Recommended structure.** Buy the first six aircraft, defer the other six by one year using the Aerovance right, and decide in Year 1 whether to exercise. This is worth about $40.3M against $28.5M for all twelve now, an improvement of $11.8M, and keeps bust-year leverage at 3.31x. If a bust seems likely when the second batch is due, lease it, accepting the NAL cost in return for covenant headroom. The Year 1 decision rule should combine value and constraint: exercise if the demand state is not Bust and projected bust-year leverage after delivery is no more than about 3.2x.

**Hedging.** Hedge 55% to 65% of next-12-month post-renewal net consumption, about 270,000 tonnes at 63%, using a mix of swaps and collars with collateral thresholds, so that the margin outflow in a 30% fuel fall stays within the revolver. Re-estimate the hedge ratio annually.

**Options.** Do not buy the purchase rights as part of the renewal. They are worth about $27M against $6M of deposits, but only if Norwind grows beyond replacement, a question for the strategy review.

### Sensitivity discussion

Operating NPV is thin: it disappears if fuel is 25% lower, if the carbon price is zero, or if the maintenance saving is overstated. Financing value is small and sign-flips with residual value (the break-even is 43.5% against 52%). Option values are large relative to NPV because the base project is close to the money and volatility is 25%. And the covenant constraint, not the NPV, decides the size of the commitment, so a plausible change to the covenant definition or to the bust-state EBITDA changes the answer more than any change in discount rate.

### Common student errors

1. **Discounting operating cash flows at the cost of debt,** or discounting the lease-versus-buy flows at the WACC (Questions 1 and 3).
2. **Omitting the tax on the sale of the old aircraft,** or treating its book value as a cost.
3. **Counting fuel savings at the base fuel price only,** forgetting the carbon price, or leaving out utilisation.
4. **Comparing the lease rate with the coupon on debt** instead of computing the NAL.
5. **Ignoring the residual value** or treating it as certain.
6. **Treating IFRS 16 as irrelevant to leverage,** or computing the covenant with the pre-renewal EBITDA.
7. **Assuming a fuel swap adds value,** or hedging 100% of consumption, which overshoots the minimum-variance ratio.
8. **Valuing the deferral as a European call** without the yield forgone, which gives a value of zero or below.
9. **Using NPV standard deviation as the measure of cyclical risk** and concluding the project is safe.

### Alternative defensible answers

The model recommendation (buy six, defer six) is not unique. **Lease all twelve** is defensible if the board wants maximum covenant headroom and treats the residual put as valuable: the NAL cost of $22.6M is the price of insurance. **Buy all twelve with equity support** is defensible if Norwind can raise equity or negotiate a covenant at 4.0x. **Do nothing** is defensible if the board doubts the carbon price, since NPV is negative without it. A student who treats deferral as costless, or who uses a lower yield forgone, will find an even larger deferral value and may recommend deferring all twelve; this is sound if slots are available later. The hedge ratio could reasonably be set anywhere in the interval of 49% to 78%. Assessment should reward a clear decision rule, correct mechanics and an explicit treatment of the covenant.
