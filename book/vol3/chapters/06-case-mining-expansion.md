# Chapter 6: Case: Ironvale Copper, a Mine Expansion under Commodity Risk

*Ironvale Copper Corp., its Karsten mine and Ridge deposit, Ledgerline Capital, Kestrel Royalty Partners, the lenders, and every peer company in this chapter are fictional and illustrative. All numbers were constructed for teaching and are not statistics about any real company, mine, or market. The chapter draws on Volume 1, Chapter 6 (NPV), Chapter 7 (capital budgeting), Chapter 8 (uncertainty and real options), Chapter 11 (WACC), Chapter 14 (debt), Chapter 18 (options), Chapter 19 (derivatives), and Chapter 20 (risk management).*

## The Situation

On Thursday, 10 December 2026, the board of Ironvale Copper Corp. will be asked to approve a final investment decision (FID) on the largest project in the company's history: a $1.9 billion expansion of its Karsten copper mine into the adjacent Ridge deposit. The vote is five weeks away. On the board's table will be a funding plan that includes a $500 million equity raise, a project finance facility from three international banks, and a hedging program that the banks have made a condition of their loan. The banks' credit approvals lapse on 18 December, a fixed-price engineering contract offered by the main contractor lapses on 31 January 2027, and the equity market window the company's brokers favour is the second half of January.

Ironvale operates Karsten, an open-pit mine and concentrator at 3,400 metres in a remote highland district, which produced 76,400 tonnes of copper in 2026 at a C1 cash cost of $2.15 per pound. The company was assembled in 2011 from two bankrupt mines by Halvard Pryce, its chief executive, who took the listed shell public in 2015. Imogen Tarrant, the chief financial officer, joined in 2019 after fourteen years in project finance. Lucinda Okonjo, a former mining engineer and now a professional director, chairs the board. Rafael Quintero, a retired lender who sat on the credit committees of two of the banks now in the syndicate, sits on the audit committee, and he has said openly that he distrusts "any mine model whose price deck is above the forward curve."

Ridge is a separate pit on the same property. The company has defined 200 million tonnes of reserves at 0.54 percent copper, which can feed an incremental 14 million tonnes a year of throughput for fourteen years once a new concentrator train, a larger tailings facility, and a power line are built. First production would come in 2030. The technical work is mature, but the economics are not obviously compelling. Net present value is positive on the consensus price deck and roughly zero on the banks' more conservative deck, and it turns negative on the price that the futures market will pay today for copper delivered in 2030. A twenty percent overrun on capital cost would erase most of the value on the consensus deck.

You are a vice-president in corporate development, reporting to Imogen Tarrant. She has asked you for a board paper that does five things: value the Ridge project under several price decks and show what drives the answer; value Ironvale as a whole on a net asset value (NAV) basis and compare the result with the market price; evaluate the funding and hedging structure that the banks require; value the managerial flexibility that the project carries, including the option to delay, to stage the commitment, and to extend the life of the mine; and recommend whether the board should approve the project on 10 December, delay, or reject it.

Three directors have formed views. Halvard Pryce wants to proceed on the consensus deck and argues that "a mine is a long-dated bet on copper that we have been right about before." Rafael Quintero will argue that the project should be tested against the forward curve and the lenders' deck, not the consensus. Lucinda Okonjo is more concerned about execution: Ironvale has never built anything of this scale, and its last concentrator upgrade overran by 14 percent. The paper must give each of them a number they can interrogate, and it must say honestly how much of the project's value is analysis and how much is opinion about copper.

## Industry Context

Copper is a metal whose price is set on an international exchange by the balance between mine supply, scrap, and demand for wiring, pipes, motors, and the equipment of electricity grids. A copper mine is a price-taker. It sells a concentrate, a powder that is about one-quarter copper, to a smelter at a price that is a formula based on the exchange price less treatment and refining charges (TC/RCs), a fee that the smelter keeps. The miner's economics therefore depend on three prices: the metal price it cannot control, the TC/RC that it negotiates annually, and the costs of digging, hauling, and grinding rock.

The industry quotes costs per pound of payable copper. C1 is the cash cost of producing and delivering payable metal, including mining, milling, site administration, freight, and TC/RCs, net of by-product credits. All-in sustaining cost adds sustaining capital, royalties, and corporate costs. The cost curve is the ranking of all the world's mines by cost; mines at the high end of the curve set the price in weak markets because they close when the price falls below their cash cost. Copper mines are in general long-lived and slow to build, with seven to ten years from discovery to production commonly quoted, and the supply response to a price signal therefore comes late. That mismatch makes the price volatile, mean-reverting over long horizons, and difficult to forecast. Annualized price volatility around 20 to 25 percent is common in textbooks; the effect of mean reversion is that volatility over a ten-year horizon is much lower than the short-run figure would imply.

Mining companies value their assets with a net asset value model. The analyst forecasts production, prices, costs, and capital for each mine over its life, discounts the after-tax cash flows at a rate that reflects the risk of that asset, sums across assets, adds the value of exploration and resources, subtracts net debt and corporate overhead, and divides by shares. The ratio of the share price to NAV per share (P/NAV) is the central relative-value metric; it is usually below one for companies with higher risk, higher leverage, or poor delivery records, and above one for companies with growth and good jurisdictions. A NAV depends on the price deck. Analysts publish a "consensus" deck, typically a near-term forecast that converges to a long-term real price. Banks use a more conservative "lenders' deck" to size debt. Companies often present a "management" or "planning" deck, which tends to be the most optimistic. The futures market provides yet another view: the forward curve, which is the price at which anyone can lock in delivery today. For a long-lived asset, a forward curve is observable for only a few years, and the remainder must be extrapolated.

Hedging is controversial in mining. Many investors buy mining shares for their exposure to copper and regard company-level hedging as a waste of money, since investors can hedge themselves. Corporate-finance theory (Volume 1, Chapter 20) agrees that hedging cannot add value in a frictionless world, and gives three reasons it might add value in practice: it reduces expected costs of financial distress, it supports debt capacity, and it protects investment plans from being starved of cash when prices fall. Project lenders add a fourth, practical reason: they will not lend against a geared project with a single commodity exposure unless a portion of the cash flows is fixed during the years when the debt is largest. The hedging program in this case is therefore partly a financing necessity, and the company must decide the form of hedge, the volume, the floor, and the price at which the cost of protection is acceptable.

Finally, mines carry real options. A mine owner can delay a project until prices are more favourable, scale production up or down, suspend operations in a bad market, or extend the life by converting resources to reserves. Real-options theory (Volume 1, Chapter 8) values these rights using lattices or simulation, and the central finding is that flexibility is most valuable where uncertainty is high and the project's value is close to its cost. It also finds, uncomfortably, that the models often say "wait" even for projects that managers should do now, because they leave out the costs of delay: the loss of contractor slots, the expiry of financing commitments, the escalation of capital costs, and the competitive reaction. The case asks students to value flexibility and then to challenge the number.

Capital cost overruns are the other persistent hazard. Studies of large resource projects commonly find that actual capital cost exceeds the approved estimate, and the pattern is stronger for projects in remote locations and for first-time builders. A company that is approving a project at a thin margin of safety against cost has little room for error, and the board's job is to separate what the project would be worth if built to budget from what it will be worth given the record of delivery.

## Complications and Exhibits

**Exhibit A: Contractor offer and cost history.** The main contractor's fixed-price offer covers the process plant and tailings facility, which is 62 percent of the $1,900.0 million, with the balance (mining fleet, power line, owner's costs, and a 10 percent contingency allowance of about $173 million) on a cost-reimbursable basis. The engineering estimate is described as "plus or minus 15 percent." Ironvale's own last major capital project, the 2021 concentrator debottlenecking, overran by 14 percent and finished seven months late. The fixed-price offer lapses on 31 January 2027, and the contractor has said that a new offer would be priced "at then-current market rates, which we expect to be higher."

**Exhibit B: Stream offer.** Kestrel Royalty Partners has offered $400.0 million upfront, paid in the same 35/40/25 proportions as capital spending, in exchange for 15 percent of Ridge's payable copper for the life of the mine. Kestrel would pay Ironvale 20 percent of the spot price for each pound delivered. Kestrel's proposal would replace $400 million of the $500 million equity raise. Management describes it as "non-dilutive." The tax advisers believe the forgone revenue would be deductible when delivered.

**Exhibit C: Grade reconciliation.** The Ridge reserve is 0.54 percent copper. A 12-hole infill program completed in October returned an average of 0.49 percent in the northeast corner of the pit, which represents about 15 percent of the first five years of ore. At Karsten, the actual grade has averaged 4 percent below the reserve model over the past six years, and management attributes this to dilution that the model does not capture. The reserve estimate has not been revised. A further 120 million tonnes of Ridge material at a similar grade is classified as resource, not reserve, because drilling is too wide-spaced to support a mine plan.

**Exhibit D: Fiscal risk.** The government has announced a consultation on a "windfall surcharge" of 20 percent of revenue from price above $4.50 per pound. The consultation paper does not say whether the threshold would be indexed to inflation. Legal advisers say that stabilization terms in Ironvale's mining agreement protect it only against increases in the general royalty, not against new instruments. The proposal is not law and may never be.

**Exhibit E: Lenders' deck review.** The banks revise their deck semi-annually. Their next review is in May 2027. One bank's research team has published a long-term real price of $3.60, and the lead arranger has told the company that "our credit committee will not be surprised if the deck moves toward the lower end of the range." The loan documents allow the banks to size the facility to the revised deck only before first drawdown.

**Exhibit F: Permits.** The water-use permit for the new tailings facility is expected in the second quarter of 2027. The board is being asked to approve FID before it is granted; the facility agreement makes the permit a condition precedent to drawdown. Management says that the probability of refusal is "very low" but that a six-month delay is possible.

**Exhibit G: Equity placement.** Brokers have indicated that a $500 million placement, 29 percent of the existing share count, would likely price at a discount of 3 to 8 percent to the prevailing price. The case data use $6.30, a 3.8 percent discount to $6.55. One broker added that the share price might rise on announcement of the project, or fall because of the dilution, and said it was "a coin toss."

**Exhibit H: Management incentives.** Short-term bonuses at Ironvale are tied to production growth and to a project milestone in 2027 ("FID and first drawdown"), and long-term awards vest on production in 2030. No part of the incentive is tied to returns on capital or to NAV per share. Rafael Quintero has asked for the incentive terms to be tabled with the investment paper.

**Exhibit I: Hedging history.** Ironvale's board has historically hedged only small amounts. In 2024 to 2026 the realised hedging result was a loss every year (Table 1), because the collars' caps were below the market price. Some shareholders have written that they "do not want a gold-plated hedge program." The lenders' letter, however, treats the hedging condition as non-negotiable.

**Exhibit J: Forward-curve and deck mismatch.** The consensus deck of $4.25 in real terms is $4.70 per pound in nominal terms in 2030, compared with a forward price of $4.36 for the same year. The finance team notes that analysts' long-term decks are conventionally flat in real terms while futures curves are conventionally flat to modestly sloped in nominal terms, and that neither is "right."

Quantify the effect of a one-year delay in first production (assume $60 million of additional owner's costs) and of the windfall surcharge in Exhibit D under both readings of the threshold.

6. **Net asset value.** Value Karsten at the three decks, subtract corporate overhead and net debt, add resources and the hedge book, and compute NAV per share, P/NAV at the current price, and the deck implied by the market price. 7. **Financing.** Evaluate the funding plan in Table 5. Compute the number of shares issued and the dilution. Estimate the expected cost of each hedge if the consensus deck is correct. State whether hedging creates value here and recommend a program.

9. **Optionality.** Value, using a binomial lattice, the option to delay FID by one, two, and three years. Value a staged FID with $180 million of early works. Value the option to extend the mine life by converting the 120 million tonne resource. Discuss the suspension option. State what costs of delay the lattice omits and whether you would act on the result.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning Objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Assess a mining company's cost position, leverage, and capacity to fund a transformational project.
2. Distinguish price decks from forward curves, convert real decks to nominal prices, and explain why the choice of deck can change the sign of a project's NPV.
3. Build a nominal after-tax project model for a mine, with ramp-up, escalation, working capital, depreciation, closure, and a mid-year discounting convention.
4. Run scenario, break-even, grid, and tornado analyses, and identify which risks matter: price, grade, capital cost, or timing.
5. Build a net asset value for a producer, interpret P/NAV, and back out the price deck implied by the market.
6. Compare equity, project debt, and a stream as funding sources, and recognise that "non-dilutive" is not "free."
7. Price a zero-cost collar with Black's model, simulate debt service cover with and without hedging, and judge whether hedging creates value or only manages financing risk.
8. Value real options with a binomial lattice, interpret the results with humility about omitted costs of delay, and value a staged commitment.
9. Weigh quantitative output against execution risk, and write a conditional recommendation.

#### Solution 2: Price decks and the forward curve

The nominal price for year t equals the real price times 1.025 raised to the power (t - 2026). For the consensus deck, 2030 is 4.25 times 1.025^4 = 4.69; 2040 is 4.25 times 1.025^14 = 6.00; 2045 is 6.79. For the lenders' deck, $3.85 real is $4.25 nominal in 2030 and $5.44 in 2040. The forward curve is 4.30, 4.32, 4.34, 4.36, and 4.38 for 2027 to 2031, then rises at 2 percent a year: 4.47 (2032), 4.56, 4.65, 4.74, 4.84, 4.93, 5.03, 5.13, and 5.23 (2040), reaching 5.78 in 2045. The real-2026 equivalent of the 2031 forward price is 4.38/1.025^5 = $3.87, and of the extrapolated 2040 price is 5.23/1.025^14 = $3.70.

Three conclusions follow. First, the lenders' deck is close to the forward curve in real terms ($3.85 versus $3.87 in 2031), so the lenders are, in effect, pricing at the market. Second, the consensus deck is 10.4 percent above the lenders' deck and about 10 percent above the forward curve in 2031, and the gap is larger (about 15 percent) by 2040, because the deck is flat in real terms while the extrapolated curve falls in real terms. Third, the decks differ because they answer different questions: the forward curve is a price at which cash can be locked in, an analyst's deck is an expectation about the price in a mean-reverting market that will, on average, eventually reflect the cost of marginal supply, and a lender's deck is a conservative case chosen to size debt. A board approving a $1.9 billion project should not rely on any one of them. A reasonable standard is that the project should be approved only if it earns its cost of capital on the market (forward) deck, or if the board is prepared to explain why it believes it knows the price of copper better than the market does. Rafael Quintero's position is therefore defensible: the case for approval should be tested at the forward-based NPV and at the lenders' deck. Management's position is defensible if the board accepts that approving a mine is a bet on mean reversion.

#### Solution 3: Cost of capital

The target debt to capital is 35 percent, so D/E is 0.35/0.65 = 0.5385. Relevered equity beta is 0.92 times (1 + (1 - 0.27) times 0.5385) = 0.92 times 1.3931 = 1.282. Cost of equity is 4.3% + 1.282 times 5.0% = 10.71 percent. The after-tax cost of debt is 7.25% times 0.73 = 5.29 percent. WACC is 0.65 times 10.71% + 0.35 times 5.29% = 6.96% + 1.85% = **8.81 percent, rounded to 8.8 percent**.

The same rate is used for Ridge and Karsten. Karsten is a producing mine in the same jurisdiction, so no operating-risk premium is warranted, and it is a good practice for a NAV to apply one rate consistently so that differences in value reflect cash flows. For Ridge, a premium of 0.5 to 1.0 point during construction would be defensible, because completion, ramp-up, and cost-overrun risks are not diversifiable on a single-mine, single-commodity project, and 9.8 percent produces an NPV of $167.0 million (Solution 5), still positive. Using the cost of the financing package (the 7.25 percent facility rate) would be wrong: debt is not a cheaper source of capital in itself, because it raises the cost of equity. Using the rate on the stream as the discount rate would equally be wrong.

#### Solution 8: Hedging

Black's model for a European put on a forward price F with strike K, volatility sigma, time T, and discount rate r gives P = e^(-rT) times [K N(-d2) - F N(-d1)], where d1 = [ln(F/K) + 0.5 sigma^2 T]/(sigma sqrt(T)) and d2 = d1 - sigma sqrt(T). For 2030: F = 4.36, K = 3.80, T = 3.5, sigma = 24 percent. Then d1 = [ln(1.1474) + 0.5 times 0.0576 times 3.5]/(0.24 times 1.8708) = (0.1375 + 0.1008)/0.4490 = 0.5307; d2 = 0.0817; N(-d1) = 0.2978; N(-d2) = 0.4674; and the discount factor is e^(-0.14) = 0.8694. The put is 0.8694 times (3.80 times 0.4674 - 4.36 times 0.2978) = 0.8694 times (1.7761 - 1.2984) = $0.4153 per pound. The zero-cost cap K_c solves the call price = 0.4153, which gives $5.255.

| Consensus deck | Collars, floor $3.80, 50% | 1.64 | 0.84 | 1.00 | 21.7% | 9.9% | -24.1 |
| Consensus deck | Swaps, 50% | 1.66 | 1.08 | 1.23 | 8.8% | 3.3% | -67.3 |
Three results matter. First, an unhedged, geared single-mine structure is fragile: even if the consensus deck is correct, there is a 20 percent chance that cover falls below 1.0 times at some point in the first four years and a 30 percent chance it falls below the 1.20 times lock-up. A prudent lender could not accept this without protection, which explains the condition. Second, swaps reduce the probability of a sub-1.0 outcome to 3 percent (consensus) or 8 percent (lenders), and collars to 10 or 20 percent. Collars leave more upside, but they leave the tail unprotected between the floor and the level at which cover fails. Third, the expected cost depends on whose deck is right. If the consensus deck is right, the collars give up $24 million of present value and the swaps $67 million, because the forward price is below the deck; if the lenders' deck is right, the collars are worth $4 million and the swaps cost $9 million. That cost is not a loss in the sense of a fair-value shortfall: at forward prices both hedges are worth about zero, so the cost is the price of disagreeing with the market.

Does hedging create value? In the Modigliani-Miller sense, not directly. The expected payoff of a zero-cost hedge at forward prices is zero. Value creation, if any, comes through (a) making the financing possible, since without it the project cannot be debt-funded at 50 percent gearing; (b) lowering the probability of financial distress during the years of maximum debt; and (c) protecting the investment plan. The recommendation is to adopt the lenders' requirement in its cheapest form: 50 percent of Ridge volumes for 2030 to 2033 hedged with zero-cost collars with a $3.80 nominal floor (caps of $5.26, $5.36, and so on), placed progressively rather than all on the day of financial close, to avoid locking in a single price. A swap is not recommended, because the protective benefit above that of the collar is smaller than its expected cost on the board's own deck. Note also that the $3.80 nominal floor is 13 percent below the 2030 forward and 11 percent below the lenders' 2030 nominal price of $4.25, so it works as insurance and not as a price view.

#### Solution 9: Optionality

The valuation uses a binomial lattice for the project's gross present value V, defined as the PV of post-construction cash flows at the WACC, with V0 = 307.8 + 1,691.9 = $1,999.7 million; the exercise price X is the PV of the capital spending, $1,691.9 million, escalating at 6 percent a year if FID is deferred. The volatility of V equals the long-run effective copper volatility of 18 percent times the elasticity of V to price, 1.69 (computed from NPV at $4.24 and $4.26), or 30.5 percent, so each annual step multiplies V by u = 1.356 or d = 0.737. The risk-neutral drift is the risk-free rate less the project's cash yield: V grows at 2.5 percent (inflation) in expectation, and its required return is 8.8 percent, so the cash yield forgone by waiting is 6.3 percent and the risk-neutral growth rate is 4.0% - 6.3% = -2.3 percent. The probability of an up-move is p = (0.977 - 0.737)/(1.356 - 0.737) = 0.387. Option values are discounted at 4.0 percent, and the option can be exercised at each year-end.

For the one-year option, at t = 1 the project value is $2,711.9 million (up) or $1,474.5 million (down), against a strike of 1,691.9 times 1.06 = $1,793.4 million; the up state pays 918.5 and the down state is out of the money, so the option is worth 0.387 times 918.5/1.04 = $342.0 million, which exceeds the intrinsic value of $307.8 million by $34.2 million. The option to delay is worth something, but on the consensus deck it is worth only about 11 percent of the NPV for one year, because the project's NPV is a substantial fraction of V and the cost of waiting, 6.3 percent a year of forgone cash yield plus capital escalation, is high. The option value is sensitive to volatility: at 12 percent the project should be built now; at 26 percent, waiting three years is worth $237 million more. On the lenders' deck the project is just out of the money and the option to wait is worth $181 million, which is a measure of what flexibility offers a marginal project.

The lattice omits costs of delay that are material here. The fixed-price contract and the banks' commitments lapse, so a deferral must be re-priced (Exhibits A and E); Ironvale's brokers' equity window may close; bonuses are tied to FID (Exhibit H); the construction schedule and the pit sequence would slip; competitors could secure contractors or power. Any deferral cost over about $34 million (for a one-year delay) erases the option value on the consensus deck. Thus the lattice says that waiting is not clearly better than proceeding if the base case is credible, and that waiting is valuable only if the project is marginal and volatile. Students who answer "wait" on the strength of the lattice should be asked what they would pay to keep the contractor's price.

Staged FID. Spend $180 million now on early works and long-lead items (a part of the $1,900 million), with a 12-month gate, with the remaining capital cost fixed, and a salvage value of 25 percent of the early works if abandoned. The value is -180 + [p times max(V_u - (X0 - 180), 45) + (1 - p) times max(V_d - (X0 - 180), 45)]/1.04. Up: 2,711.9 - 1,511.9 = 1,200.0; down: 1,474.5 - 1,511.9 < 0, so the abandon value of 45 applies. The value is -180 + [0.387 times 1,200.0 + 0.613 times 45.0]/1.04 = $293.3 million, slightly below the value of investing now ($307.8 million). The staged option is valuable only in worse states of the world: at the lenders' deck it is worth $132.5 million (against -10.8 now), and at the management deck it costs $80 million relative to proceeding. So staging is insurance whose premium (about $14 million on the consensus deck) is small relative to the protection it gives against a bad outcome, which is a reasonable bargain if the board thinks the lenders' deck is plausible, but it is not a free lunch.

Resource conversion. The 120 million tonnes of resource could add eight to nine years of production after 2043 if it converts to reserve and $250 million of stripping capital is spent in 2042 and 2043. On the consensus deck, the present value of the extension today is about $548 million (the cash flows are far in the future, but the nominal margins are large), $446 million on the lenders' deck, and $638 million on the management deck. At a 35 percent probability of conversion, the expected value is $192 million on the consensus deck, less roughly $30 million of present-value drilling and study cost, about $162 million, equal to half of Ridge's NPV. This is the largest single source of optionality, and it is exercised, if at all, in 2040, after the project has been paid for. It is not in the NAV, the banks do not credit it, and the market probably does not either. It deserves a place in the memo as qualitative support, with the caveat that its value depends on prices fifteen years out and that it should not be added to the NPV in the base case.

Suspension. Ridge's cash cost, including TC/RC, is about $1.71 per pound in 2026 dollars, and Karsten's is $2.15, plus the royalty. Under the mean-reverting price model, the probability that the annual average price in 2030 falls below $2.30 (the point at which Karsten would lose cash) is below one percent, so the option to suspend is worth little and should be excluded from the valuation. Its value would rise only if costs were higher or the price much more volatile. The low-cost position of the new pit is itself a protection.

#### Solution 10: Recommendation

The evidence can be summarised in five numbers. Ridge has an NPV of $307.8 million on the consensus deck (IRR 11.2 percent), about zero on the lenders' deck (-$10.8 million), and negative on the market forward curve (-$60.1 million, IRR 8.3 percent). The project's break-even price is $3.86 real, and its break-even capital cost is $2,301 million, 21 percent above the estimate. NAV per share rises from $6.78 to $7.57 on the consensus deck after the raise. The probability of a covenant-level cash shortfall is 22 to 30 percent without full hedging if the consensus deck holds. And the company has a record of 14 percent overrun on its last project.

The project clears its hurdle on the consensus deck by a margin that is thinner than the uncertainty in the estimate: the NPV equals about 16 percent of the capital cost, a 10 percent overrun removes 47 percent of it, and a 9 percent shortfall in grade removes 92 percent. The market-forward NPV is negative by a small amount ($60 million is 3 percent of capital), and the deck-versus-forward gap is the single most important judgment. The forward curve is not a forecast of the price in 2035; copper is mean-reverting and the curve is flat in nominal terms, which understates the long-run price if the real price recovers. But a project that is valuable only if the board knows better than the market has not been shown to be good. The real-options analysis says delay has modest value ($34 million for one year) and the lattice omits the costs of delay, which here are large: the lapse of contract and finance terms, and the cost of a one-year delay in first production, about $160 million. Approving the project is therefore preferable to delaying or rejecting it, provided that the board is protected against the cases in which it is wrong.

**Recommendation: approve FID on 10 December 2026, subject to conditions precedent, with delegated authority to withdraw if any condition fails.** The conditions are:

1. A fixed-price or capped-price contract covering at least 80 percent of the capital cost (up from 62 percent), or a contingency increased to at least 15 percent of the estimate, so that the break-even overrun of 21 percent is not consumed by expected overrun.
2. Satisfactory completion of the water permit before first drawdown, as in the facility agreement, and a sponsor completion guarantee limited to the 10 percent cost-overrun facility.
3. The hedging program recommended in Solution 8: zero-cost collars with a $3.80 floor on 50 percent of 2030-2033 Ridge volumes, layered over the first six months after financial close.
4. An equity raise of $500 million, with a minimum price of $5.50 per share, and a fallback to a part-stream hybrid if the minimum cannot be met.
5. Resolution of the head-grade question: a revised reserve reconciliation, including the 12-hole infill program, before the first drawdown. If the revised grade is 0.49 percent, the NPV on the consensus deck falls to $23 million, and the board should treat that as a stop condition.
6. An incentive structure for the executive team amended so that part of the long-term award depends on return on capital or NAV per share, not just production (Exhibit H).

**Walk-away points.** The board should not proceed if the lenders' revised deck falls below $3.60 real, since the project's NPV is then about -$210 million and DSCR cover will be too thin; if the capital cost, after contracts are signed, exceeds $2,100 million (about a 10 percent overrun, at which NPV on the consensus deck is $162 million but on the lenders' deck is -$157 million); if the revised grade is below 0.50 percent; or if the windfall surcharge becomes law with a fixed nominal threshold, which would cut NPV by $114 million on the consensus deck. **Monitoring metrics:** monthly spend against the control budget with a 5 percent trigger for board review, drilled grade against the model, the forward curve against the lenders' deck, hedge coverage, DSCR forecast against 1.35 times, and NAV per share.

### Sensitivity Discussion

The valuation has four dominant sensitivities. Price matters most: the NPV falls by $338 million for a 10 percent fall in the real price, and the sign flips at $3.86. The deck-versus-forward gap is, in practice, a 10 percent price difference. Head grade is nearly as large as price, and it is the variable the company can most influence. Capital cost matters less than the other two in a symmetric sense, but its distribution is skewed: overruns are common and underruns rare, so the expected loss from capex is larger than the table suggests. The discount rate matters at about $140 to $160 million per point, so the choice between a development-stage rate and the corporate rate is worth about half the NPV. Timing risk (the one-year slip) costs $160 million, mostly from the deferral of cash flows. The windfall surcharge ambiguity is worth $114 million. Hedging and funding structure do not change NPV materially but determine whether the project can be financed and whether the company survives the downside, which is why they take priority in the recommendation.

### Common Student Errors

1. Using the real price deck without escalating it, while escalating costs, or the reverse. This changes NPV by hundreds of millions.
2. Discounting nominal cash flows at a real rate, or the reverse.
3. Forgetting working capital, or forgetting its release at closure, or omitting closure costs.
4. Treating the 2026 C1 cost of $2.15 as the Ridge cost. Ridge's cost is $1.71 per pound in real terms.
5. Using end-year discounting for a mid-year deck and cost profile and then comparing with a mid-year model.
6. Comparing the NPV on the consensus deck with the market capitalisation, or adding the NPV to the share price. NPV is value added to NAV, not value per share, and must be divided by the diluted shares after the issue.
7. Crediting the project with the accretion that arises from selling shares above NAV.
8. Believing that a hedge creates value because it "reduces risk." Zero-cost collars at forward prices have zero expected value.
9. Pricing options on the spot price instead of the forward price, or using a call price that does not equal the put price in a zero-cost collar.
10. Reading the lattice's value of waiting as a recommendation to wait, without considering the costs of delay.
11. Treating the stream as free because it is "non-dilutive."
12. Forgetting the tax shield on depreciation in the project calculation, or taking tax on negative taxable income as zero when the group can use the loss.

### Alternative Defensible Answers

The case has no unique answer. A student who weights the forward-based NPV heavily can defend deferral or rejection: the project has a negative NPV at market prices, the margin of safety on cost is thin, and the company has a poor delivery record. Such a student should still address the one-year delay cost of about $160 million and the lapse of the lenders' approvals. A student who trusts management's deck can recommend approval without conditions, on a $586 million NPV, and argue that hedging destroys $24 to $67 million of expected value; that student should be pressed on whether the project can be financed. A third defensible answer is the staged approach: authorize $180 million of early works and defer full FID to the second quarter of 2027, when the permit, the lenders' May deck review, and the grade reconciliation are known. The lattice values this at $293 million against $308 million for immediate approval on the base deck, and at $133 million against -$11 million on the lenders' deck, so it is a sound choice for a board that puts more weight on the downside than the consensus deck implies. The lapse of the contractor's fixed-price offer on 31 January weakens the case for staging. Credit should go to answers that identify which assumption their recommendation depends on and state the evidence that would change their mind.
