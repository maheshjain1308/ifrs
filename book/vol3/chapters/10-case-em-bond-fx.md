# Chapter 10: Case: Andara Foods, an Emerging-Market Bond Issue and FX Risk

*Andara Foods, the Republic of Orvania, the Orvanian sol (ORS), the banks, the investors and every number in this case are fictional and illustrative. Exchange rates, interest rates, spreads and tax rules are invented and stylized so that the arithmetic is transparent; they describe no real country or market. This chapter applies the bond and debt-market material of Volume 1, Chapters 4 and 14, the foreign-exchange and derivatives material of Volume 1, Chapters 19 and 20, and the cost-of-capital and capital structure material of Volume 1, Chapters 11 and 15.*

## The Situation

On Monday, 16 November 2026, Anneliese Dragomir-Sato, Chief Financial Officer of Andara Foods S.A., received a two-line message from the chairman: "The board wants one structure to vote on on 14 December. Not a menu." The CFO had spent six weeks assembling exactly such a menu. Her team had three term sheets, two conflicting economist forecasts, a hedging quotation that was valid for only ten days, and a growing suspicion that the cheapest-looking bond in the pile was not the cheapest.

Andara Foods is a packaged-food and edible-oil company headquartered in the port city of Calvera, in the Republic of Orvania, a middle-income economy of about 60 million people with a floating exchange rate, an inflation-targeting central bank and a history of sharp currency adjustments. The company was founded in 1971 by the Ventresca family, which still owns 58 percent of the shares; the remaining 42 percent trades on the Orvanian Stock Exchange. Andara sells rice, pasta, sauces and frozen products to Orvanian supermarkets and traditional retailers (55 percent of revenue), refined edible oils to households, bakeries and exporters (30 percent) and snacks and frozen foods (15 percent). In FY2026E revenue is ORS 36,000 million, roughly US$1.8 billion at today's rate of ORS 20.00 per US dollar. About 22 percent of revenue is exported, priced and invoiced in US dollars, mainly refined oil sold to neighboring countries.

### The project and the funding need

The board has approved in principle the Port Calvera complex, a crushing, refining and packaging plant that would raise oilseed processing capacity by 60 percent and create an export platform. The capital cost is ORS 4,000 million (US$200 million), spent mostly in FY2027 (ORS 3,200 million) and FY2028 (ORS 800 million). Management expects the plant to add ORS 1,100 million of EBITDA by FY2031, roughly half of it in US dollars. In addition, the company wants to refinance ORS 1,000 million (US$50 million) of floating-rate bank debt that matures within eighteen months and carries a 15.5 percent rate. Together this is a funding need of ORS 5,000 million, or US$250 million at the current exchange rate.

### The people

- **Anneliese Dragomir-Sato, CFO.** Joined from a regional bank. She will present the recommendation, and she is personally uneasy about leaving US-dollar debt unhedged: "Our costs are in dollars and our customers pay in sols."
- **Rafael Ibarrena-Quist, CEO.** A food engineer who wants the plant on schedule and the financing settled before the presidential campaign season. He is attracted by the Eurobond's lower coupon: "Nine and a quarter versus fifteen and a half. Explain the difference to me in one sentence."
- **Don Aurelio Ventresca, Chairman.** Representing the family. He remembers the devaluation of 2023, when a US-dollar import loan nearly cost the family control, and refuses to approve an instrument whose worst case he cannot describe.
- **Kofi Marchetti-Adeyemi, Treasurer.** Responsible for relationships with banks, rating agencies and swap counterparties, and for executing whatever the board approves.
- **Dr. Thandeka Voss-Okonjo, independent director, Chair of the Audit and Risk Committee.** A former central-bank economist. She wants the board paper to say, in numbers, what Andara is betting on.
- **Northbridge Securities (fictional).** Lead bookrunner for both the local and the international bond. Its banker has promised a Eurobond coupon of about 9.25 percent, a local-currency coupon of about 15.5 percent, and a swap quote that "is better than you think."

### The decision

The board must choose one financing structure and the covenant package that goes with it. The three structures on the table are:

- **Option A: local-currency bond.** An ORS 5,000 million, five-year bullet bond at a 15.50 percent coupon, sold to Orvanian pension funds, insurers and banks. (A seven-year tenor is possible at a coupon of 16.25 percent.)
- **Option B: US-dollar Eurobond, unhedged.** A US$250 million, seven-year bullet at 9.25 percent, sold to international investors, with proceeds converted into sols at the spot rate.
- **Option C: US-dollar Eurobond with a cross-currency swap.** The same Eurobond, with a swap that converts US$175 million (70 percent) of the exposure into fixed-rate sols at 17.60 percent. Counterparty lines limit the swap to US$175 million.

Students are also asked to design Option D, a combination that might beat all three, and to specify the covenants.

### The deadline

The board meets on 14 December 2026. The investor roadshow would run from 25 to 29 January 2027, and pricing is targeted for 4 February 2027, ahead of the campaign season for the October 2027 presidential election. The dealer's swap quotation expires on 26 November, and a delay beyond mid-February would push the plant's first milestone payment (ORS 1,200 million, due 15 March 2027) onto the existing bank lines.

## Industry Context

Orvania's food-processing industry has three features that shape the financing choice.

**A structural mismatch between revenue and cost currencies.** Processors sell mostly in local currency to price-sensitive consumers, while their raw materials (oilseeds, wheat, packaging resin and a share of energy and freight) are priced off global markets in US dollars. When the sol weakens, imported costs rise immediately, but retail prices adjust slowly, partly because the government has twice in the past decade threatened price caps on staples. Domestic competitors with fewer imported inputs have the opposite exposure. As a result, a processor's operating profit is typically *short* the US dollar even before any dollar borrowing.

**A shallow local bond market, a deep sovereign curve.** Orvania has a liquid sovereign curve, but its corporate bond market is young: the largest ever corporate issue is ORS 2,500 million, most investors want maturities of five years or less, and a bond larger than ORS 3,000 million must be priced wide to clear. Local investors are mostly domestic pension funds, insurers and banks who hold to maturity and demand a premium over sovereign yields for credit and illiquidity. International investors, by contrast, buy Orvanian corporate Eurobonds as a diversified emerging-market allocation; they accept lower coupons in dollars because they do not bear the local-currency risk, but they price the sovereign, transfer-and-convertibility and political risk of Orvania into the spread.

**A thin, expensive hedging market.** Cross-currency swaps into sols are available from a handful of international dealers and from the two largest local banks, but tenors beyond five years are rare, counterparty credit lines are limited, and the pricing reflects the large interest-rate gap between the sol (about 12 percent on seven-year government paper) and the dollar (about 4.2 percent). Under covered interest parity this gap is the price of the hedge: it is not a fee that a clever negotiator can remove.

**Currency history.** The sol has depreciated against the dollar in every year of the past five, from ORS 14.00 at the end of 2021 to ORS 20.00 today, an average of about 7.4 percent a year. The pace has been uneven: a six-week sell-off in the spring of 2023, triggered by a commodity-price shock and a rating downgrade, took the sol down 17 percent before it partially recovered. During that episode the central bank imposed temporary limits on transfers of foreign currency abroad, and some companies could not service dollar debt on schedule even though they held sols.

**Covenant norms.** International bond covenants for emerging-market corporates are typically "incurrence" tests (limits on new debt, liens and restricted payments that apply only when the company takes an action) rather than "maintenance" tests. Local bank loans and some local bonds, in contrast, use maintenance tests measured every six months. The lead banks have proposed a hybrid package for Andara, reproduced later in the case, whose definitions will matter as much as its levels.

## Complications and Exhibits

The following facts emerged late in the Treasurer's review. Some are firm; others are ambiguous, and students should say which.

1. **Execution risk on the local bond.** The bookrunner puts the probability that an ORS 5,000 million local bond clears at 15.50 percent at about 70 percent. In the other 30 percent the book supports only ORS 3,000 million at 14.75 percent, and the remaining ORS 2,000 million would have to be raised from banks at a floating rate that is today 16.5 percent. A record-sized deal also uses up the local market's capacity for Andara for at least a year.

2. **Withholding tax on the Eurobond.** Orvania levies a 15 percent withholding tax on interest paid directly to non-residents. The 9.25 percent coupon assumes issuance through a treaty-eligible financing vehicle in a third country, which reduces the withholding rate to zero but takes about four months to establish. If the vehicle is not ready by February, investors will require a gross-up so that they receive 9.25 percent net, and the company must pay 9.25 / 0.85 = 10.88 percent.

3. **Tax treatment of foreign-exchange results.** Interest paid is deductible at the 25 percent rate in the amount of sols actually paid, so a dollar coupon is deductible at the exchange rate of the payment date. Unrealized translation losses on dollar debt are not deductible. The realized loss on repaying principal is deductible in the year of repayment, but the tax authority has contested similar deductions in the past, and the company's tax advisers rate the deduction "probable but not certain." Swap payments are deductible as paid.

4. **Convertibility and transfer risk.** In the 2023 sell-off the central bank limited transfers abroad for fourteen weeks. A company with sols but no dollars could not service dollar debt. The dealers' swap documentation allows termination if currency is no longer freely convertible. Neither the Eurobond nor the swap eliminates this risk, and Dr. Voss-Okonjo has asked how the structure behaves if transfers are blocked for a quarter.

5. **Swap line limit.** Dealers A and B together limit the swap to US$175 million. Any additional line would require cash collateral of US$50 million, which would sit idle at about 4 percent while the company borrows at 15 percent or more.

6. **Existing bank covenants.** Andara's existing bank loans contain a maintenance covenant of net debt to EBITDA of 3.0x or less, tested at spot exchange rates, with cross-default to any other debt. The banks have not agreed to amend this covenant.

7. **Rating agency.** The B+ rating is on stable outlook. The agency has said it would consider a downgrade if gross debt to EBITDA exceeds 3.0x for two consecutive quarters, and it adds a stress to unhedged dollar debt by converting it at a rate 30 percent weaker than spot.

8. **The plant's dollar earnings arrive late.** Port Calvera's EBITDA, and its dollar exports in particular, arrive from FY2029. The first two coupons on any dollar bond (FY2027 and FY2028) are paid when Andara's net operating position is still short dollars by US$154 million and US$128 million.

9. **Election risk.** Pricing in February is ahead of the October 2027 presidential election. The central bank governor has hinted that capital controls "remain an option" if outflows accelerate. The probabilities in Exhibit 10 are the committee's judgments, not market-implied.

10. **Call features.** The local bond is callable at par from year three; the Eurobond is non-callable for three years and then callable at a make-whole premium. The option value of these features is not priced in the quoted coupons and is ambiguous.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Compare bonds in different currencies by converting each to a common-currency all-in cost (IRR after fees), and explain why headline coupons are not comparable.
2. Use covered interest parity, purchasing-power parity and history to frame the exchange-rate bet implicit in an unhedged foreign-currency bond, and compute the break-even depreciation (Volume 1, Chapters 19 and 20).
3. Analyze a company's operating currency exposure and recognize when foreign-currency debt is a wrong-way exposure.
4. Price and value a cross-currency swap, interpret the hedge as the carry given up, and manage collateral, counterparty and convertibility risk.
5. Stress-test credit metrics and covenants under devaluation, and identify which covenant binds.
6. Design covenants whose definitions survive currency moves, and write a board recommendation that states plainly what the company is betting on.

### Solution to Question 1: Funding need and the true cost of the coupons

Debt after: existing bank debt 6,000 less 1,000 repaid, plus 5,000 new, equals 10,000. Pro forma metrics on FY2026E EBITDA of 4,140: gross debt / EBITDA = 10,000 / 4,140 = **2.42x** (from 1.45x); net debt / EBITDA = (10,000 - 2,600) / 4,140 = **1.79x**. On FY2027E EBITDA of 4,677: 2.14x gross and 1.58x net. Interest cover at close (FY2026E EBITDA, today's rate):

| Option | Annual interest (ORS m) | EBITDA / interest |
|---|---|---|
| A | 775 (bank) + 775 (bond) = 1,550 | 2.67x |
| B | 775 + 23.125 x 20 = 1,237.5 | 3.35x |

Note that the cheapest-looking option, B, has the highest interest cover at close. The Eurobond's lower coupon is what makes it attractive to the CEO and to lenders applying a simple cover test.

**(b) All-in cost as IRR in sols.**

*Option A, five years.* Net proceeds are 5,000 x (1 - 0.008) = 4,960. Flows: 775 a year for years 1 to 4, and 775 + 5,000 = 5,775 in year 5. The rate r solving 4,960 = 775 x annuity(r, 5) + 5,000 / (1 + r)^5 is **15.74 percent**. *Seven years at 16.25 percent:* coupons of 812.5 and the same fee give **16.45 percent**.

*Option B at an unchanged exchange rate.* Net proceeds are US$250 million x 0.984 x 20.00 = 4,920. Coupons are US$23.125 million x 20.00 = 462.5 a year, and 5,000 of principal in year 7. The IRR is **9.57 percent**.

*Fully hedged Eurobond.* Proceeds of 4,920 less a swap fee of 0.4 percent x 5,000 = 20 give 4,900. Swap payments are 17.60 percent x 5,000 = 880 a year and 5,000 at maturity. The IRR is **18.13 percent**.

| Structure | Headline coupon | All-in cost in sols |
|---|---|---|
| A, five-year local | 15.50% | 15.74% |
| A, seven-year local | 16.25% | 16.45% |
| B, unhedged, sol unchanged | 9.25% in dollars | 9.57% |
| Eurobond fully hedged | 9.25% plus swap | 18.13% |

**(c) Why the coupons are not comparable.** A coupon is the price of borrowing a particular currency. The 9.25 percent buys dollars; the sol cost of those dollars depends on how many sols must later be exchanged for them. Converting the dollar coupon into sols requires an exchange-rate forecast, or a hedge that fixes it. The answer to the CEO is therefore one sentence: *the 9.25 percent and the 15.5 percent are prices of different currencies, and the six-point gap is the market's estimate of how much the sol will lose against the dollar; if it loses more than about 5.6 percent a year, the dollar bond costs more.*

### Solution to Question 2: Parity and expectations

**(a) Covered interest parity.** With sol and dollar zero rates of 12.0 and 4.2 percent, the t-year forward rate is F(t) = 20.00 x (1.12 / 1.042)^t. The annual ratio is 1.12 / 1.042 = 1.07486, an implied depreciation of **7.49 percent a year**.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forward rate | 21.50 | 23.11 | 24.84 | 26.70 | 28.69 | 30.84 | 33.15 |
| Cumulative depreciation | 7.5% | 15.6% | 24.2% | 33.5% | 43.5% | 54.2% | 65.7% |

**(b) Purchasing-power parity.** Depreciation equals the inflation ratio: 1.085 / 1.030 - 1 = **5.34 percent a year**; the seven-year PPP rate is 20.00 x 1.0534^7 = 28.8.

**(c) Break-even depreciation.** The unhedged Eurobond costs (1 + 0.0957)(1 + d) - 1 when the sol depreciates at d a year. Setting this equal to Option A's all-in cost:

```
Versus A, five-year:  (1 + 0.1574) / (1 + 0.0957) - 1 = 5.63%
Versus A, seven-year: (1 + 0.1645) / (1 + 0.0957) - 1 = 6.28%
```

(The fee-free approximations 1.155 / 1.0925 - 1 = 5.72 percent and 6.41 percent are close.) These figures were confirmed by solving the IRR equation numerically with the depreciation path as the unknown.

**(d) Evaluating the views.**

| View | Depreciation d | Eurobond B all-in cost (approx.) | Versus A five-year (15.74%) | Versus A seven-year (16.45%) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Calvera Merchant Bank | 4.0% | 13.95% | B cheaper | B cheaper |
| PPP | 5.34% | 15.42% | B cheaper by 0.3 | B cheaper |

| Forward market | 7.49% | 17.78% | B dearer by 2.0 | B dearer |

The unhedged bond wins only if the sol depreciates by less than about 5.6 percent a year; the inflation differential alone, 5.3 percent, leaves a margin of 0.3 points; history and the forward market both say the margin is negative. The Eurobond's apparent advantage rests on a forecast that is more optimistic than the market's own price. A forward-market view is the only view an investor can trade against, and an unhedged borrower is a net seller of the sol at the forward rate; the hedge market charges the full 7.49 percent for that.

### Solution to Question 4: Operating exposure and the natural hedge

**(a) Net position and sensitivity.** US-dollar revenue is 22 percent x 36,000 = ORS 7,920 million; US-dollar-linked costs are ORS 11,000 million. The net position is 7,920 - 11,000 = **-ORS 3,080 million, short US$154 million** at 20.00. A 20 percent devaluation raises the gross cost of the net short by 0.20 x 3,080 = ORS 616 million. Management's estimate (Exhibit 11) is that after pricing pass-through EBITDA falls by 190 x 2 = **ORS 380 million** in the year of the move (8.1 percent of FY2027E EBITDA of 4,677) and by 95 x 2 = **ORS 190 million** a year thereafter (3.6 percent of FY2028E EBITDA of 5,234). The implied pass-through is 38 percent in the first year (1 - 190 / 308) and 69 percent in steady state (1 - 95 / 308).

**(b) Wrong-way exposure.** A natural hedge exists when debt and operating cash flows move in opposite directions. Here they move together:

| Effect of a 20% devaluation | Local bond (A) | Unhedged Eurobond (B) |
|---|---|---|
| EBITDA, year of move | -380 | -380 |
| Principal liability | none | +ORS 1,000 (250 x 20 x 0.20) |
| Annual coupon in sols | none | +ORS 92.5 (23.125 x 20 x 0.20) |
| Combined net dollar short | US$154 million | US$154 + 250 = **US$404 million** |

Borrowing US$250 million unhedged makes Andara's dollar short 2.6 times larger. A dollar loan is a hedge only for a company that is long dollars. The peer table in Exhibit 8 corroborates this: Orvis Grain, with 80 percent of its debt in unhedged dollars, lost 120 percent of net income in the 2023 sell-off.

**(c) The plant changes the picture slowly.** The net dollar operating position (Exhibit 3) is -154, -154, -128, -71, -1 and +52 (US$ millions) for FY2026 to FY2031. The cumulative operating shortfall over FY2027 to FY2030 is 154 + 128 + 71 + 1 = US$354 million. The company turns long only in FY2031, and by then its net long position of US$52 million would cover the US$23.1 million coupon more than twice but only a fifth of the US$250 million principal. The first two coupons (FY2027, FY2028) are paid while the company is short US$154 million and US$128 million. A reasonable guideline is that unhedged dollar debt should not exceed the net dollar position the company expects to hold when the principal falls due: about US$50 million. A US$250 million bond with the maximum 70 percent swap leaves US$75 million unhedged, above that limit, which argues for a smaller dollar tranche or a higher hedge ratio.

### Solution to Question 5: The cross-currency swap

**(a) Checking the quote against covered interest parity.** A hedged dollar borrower pays approximately the dollar coupon compounded with the forward premium: (1 + 0.0925)(1 + 0.0749) - 1 = **17.43 percent**. The dealer's quote of 17.60 percent therefore embeds a margin of about **17 basis points** for credit, funding and liquidity. The upfront fee of 0.40 percent adds roughly another 7 basis points a year over seven years. The swap is fairly priced against parity; its cost is the parity itself.

**(b) Annual carry on US$175 million.**

```
Notional in sols: 175 x 20.00 = 3,500
Sol payment:  3,500 x 17.60% = 616.0 a year
Dollar coupon received (to pay the bond): 175 x 9.25% = 16.1875, equal to 323.75 sols at 20.00
Extra cost relative to an unhedged coupon at today's rate = 616.0 - 323.75 = 292.25 sols (US$14.6 million), or 8.35% of notional a year
```

This 8.35-point gap is the hedge cost. It compares with the forward-implied depreciation cost of 1.0925 x 7.49 percent = 8.18 points plus the dealer margin of about 0.17. The hedge costs roughly what the forward market says depreciation will cost; it is a bet on nothing, which is why hedging is rarely cheaper than bearing the risk unless the sol depreciates faster than the forward.

**(c) Mark-to-market at the end of year 2.** With five years remaining, the dollar leg is worth (at the 4.2 percent dollar rate) 16.1875 x 4.426 + 175 / 1.042^5 = 71.65 + 142.47 = US$214.12 million. The sol leg is worth, at 12.0 percent, 616 x 3.6048 + 3,500 / 1.12^5 = 2,220.6 + 1,985.9 = ORS 4,206.5 million.

| Scenario | Sol per dollar at end of year 2 | Dollar leg in sols | Sol leg | Swap value to Andara |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| S5: shock | 28.35 | 6,070.4 | 4,206.5 | +ORS 1,863.9 million (+US$65.7 million) |
| S6: appreciation | 19.21 | 4,113.3 | 4,206.5 | -ORS 93.2 million (-US$4.9 million) |

In S5 the swap gains ORS 1,864 million, more than the ORS 1,461 million (175 x 8.35) by which the hedged bond principal has grown, because it also covers the higher cost of the remaining coupons. Under the credit support annex with a US$10 million threshold, the dealers would post collateral of about US$55.7 million to Andara. That is a source of liquidity, but also a concentration of exposure to two dealers. In S6 the value to Andara is negative but within the threshold, so no collateral is posted.

**(d) Convertibility.** A swap hedges value, not transferability. If transfers abroad are blocked for a quarter, Andara may hold sols but be unable to buy dollars to pay a Eurobond coupon; the swap's termination clause on inconvertibility may allow dealers to close it out at a time when the company can least manage a settlement. The sound mitigation is structural: retain export proceeds offshore in a dollar collection account (US$396 million a year of exports, against US$23 million of coupons) and hold a debt-service reserve of at least twelve months of coupons offshore. The local bond has no such exposure, which is a point in its favor that the Audit Chair should record.

### Solution to Question 7: Covenant design

**(a) Critique of Exhibit 9.**

| Element | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Net debt at spot on the test date | Devaluation raises dollar debt in sols instantly, so the covenant measures the exchange rate, not credit quality; the same shock that cuts EBITDA also inflates debt | Translate at a constant (budget) rate, or hedged debt at the swap rate and unhedged debt at spot |
| Interest cover of 2.5x | At a 15 to 17 percent cost of sol debt, cover is structurally low: a stable Andara with 2.1x gross leverage already has cover near 3.0x. The test is breached in the stress year by every structure except the unhedged bond | Lower to 2.0x, define on cash interest including net swap payments, add an equity cure |
| Debt includes derivative mark-to-market | In an appreciation the swap value to Andara turns negative and inflates debt; the test is asymmetric and creates a perverse incentive not to hedge | Exclude mark-to-market except termination amounts due and unpaid |
| EBITDA as reported | Unrealized translation losses of the sort in FY2025 (ORS 310 million) flow through earnings | Exclude unrealized foreign-exchange gains and losses from EBITDA |
| Maintenance tests in a Eurobond | International investors usually accept incurrence tests only; a technical default on a maintenance test hands lenders leverage that does not match the risk | Keep one maintenance test (leverage) and make the rest incurrence-based |
| Cross-acceleration of US$25 million | Andara's ORS bank covenants at 3.0x at spot could trigger a cross-default through a definitional mismatch | Align definitions across all documents; amend the bank covenant to the same constant-currency basis |
| No transfer-risk protection | A convertibility event can cause a payment default with no credit deterioration | Offshore export-proceeds account and a twelve-month debt-service reserve |

**(b) Proposed package.**

| Covenant | Proposed term | Test result |
|---|---|---|
| Maintenance net debt / EBITDA | 3.5x or less at constant currency | Worst case 2.25x (B, stress Y2): EBITDA could fall a further 36 percent before breach |
| Maintenance interest cover | 2.0x or more, cash basis, two equity cures permitted | Worst stress-year cover 2.28x (fully hedged), 2.36x (C), 2.40x (D), 2.41x (A), 2.59x (B) |
| Incurrence net debt / EBITDA | 3.0x or less pro forma | Stress-year net leverage of 1.75x to 2.25x leaves ample headroom |
| Restricted payments | Dividends up to 50 percent of net income; blocked above 2.5x | Current payout is 30 percent |
| Reserves and structure | Offshore collection account; reserve of US$23 million | Addresses convertibility |
| Definitions | Exclude unrealized FX and derivative MTM | Removes artificial breaches |

Headroom at the 2.0x interest cover test, as the fraction of EBITDA that can still fall in the stress year (1 - 2.0 / cover): A 17.0 percent, B 22.8 percent, C 15.3 percent, D 16.7 percent, fully hedged 12.3 percent. A fully hedged structure with 12 percent headroom is thin, which is another reason to keep equity-cure rights.

**(c) Currency definitions.** Three options. *Spot* is volatile and confuses currency and credit. A *budget (constant) rate* is stable but lets lenders ignore real devaluation. A *hedged-rate* translation, with swapped debt at the swap rate and unhedged debt at spot, is the most faithful to economic exposure. Illustration, Option B in stress year 2: net debt / EBITDA is 2.25x at spot (28.35), 1.87x at the budget rate (22.05: debt 5,000 + 250 x 22.05 = 10,512.5, net 7,912.5, divided by 4,222), and 2.25x on a hedged basis (nothing is hedged). For Option C on a hedged basis: debt = 5,000 + 3,500 + 75 x 28.35 = 10,626, net 8,026, so 1.90x. The right choice depends on who bears the risk the covenant protects; a constant-rate test with a spot "long-stop" at a higher level (for example 4.5x) is a common compromise.

### Solution to Question 10: Recommendation

**Recommended structure.** (1) Issue **ORS 5,000 million in the local market in two tranches**: ORS 3,000 million for five years at 14.75 percent and ORS 2,000 million for seven years at 16.25 percent, all-in cost **15.65 percent**, with no dollar debt. (2) Prepare, but do not execute, a dollar financing: complete the treaty-eligible financing vehicle and establish hedging lines so that a hedged Eurobond can be issued from FY2029, when the net dollar operating position approaches neutral (-US$1 million in FY2030, +US$52 million in FY2031), sized to the net long position plus a hedged remainder. (3) Adopt the covenant package of Question 7 for the local bond and the bank amendments. (4) **Fallback:** if the local book is below ORS 3,500 million, switch to Option D.

**Why.** Every comparison favors the local bond: it is the cheapest in expected cost, ties with the unhedged bond on expected cost while carrying no dispersion, has the best stress metrics apart from the unhedged bond's cash cover, carries no convertibility or withholding risk, and does not add to an operating dollar short. The Eurobond's headline coupon is lower, but the all-in cost depends on a depreciation view that history and the forward market contradict. Its advantage is tenor and diversification, which D delivers at a premium of about 1.1 points.

Each 100 basis points on either rate moves the break-even by about 0.9 points. The hedged cost moves one for one with the swap rate: 16.6, 17.6 and 18.6 percent give all-in costs of 17.11, 18.13 and 19.14 percent. *Probabilities:* shifting 10 points of probability from S6 to S5 raises the expected cost of the unhedged bond from 15.75 to 17.02 percent; a more benign set of weights (20, 30, 20, 10, 5, 15) lowers it to 14.31 percent. The recommendation therefore holds for a wide range of probabilities, but the unhedged bond wins in a benign set; this is what the committee is betting on.

### Common student errors

1. Comparing 9.25 and 15.50 percent as if they were in one currency.
2. Computing the hedge cost as the swap fee, rather than the interest differential.
3. Treating the forward rate as a forecast with a premium rather than the price of the hedge.
4. Forgetting that dollar debt adds to an operating short.
5. Testing covenants in the base case only, or only on leverage and never on interest cover.
6. Assuming hedging makes covenants safer in every respect.
7. Ignoring withholding tax, deductibility and issuance costs.
8. Ignoring convertibility, and assuming a swap protects against a transfer block.

### Alternative defensible answers

*Option C (70 percent hedged Eurobond)* is defensible if the board values investor diversification and the company wants to build an international franchise before the dollar earnings arrive; it costs 1.75 points more than A2. *Option D* is the natural fallback and arguably the best balance if the local market cannot absorb the size. *Option B* is defensible only with a documented view that depreciation will be below about 5.6 percent a year and a plan for the operating short; the evidence does not support it. *Hedging more with cash collateral* beyond the swap limit is defensible but expensive: US$50 million idle at about 4 percent while borrowing at 15 percent or more.

**To:** Board of Directors, Andara Foods S.A.
**From:** Anneliese Dragomir-Sato, Chief Financial Officer
**Date:** 8 December 2026
**Subject:** Financing of Port Calvera and the refinancing of bank debt; recommendation for decision on 14 December

**Recommendation.** Issue ORS 5,000 million of sol-denominated bonds in two tranches (ORS 3,000 million for five years at 14.75 percent and ORS 2,000 million for seven years at 16.25 percent), adopt a revised covenant package, and defer any dollar issue until the plant's dollar earnings arrive in FY2029. If the local book is short, issue Option D (ORS 2,000 million local plus a US$150 million Eurobond, 60 percent hedged).

**The CEO's question.** The 9.25 percent coupon buys dollars and the 15.50 percent buys sols; the gap is the market's estimate of the sol's decline. The dollar bond costs less only if the sol falls by less than about 5.6 percent a year. History has been 7.4 percent, inflation parity implies 5.3 percent, and the forward market prices 7.5 percent.

**The Chairman's question: the worst case.** An unhedged Eurobond in the shock scenario costs 20.1 percent, raises our sol debt by ORS 2,088 million within two years (US$250 million times a move of 8.35) while our EBITDA falls by up to 543 million. The recommended local bond has none of that exposure: its worst case is a refinancing of ORS 3,000 million in 2031 at up to 400 basis points higher, an additional ORS 120 million a year.

**The Audit Chair's question: convertibility.** The local bond is unaffected by transfer restrictions. Any dollar issue will have an offshore export account and a twelve-month reserve of US$23 million.

**Covenants.** Constant-currency net leverage of 3.5x, cash interest cover of 2.0x with an equity cure, exclusion of unrealized FX and derivative mark-to-market, and alignment of the bank covenants. Under the stress case, the tightest cover is 2.42x, a margin of 17 percent against the 2.0x test and a breach of the lenders' draft 2.5x.

**Cost.** All-in cost 15.65 percent; no dispersion. A hedged Eurobond would cost 1.1 to 1.75 points more (ORS 57 to 87 million a year).

**Decision requested.** Approve the local issue, the covenant package and a mandate to the Treasurer to prepare the dollar financing vehicle for FY2029.
