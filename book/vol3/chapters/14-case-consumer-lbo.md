# Chapter 14: Case: Oakhaven Brands, a Consumer-Brand LBO

*Type: Case. Oakhaven Brands, Ridgemont Partners, Northbridge Credit, Whitcombe & Rowe, Marlowe Foods and every other company, fund, adviser and person named here are fictional and illustrative. All figures were constructed for teaching and bear no relation to any real company, fund or transaction. Nothing here is investment advice.*

## The Situation

On Monday, 2 November 2026, Whitcombe & Rowe, the investment bank advising the family owners of Oakhaven Brands, sent final-round process letters to four bidders. Binding bids are due at 5:00 p.m. on Friday, 20 November 2026, with committed financing papers. Ridgemont Partners is one of the four. Its investment committee meets on Wednesday, 18 November 2026. Today is Wednesday, 4 November 2026, and the deal team has two weeks to settle on price, structure and a recommendation.

Oakhaven Brands makes premium pantry sauces, shelf-stable snacks and sparkling beverages, sold mainly through grocery chains, club and mass retailers, and a growing e-commerce channel. It was founded in 1971 by Arthur Pruitt, and his descendants own 78 percent of the equity; a regional investor owns the remaining 22 percent and wants liquidity. Chief executive Gwen Halloran, a twelve-year company veteran, and chief financial officer Ibrahim Nasser would roll $40 million into the new structure and receive a management incentive plan. FY2026 is on track to deliver revenue of $600 million and adjusted EBITDA of $120 million, a 20.0 percent margin. The business has $210 million of existing debt and $30 million of cash. Its three brands are described in Table 14.3.

The Ridgemont deal team is led by partner Naomi Castellanos and principal Daniel Ferreira. You are an associate on the team. Ridgemont Partners Fund VII has $5.2 billion of commitments and a concentration limit of 10 percent of commitments per investment, with any excess to be placed with co-investors. The investment committee's rules are these: a base-case gross IRR of at least 15 percent and a base-case multiple of at least 2.0x; a downside case that returns at least 1.0x of invested capital; and a "target" of 20 percent and 2.5x that the partners hope, but do not require, to reach. The committee will not underwrite an exit multiple above 11.0x EBITDA, whatever the entry price.

Whitcombe & Rowe has signaled that the sellers hope for 12.0 times adjusted EBITDA. Ms. Castellanos believes that Marlowe Foods, a listed strategic peer, is among the other bidders and that a strategic can pay more because it can capture cost synergies a sponsor cannot. Northbridge Credit, the lead arranger, has offered a committed package of a $480 million seven-year term loan B, $180 million of eight-year senior notes and a $75 million five-year revolver, which would be undrawn at closing. The terms are in Table 14.6, with the maintenance covenants in Table 14.7.

The questions for the team are the questions every sponsor asks. How should the purchase be financed, and how much equity does it need? Given a plan, how quickly does the debt come down, and how much cash is there for debt service? What do the investors earn if the plan is achieved, and where does that return come from? What if the exit multiple, or the operating plan, is worse? How close to the covenants does the structure run in a bad year? And what is the highest price at which Ridgemont can submit a bid and still meet its own rules? Ms. Castellanos wants a short memo for the committee and a complete model behind it. Assume the transaction closes on 31 December 2026, which is a simplification of the signing and closing timetable.

## Industry Context

Branded consumer food and beverage is a favorite of private equity for reasons that deserve both respect and skepticism. Brands with loyal buyers can raise prices modestly each year, shelf placement is sticky once won, capital spending is light, working capital is moderate and cash flow conversion is high. Those features support leverage, since a business that converts about 80 percent of EBITDA to pre-interest cash can carry a heavy interest load.

The same industry has recognizable risks. Several are central to the case.

**Input cost pass-through.** Sugar, tomatoes, edible oils, glass and cartons are commodity-priced. Retailers resist price increases and the lag between a cost spike and a price action can compress margin for two to four quarters. Oakhaven passed through most of the 2022 inflation but only after a 190-basis-point margin trough.

**Retailer power and private label.** The top customer accounts for 21 percent of Oakhaven's revenue, and the top five for 54 percent. Retailers use private-label alternatives to pressure branded suppliers, and a brand that loses distinction loses price. Brand investment in marketing is therefore a protective cost, not a discretionary one. Cutting it lifts EBITDA in the short term and erodes the franchise slowly.

**Growth and category trends.** Volume growth in the category is flat; growth comes from price, mix and new channels. Beverages are growing faster than pantry staples and have lower margins. E-commerce is attractive but carries higher fulfillment costs.

**Leveraged finance conditions.** Floating-rate term loans reprice with the benchmark rate. A business that carries 5.5 times leverage at an 8.25 percent term-loan rate has an interest bill that absorbs about half of EBITDA, so a rate shock, an EBITDA miss or both can bring coverage covenants into play quickly. Many tax regimes also limit the deductibility of net interest to a percentage of earnings, which raises the after-tax cost of debt for highly levered companies.

**Exit routes.** Sponsors exit through a sale to a strategic acquirer, a sale to another sponsor, or a public offering. Strategics have paid the highest multiples for scarce brands, but each route depends on conditions at the time. Exit multiples are the least controllable input in an LBO model, which is why committees insist on underwriting them conservatively.

The consolidation logic is also worth noting. Large food companies have been pruning slow-growth portfolios and buying smaller, faster-growing brands, which supports exit values. A sponsor that buys at a high multiple is, in effect, betting that this buying will continue. The assignment tests whether the numbers justify that bet at 11 times, at 12 times, or at neither.

## Complications and Exhibits

**Exhibit A: The add-backs.** Adjusted EBITDA of $120.0 million includes add-backs of $8.0 million for "restructuring and non-recurring items," up from $4.0 million in FY2024 and $6.0 million in FY2025. Management says the items are plant consolidation costs that will end in FY2027. A skeptical lender's quality-of-earnings review has so far accepted $5.0 million and challenged $3.0 million. At 11.0 times adjusted EBITDA the price is 11.8 times reported EBITDA of $112.0 million. The lenders' leverage multiples are quoted on adjusted EBITDA.

**Exhibit B: Procurement program.** About 70 percent of the base-case margin expansion, roughly $9 million of savings, depends on renegotiating tomato-paste and packaging contracts that run to 2028. Management has signed letters of intent covering about a third of the volume. The base case also assumes that Oakhaven holds marketing spending at 6.5 percent of revenue.

**Exhibit C: Customer concentration.** The largest customer, a national grocery chain, has asked for a 1.5 percent price rollback in exchange for expanded shelf space for Hearthstone. The base case assumes no rollback. Accepting it would cost roughly $5 million of EBITDA a year before any volume benefit.

**Exhibit D: The competing bid.** Whitcombe & Rowe has told Ridgemont that another bidder has indicated 12.0 times. If Marlowe Foods is the bidder, a strategic acquirer might capture cost synergies of $12 to $15 million a year (procurement and overhead), worth perhaps one to one and a half turns of EBITDA at a capitalized multiple. A sponsor's synergies are limited to the operating plan.

**Exhibit E: Interest and tax.** The term loan floats with the benchmark rate; Northbridge offers a three-year swap that fixes the benchmark at 3.90 percent for half of the term loan. In the tax regime assumed here, net interest deductions are capped at 30 percent of EBITDA; disallowed interest is carried forward but the model should ignore the carryforward. The base case in Table 14.6 assumes full deductibility; Question 9 asks for the effect of the cap.

**Exhibit F: Management terms.** Ms. Halloran has asked for a larger incentive pool, 10 percent of the equity gain, and a higher rollover. A larger MIP reduces the sponsor's return; a larger rollover reduces the sponsor's equity cheque.

**Exhibit G: Fund concentration.** Fund VII's concentration limit is 10 percent of commitments, or $520.0 million. Three limited partners have asked about co-investment on a fee-free, carry-free basis. Co-investors would be treated like the sponsor on terms and returns.

**Exhibit H: Measurement ambiguities.** Defensible choices differ on whether to charge interest on opening or average balances (the case uses opening balances); whether transaction fees are capitalized or expensed (financing fees are capitalized and amortized; advisory costs are not deductible in the model); whether the MIP applies to total equity or to sponsor equity only; whether the minimum cash of $25.0 million is a use at closing; and whether exit proceeds are measured after exit costs. The case answers each of these as stated in Table 14.6, but a student who chooses differently and says so should get credit.

## Assignment Questions

Answer all ten. State formulas and inputs. Use the Base case unless told otherwise, the terms in Table 14.6 and exit at the end of FY2031 at 11.0 times EBITDA, with sponsor returns measured after the MIP and exit costs. Round dollars to one decimal, multiples to two decimals, returns to one decimal place of a percentage.

1. **Entry valuation.** Compute the implied equity price paid to sellers at 11.0x and 12.0x, EV/revenue, EV/reported EBITDA and EV/FY2027E base EBITDA. Compare with the peers (Table 14.8) and the precedents (Table 14.9).

2. **Sources and uses.** Construct the sources and uses at 11.0x, solving for the sponsor equity cheque. Compute total, senior and net leverage, equity as a percentage of total sources, the sponsor's ownership, interest cover at closing, and the co-investment required by Exhibit G.

3. **Free cash flow.** For FY2027 to FY2031, build the base-case cash flow available for debt repayment: EBITDA less cash interest, cash taxes, capital expenditure and the investment in working capital. Show the tax calculation for FY2027.

4. **Debt schedule.** Build the year-by-year schedule for the term loan, notes and revolver with mandatory amortization and a full cash sweep. Report net leverage and interest cover each year and the percentage of the term loan repaid by FY2031.

5. **Returns.** Compute exit enterprise value, exit equity, the MIP, and the sponsor's proceeds, multiple of invested capital and IRR. Show the IRR before the MIP.

6. **Returns attribution.** Decompose the total equity gain into revenue growth, margin expansion, multiple change, debt paydown, and fee and exit-cost leakage. What share of value creation does each deliver?

7. **Exit scenarios.** Tabulate sponsor IRR and multiple for the three operating cases at exit multiples of 9.0x to 13.0x, and for the Base case by exit year (three to seven). What exit multiple does the Base case need for 15 percent and for 20 percent? At what exit multiple does the Downside case return 1.0x?

8. **Covenant headroom.** For Base and Downside, compute each year's leverage and coverage and the percentage by which EBITDA could fall before each covenant is breached. Find the uniform EBITDA shortfall to the Base case that causes the first breach, and the size of an equity cure in the Downside case with a further 10 percent EBITDA shortfall in FY2029.

9. **Financing sensitivities.** Compute the effect on Base IRR of (a) a 200-basis-point rise in the term-loan rate, (b) the 30 percent EBITDA interest cap of Exhibit E, (c) both together, and (d) total leverage of 4.5x to 6.5x at the same notes amount. Comment on what leverage does to the Downside.

10. **Price, structure and recommendation.** Find the maximum entry multiples consistent with a 15 percent, 18 percent and 20 percent base-case IRR (exit 11.0x). Evaluate the sellers' 12.0x. Recommend a bid, a walk-away price and the structure, and draft a short memo to the investment committee.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After this case students should be able to:

1. Construct a sources-and-uses table and compute the leverage, coverage and equity statistics that lenders and sponsors quote.
2. Build an operating forecast into a free-cash-flow and debt schedule with mandatory amortization and a cash sweep, using opening-balance interest to avoid circularity.
3. Compute sponsor IRR and multiple of invested capital, including the effect of a management incentive plan and exit costs.
4. Attribute equity value creation to revenue growth, margin expansion, multiple change, deleveraging and leakage, and explain why attribution is not causation.
5. Build exit-scenario and exit-timing grids, and interpret the asymmetry between upside and downside.
6. Measure covenant headroom statically and dynamically, and size an equity cure.
7. Assess the effect of interest rates, interest-deductibility caps and leverage on returns and risk, and set a bid price against a committee's hurdle rules.

### Solutions

#### Question 1: Entry valuation

**Equity price to sellers** = enterprise value less existing net debt (debt $210.0m less cash $30.0m = $180.0m).

```
At 11.0x:  EV = 11.0 x 120.0 = 1,320.0;  equity = 1,320.0 - 180.0 = 1,140.0
At 12.0x:  EV = 12.0 x 120.0 = 1,440.0;  equity = 1,440.0 - 180.0 = 1,260.0
```

| Statistic | 11.0x | 12.0x |
|---|---:|---:|
| EV / FY2026E revenue | 2.20x | 2.40x |
| EV / reported EBITDA ($112.0m) | 11.79x | 12.86x |
| EV / FY2027E base EBITDA ($132.9m) | 9.93x | 10.83x |
| Equity price / FY2026E net income ($64.0m) | 17.8x | 19.7x |

Comparison. The peer median is 11.9x (mean 12.0x), so 11.0x is 0.9 turns below the median and 12.0x is 0.1 above it. Oakhaven's growth (4.5 percent) and margin (20.0 percent) are close to the peer medians (4.5 and 20.5 percent), so on fundamentals 11.0x is not expensive. The precedent buyout median is 11.6x with a range of 9.9x to 13.1x; 11.0x is 0.6 below the median and 12.0x is 0.4 above. Two cautions apply. The peers are listed and carry 2.6x net debt, not 5.5x, so a buyer's equity must absorb more risk at the same multiple. And the add-backs mean that on reported EBITDA the price is 11.8x at the lower bid and 12.9x at the sellers' ask; the sellers' 12.0x on adjusted EBITDA is a precedent-high price on reported earnings.

#### Question 3: Free cash flow

**Tax calculation, FY2027.** Revenue = 600.0 x 1.055 = 633.0; EBITDA = 633.0 x 21.0% = 132.9. D&A = 3.5% x 633.0 = 22.2. Financing-fee amortization = 16.5 / 7 = 2.4. EBIT for tax = 132.9 - 22.2 - 2.4 = 108.4. Cash interest = 57.975 (opening balances). Pre-tax income = 108.4 - 58.0 = 50.4. Tax = 25% x 50.4 = 12.6.

#### Question 8: Covenant headroom

**Static headroom.** For the leverage covenant: EBITDA can fall until net debt / EBITDA equals the covenant, so the cushion is 1 - (net debt / covenant) / EBITDA. For the coverage covenant: the cushion is 1 - (minimum cover x cash interest) / EBITDA. Both hold net debt and interest fixed, so they are static measures that ignore the feedback of a lower EBITDA on cash flow and debt reduction.

The tightest point in the Downside is the FY2029 coverage test, because the covenant steps up from 1.75x to 2.00x just as EBITDA is still depressed from the FY2028 destocking.

**Dynamic breaches.** A uniform shortfall applied to every year of the Base EBITDA, with cash flow and debt paydown recomputed, produces the first breach (FY2027 coverage) at a shortfall of about 24 percent; at 25 percent the FY2027 cover is 1.72x versus the 1.75x minimum. Base IRR at shortfalls of 5, 10, 15 and 20 percent is 14.4, 12.5, 10.3 and 8.0 percent; the structure survives a bad year long before returns do. In the Downside case, a further uniform EBITDA shortfall of only 4 percent produces a breach in FY2029 (coverage). The thin Downside cushion of 5 to 9 percent is the true constraint on the structure.

**Equity cure.** Downside with an extra 10 percent shortfall: FY2029 EBITDA is 110.9 x 0.90 = 99.8, net debt 568.2, leverage 5.69x versus the 5.25x covenant, and coverage 1.84x versus 2.00x. Coverage requires EBITDA of 2.00 x 54.2 = 108.5, short by $8.7m; if the cure counts as deemed EBITDA, the combined cure is the larger of the two tests, and sponsors negotiate deemed-EBITDA cure rights in advance. Students should note that the cure is new money in a failing case, which reduces the multiple on a larger invested amount.

### Sensitivity discussion

The recommendation depends mainly on four inputs. (1) The exit multiple: 0.5 turns moves Base IRR about 1.2 points. (2) Margin: the procurement program contributes 29 percent of equity gain; a failure to reach it (margin held at 20 percent) is roughly the Downside's effect on value and the committee should require evidence for it. (3) Interest rates: 200 basis points costs 0.5 points of IRR and 0.3x of cover. (4) Price: each 0.5 turn costs about 1.6 to 2.0 points of IRR when the exit is fixed at 11.0x. Ask students to rank the four by the cost of reducing the uncertainty: the cheapest is the hedge, then the procurement diligence, then price discipline; the exit multiple is not controllable.

### Common student errors

- Forgetting that existing net debt is deducted to get the equity price while existing debt is repaid as a use.
- Treating the fees as outside the sources and uses, or capitalizing advisory fees and amortizing them.
- Computing interest on the average balance in one year and the opening balance in another, producing inconsistent schedules.
- Sweeping cash above the minimum balance but leaving the revolver undefined; or sweeping before the mandatory amortization.
- Applying the MIP to sponsor equity only, or computing it on exit equity rather than on the gain.
- Computing IRR with the wrong number of periods (the close to the end of FY2031 is five years).
- Reading the attribution table as causal, or double counting fees.
- Calculating covenant headroom on total debt instead of net debt, or measuring it in turns rather than percentage of EBITDA.
- Ignoring that a covenant breach is a function of both EBITDA and the debt paydown that EBITDA funds.
- Treating the 12.0x ask as acceptable because "the strategic will pay it."

### Alternative defensible answers

Three answers can be defended with the same model. **Bid at or just below 11.0x and walk at 11.3x**: the recommended answer, which follows the rules as written. **Stretch to 11.5x to 12.0x**: defensible only if the committee accepts a lower floor in return for the Upside's potential, or if the team believes the exit can be to a strategic at a higher multiple; the student should state that the exit assumption breaches the committee's rule and quantify the cost (12.9 percent at 12.0x). **Lower leverage and a lower price**: a 5.0x structure at 10.5x gives 17.5 percent and 2.24x in the Base case, 1.24x in the Downside, and first-year net leverage of 4.0x, with more covenant room, and may suit a committee that weighs the downside more heavily. Credit the logic from the numbers to the choice and the explicit statement of what would change it.

### Extension exercises with solutions

**Exercise 1: Interest on average balances.** Recompute the Base case charging the term-loan interest on the average of opening and closing balances, which creates a circularity (interest depends on the sweep, which depends on interest). *Solution.* Iterate each year until the closing balance stops changing. FY2027 cash interest falls to 56.3 (from 58.0), the sweep rises, and by FY2031 net debt is 309.9 against 321.0. Exit equity is higher and the sponsor IRR is 16.5 percent with a 2.14x multiple, versus 16.3 percent and 2.13x. The opening-balance convention is conservative by about 0.2 points; it is acceptable for a first model, but lenders' models use averages or daily balances.

**Exercise 2: Management's asks (Exhibit F).** (a) Raise the MIP to 10 percent of the gain. (b) Raise the rollover to $80.0 million. *Solution.* (a) The MIP becomes 0.10 x 884.8 = 88.5; investors receive 1,606.1 - 88.5 = 1,517.6; the sponsor's 94.45 percent is 1,433.5, a multiple of 2.10x and an IRR of 16.0 percent: the extra two points of MIP cost 0.3 points of IRR. (b) With $80.0m rolled, sponsor equity is 1,411.3 - 480.0 - 180.0 - 30.0 - 80.0 = 641.3 and its ownership is 641.3 / 721.3 = 88.9 percent. Because both parties hold the same security the sponsor's multiple and IRR are unchanged at 2.13x and 16.3 percent, but the cheque falls by $40.0m and the co-investment needed falls to 641.3 - 520.0 = $121.3m. A higher rollover is worth giving because it aligns management without diluting returns, but it also concentrates management's wealth in a leveraged structure.

**Exercise 3: What the strategic can pay.** Suppose Marlowe Foods can capture $13.5 million of annual cost savings, taxed at 25 percent, capitalized at 8.5 percent. *Solution.* After-tax savings are 13.5 x 0.75 = 10.1; capitalized, 10.1 / 0.085 = $119.1m, or about 1.0 turn of the $120.0m EBITDA. If a strategic's own valuation of Oakhaven at 11.0x equals the sponsor's, it could pay up to roughly 12.0x and keep its return; that is exactly the sellers' ask, and it is why Ridgemont should not expect to win a price war. The sponsor's route to a better price is certainty, speed and management continuity, not a higher headline.
