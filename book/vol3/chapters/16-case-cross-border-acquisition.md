## The Situation

On 2 December 2026, Lukas Brandt, chief financial officer of Zephyr Components, Inc. ("Zephyr"), received an email from the head of corporate development, Priya Nandakumar, with a subject line he had learned to dread: "Tavira: seller wants an answer, and the number has moved." Zephyr is a US-based manufacturer of brake, transmission and driveline components for commercial vehicles, with revenue of $2.1 billion, EBITDA margin of 14 percent and investment-grade credit. For two years it had searched for a way into the fast-growing commercial-vehicle supply chain of the Republic of Marenta, a mid-sized emerging economy where truck and bus fleets are expanding and where Zephyr's imported products face a 17 percent tariff. Corporate development had now found a target.

Tavira Drive Components S.A. ("Tavira") is the leading local maker of drive shafts, clutches and axle components for Marenta's truck and bus assemblers. It is owned 100 percent by Orvane Group, a family-controlled conglomerate led by Teodor Kasrin, whose interests range from cement to retail banking. Tavira's revenue for 2026 is forecast at MRD 6,000 million (about $300 million) and EBITDA at MRD 960 million (a 16 percent margin). It has three plants near the port city of Alvara, 2,400 employees, long relationships with three assemblers and an aftermarket distribution network. It also has net debt of MRD 900 million.

Orvane has granted Zephyr exclusivity until **18 December 2026**. Its opening position is an enterprise value of **MRD 6,720 million, or 7.0 times 2026E EBITDA**, payable in cash, for 100 percent of Tavira. At the current exchange rate of MRD 20.00 per dollar, that is $336 million. Mr Kasrin has said he is also willing to discuss a joint venture in which Zephyr acquires 49 percent, but has not said at what price. The Zephyr board meets on **10 December** to decide what to offer, if anything.

The question is deceptively simple: what is Tavira worth to Zephyr, in dollars, and how should Zephyr be structured to hold it? Answering it requires solving several problems at once.

- **Country risk.** Marenta is rated B+ (illustrative). Its dollar sovereign bonds yield 4.6 percentage points above US Treasuries. The central bank has devalued the dinar sharply in the past. A good deal in dinars can be a poor deal in dollars.
- **Currency and inflation.** Marenta's inflation is 8.0 percent against 2.5 percent in the United States. Tavira's dinar cash flows grow nominally by 8 to 10 percent a year, but a dollar-based investor receives a currency that is steadily weakening. Forecasting in dinars and discounting at a dollar rate (or the reverse) is the most common error in cross-border valuation.
- **Repatriation.** Marenta taxes dividends at 12 percent, caps annual remittances at 60 percent of net income without special central-bank approval, and has an unpredictable queue for converting dinars to dollars. Free cash flow in Marenta is not the same as cash in Zephyr's hands.
- **Structure.** Should Zephyr buy 100 percent and take control, with the synergies that control brings, but with all of the country exposure? Or should it enter a 49 percent joint venture with a well-connected local partner, accepting less control in exchange for lower risk and an option to increase its stake?
- **Financing.** Should the acquisition debt be raised in dollars at 6.8 percent or in dinars at 14.0 percent?

The people involved: Imani Okafor-Reyes, Zephyr's chief executive, wants a clear recommendation and has told the board that Zephyr will not "buy a currency bet dressed up as a factory." Lukas Brandt owns the valuation. Priya Nandakumar wants the deal done; she has spent eighteen months on it. Tobias Mwangi, Zephyr's treasurer, will have to hedge whatever structure is chosen. Zephyr's general counsel, Ingrid Salazar, is worried about the central-bank approval process and about what happens if Marenta's government, which has nationalised assets twice in the past thirty years, takes an interest in a foreign-owned supplier to its military trucks.

Your task is to produce the valuation and structuring analysis that the CFO will present to the board on 10 December, and to recommend a price, a structure and a set of protective conditions.

## Industry Context

**The commercial-vehicle supply chain in emerging markets.** Truck, bus and trailer fleets in emerging markets grow roughly in line with GDP and infrastructure spending, with a multiplier for fleet renewal as emission and safety standards tighten. Original equipment makers prefer local suppliers for heavy drivetrain components because of weight, logistics and tariff considerations; imported components face tariffs and long lead times. Local content rules, where they exist, create a protected position for established local suppliers such as Tavira.

**Margin structure.** Local suppliers earn mid-teen EBITDA margins, helped by lower labour costs but hurt by imported steel and bearings priced in dollars. Tavira imports about 30 percent of its input costs, which makes its margin sensitive to the exchange rate: when the dinar weakens, imported inputs cost more in dinars, and price increases to assemblers lag by two to three quarters.

**Valuation conventions.** Emerging-market industrial companies trade at lower multiples than their developed-market peers, a reflection of country risk, liquidity and governance discounts. Control transactions typically carry a premium of 20 to 30 percent to trading multiples. Local sellers often anchor on developed-market multiples or on "replacement cost", and buyers have to explain why a 5.5 times local multiple is not 9 times.

**Cross-border deal practice.** Buyers typically use some combination of: dollar discount rates with country risk premiums; scenario analysis for devaluation, capital controls and expropriation; political risk insurance; staged investment through joint ventures with call and put options; and royalty, management-fee and shareholder-loan structures to move cash out more efficiently than dividends alone. Each has costs and limits, all of which appear in this case.

**Marenta in brief.** Table 1 summarises the economy. Marenta has had three significant devaluations in the past twenty years and a currency that lost 38 percent against the dollar between 2019 and 2024. Its central bank holds foreign reserves covering 3.4 months of imports. The government is generally pro-investment but has used capital controls (a freeze on dividend remittances for 18 months) and renegotiated contracts with foreign investors in other sectors.

## Complications and Exhibits

**Exhibit A: Related-party sales.** About 14 percent of Tavira's revenue comes from Alvara Coach, a bus assembler owned by Orvane Group, on 120-day payment terms and at prices that Zephyr's due diligence team believes are about 4 percent above what an arm's-length customer would pay. If Orvane remains an owner (in the joint venture) the arrangement is likely to continue; if Zephyr buys 100 percent, Alvara Coach will be free to renegotiate, and Orvane has hinted it could shift volume to a competitor. The forecast in Table 4 assumes the current terms continue. Reducing the margin on those sales to arm's-length levels would cut group EBITDA margin by about 0.6 percentage points.

**Exhibit B: The military truck contract.** Nine percent of revenue comes from a government contract for axle assemblies used in military trucks. The contract contains a clause allowing the ministry to terminate or re-tender if a foreign entity acquires "control" of a supplier. In the joint-venture structure, Orvane retains management control and the clause is arguably not triggered; in the 100 percent structure, it almost certainly is. The ministry has not indicated how it would exercise its right. This single contract also accounts for the general counsel's worry about nationalisation.

**Exhibit C: Central-bank registration.** Marenta's central bank registers foreign investments, and registered investments are guaranteed conversion of sale proceeds (Table 7). Registration takes about 90 days and requires evidence that the purchase price was converted through the official market. Dividend remittances above the 60 percent cap require discretionary approval, and in 2023 a different ministry froze remittances for 18 months.

**Exhibit D: Competing exchange-rate views.** Zephyr's economics team assumes purchasing-power parity: the dinar loses value at the inflation differential, which is 5.4 percent in 2027, falling to 2.4 percent a year. A broker's research note argues the dinar is overvalued, that reserves are falling, and that a 15 percent devaluation is likely in 2027. The forward rate implies a 7.4 percent annual depreciation, which is more than purchasing-power parity but less than the broker's view. The forward rate is a hedge price; it includes the interest-rate differential, which includes a risk premium.

**Exhibit E: Political risk insurance quote.** A multilateral-backed insurer has quoted cover against expropriation, political violence and currency inconvertibility on up to $300 million of investment for seven years, covering 90 percent of insured losses, for an annual premium of 1.0 percent of the insured amount. Currency devaluation is explicitly excluded, and capital controls are covered only if the central bank refuses conversion of registered dividends for more than 180 days.

**Exhibit F: Tavira's management and employees.** The chief executive of Tavira is Mr Kasrin's nephew and will leave if Zephyr buys 100 percent. Two plant managers are the real source of the customer relationships. The collective agreement covers 1,300 workers and requires a 24-month job guarantee on a change of control.

**Exhibit G: Treaty status.** A tax treaty between the United States and Marenta that would reduce the dividend withholding tax to 5 percent has been signed but not ratified. The valuation should use 12 percent, with 5 percent shown as an upside.

**Exhibit H: Seller's financing offer.** Orvane has offered, as an alternative to a cash price, to accept $200 million of the price in cash at closing and the remainder as a dollar-denominated vendor note, 5-year, at 6.0 percent, secured by Tavira's shares, which would reduce Zephyr's upfront cash but not the enterprise price. The case does not model this offer; students may comment on it.

**Ambiguities students must handle.**

- Whether the country risk premium should apply to the whole firm, to the equity only, or to the share of cash flows exposed to Marenta (the lambda debate).
- Whether the terminal value should use dinar inflation of 5 percent forever, even though the long-run exchange rate may depreciate by 2.4 percent a year.
- Whether to treat repatriation leakage as a cash-flow adjustment or a discount-rate adjustment.
- Whether synergies deserve the same country risk premium as Tavira's standalone cash flows.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning Objectives

After this case, students should be able to:

1. Estimate a country risk premium by several methods and build a hard-currency WACC for an emerging-market target.
2. Forecast exchange rates from inflation differentials (purchasing-power parity) and distinguish them from forward rates (covered interest parity).
3. Value a foreign business in local currency and in hard currency, and show the two are equivalent when inflation, exchange rates and discount rates are consistent.
4. Recognise and quantify common cross-border valuation errors.
5. Quantify repatriation leakage from withholding tax, conversion delay and remittance caps, and evaluate structures that reduce it.
6. Treat country risk as an expected loss, reconcile that with the premium approach, and evaluate political risk insurance.
7. Compare an acquisition with a joint venture including a call option, and compute break-even prices.
8. Choose the currency of acquisition financing and write a decision-ready recommendation.

#### Question 1: Country risk premium and WACC

*Step 1: premiums.*

```
(a) Spread only:                     4.60%
(b) Spread x vol ratio:              4.60% x 1.4 = 6.44%
(c) Lambda-weighted (0.7 x (b)):     0.7 x 6.44% = 4.51%
(d) Rating-based:                    5.20%
```

*Step 2: cost of equity, cost of debt and WACC under (b).* Cost of equity = 4.3% + 1.10 x 5.0% + 6.44% = 4.3 + 5.5 + 6.44 = **16.24%**. After-tax cost of debt = 10.5% x (1 - 0.25) = **7.875%**. With D/V of 30 percent:

```
WACC = 0.70 x 16.24% + 0.30 x 7.875% = 11.368% + 2.3625% = 13.73%
```

*Step 3: all methods.*

| Method | Premium | Cost of equity | WACC | Enterprise value ($m) | EV / 2026E EBITDA |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| (a) Spread only | 4.60% | 14.40% | 12.44% | 296.9 | 6.19x |
| (b) Spread x volatility ratio | 6.44% | 16.24% | 13.73% | 253.8 | 5.29x |
| (c) Lambda 0.7 | 4.51% | 14.31% | 12.38% | 299.4 | 6.24x |

*Interpretation.* The country premium is the single most powerful assumption in the case: removing it more than doubles the value. The four methods give $253.8 million to $299.4 million, or 5.3 to 6.2 times EBITDA. All are below the seller's ask of $336 million (7.0 times). Method (b) is the most conservative; (c) is justified only if part of Tavira's exposure is not Marenta-specific, which is weak for a business that earns all its revenue locally (and the debt rate already includes the sovereign spread). The base case in the rest of the chapter is (b) at 13.73 percent, with 12.4 percent as the bottom of a defensible range.

A buyer should model exchange rates consistently with the inflation in the forecast and treat the difference as the cost of hedging (or of the risk premium Zephyr is being paid to bear).

#### Question 7: Country risk as expected loss

*Step 1: annual stress probability.* p = spread / loss given default = 4.6% / 60% = **7.67 percent** a year.

*Step 2: expected loss given an event (Table 8).* L = 0.60 x 35% + 0.25 x 50% + 0.15 x 100% = 21.0 + 12.5 + 15.0 = **48.5 percent**. Expected annual loss of value = p x L = 7.67% x 48.5% = **3.72 percent**.

*Step 3: equivalent discount rate.* Start with the home-market WACC of 8.32 percent plus a systematic premium of 1.05 percent for bearing country risk that cannot be diversified away (a 1.5 percent addition to the cost of equity at 70 percent weight): 9.37 percent. Then the effective rate is (1.0937) / (1 - 0.0372) - 1 = **13.60 percent**. The enterprise value at 13.60 percent is **$257.7 million**, against $253.8 million from the country-risk-premium method, a difference of 1.5 percent. The two approaches reconcile: the 6.44 percent premium corresponds to an expected loss of about 3.8 percent a year (1 - 1.0937 / 1.1373) plus compensation for risk.

*Step 4: what the expected value hides.* The distribution of outcomes is wide. Conditional on a first stress event in 2029 (and no further events), the enterprise value under each event, computed at the rate of 9.37 percent (the value if nothing happens is $493.2 million, of which 2027 and 2028 flows contribute $34.5 million before the event and $458.7 million is at risk):

| Outcome | Value ($ million) | Calculation |
|---|---:|---|
| No stress event | 493.2 | No loss at 9.37% |
| Currency crisis in 2029 | 332.7 | 34.5 + (1 - 0.35) x 458.7 |
| Capital controls in 2029 | 263.8 | 34.5 + (1 - 0.50) x 458.7 |
| Expropriation in 2029 | 34.5 | 34.5 + 0 |

The probability of no stress event in seven years is (1 - 0.0767)^7 = 57.2 percent. A buyer paying $336 million is therefore betting that the country behaves (493.2) or only a currency crisis happens (332.7); in the other states it loses most of its investment.

*Step 5: joint venture and insurance.* In the joint venture, the loss given an event is 0.60 x 35% + 0.25 x 30% + 0.15 x 50% = 36.0 percent, so p x L = 2.76 percent and the effective rate is 1.0937 / 0.9724 - 1 = **12.48 percent**, which gives a whole-company enterprise value of **$295.6 million**, 14.7 percent above $257.7 million. Political risk insurance covering 90 percent of expropriation losses lowers L to 0.60 x 35% + 0.25 x 50% + 0.15 x 10% = 35.0 percent, an effective rate of 12.39 percent and an enterprise value of **$299.0 million**, a gain of $41.3 million. The premium of 1.0 percent on $300 million for seven years has a present value of 3.0 x [1 - 1.1373^-7] / 0.1373 = 3.0 x 4.324 = **$13.0 million**, so the net benefit is about $28 million. Insurance does not cover devaluation (the largest event by probability) and covers capital controls only after 180 days, so the real benefit is somewhat smaller, but at this price it is cheap protection against the tail.

### Sensitivity Discussion

*Price versus country premium.* At the lowest country premium (12.38 percent, lambda 0.7), standalone enterprise value is $299.4 million; with a 3.2 percent leakage and $28.6 million of synergies the value is $318 million against a total cost at the ask of $342.7 million (NPV -$24 million). The ask breaks even only at a WACC of about 11.85 percent, which implies a cost of equity of 13.55 percent and a country premium of about 3.75 percent, below even the 4.6 percent sovereign spread. Justifying the ask requires believing that equity in Marenta is less risky than Marenta's own government bonds.

*Exchange-rate path.* Value falls 18.6 percent if the dinar follows the forward curve, and 19.6 percent after a 35 percent devaluation. The dinar loan, a royalty route or a JV do nothing about a real devaluation of the operating business; only price and cost pass-through and insurance help.

*Joint venture parameters.* The break-even multiple moves from 6.0 to 6.6 as option volatility rises from 25 to 45 percent (the option is worth $6.8 million to $16.9 million). If the call strike were 8.0 times rather than 7.0 times, the option would be worth less; if the partner's expropriation and controls loss reductions were smaller, the JV discount rate would rise toward 13 percent and break-even would fall.

### Common Student Errors

1. **Discounting dinar cash flows at a dollar rate** (or the reverse), the 52 percent error in Question 4.
2. **Converting at the spot rate for every year** and ignoring depreciation.
3. **Using a forward rate as a forecast** and comparing it with an inflation-based cash-flow forecast.
4. **Adding a country premium and subtracting expected loss in cash flows** (double counting), or adding nothing at all.
5. **Applying the sovereign spread alone to equity**, ignoring that equity is riskier than the bond.
6. **Treating free cash flow as cash in the parent's hands**; ignoring withholding tax, the cap and the lag.
7. **Paying the control premium without pricing the synergies,** or pricing synergies at 100 percent with the standalone country premium and no integration cost.
8. **Forgetting the terminal-value growth consistency check:** dinar growth of 7.1 percent equals dollar growth of 4.55 percent only with depreciation of 2.4 percent.
9. **Reading the option value from a formula without noting the linked strike**; or ignoring the option altogether.
10. **Choosing the loan currency by headline rate** (14.0 versus 6.8 percent) without computing the expected dollar cost.

### Alternative Defensible Answers

- **Buy 100 percent at a lower price.** A team that values the synergies and control highly (the military contract, integration, a platform for regional expansion) can defend a bid of up to $264 million for 100 percent, which is 5.5 times, with a seller note and insurance. It must explain why Orvane would accept a price that is 21 percent below its ask.
- **Walk away.** A risk-averse board could decide that no price compensates for the exposure; this is defensible if Zephyr's hurdle rate for Marenta is higher than ours and if alternative uses of $150 to $260 million in safer markets exist.
- **A different country premium.** Using the rating-based premium (5.2 percent, WACC 12.86 percent) gives $281 million and a higher ceiling; using lambda 0.7 gives $299 million. Both are defensible provided the team states why.
- **Cash-flow haircuts instead of a premium.** A team may value the base case at the home-market WACC and apply the scenario weights; it must reach about the same value (Question 7) and must avoid double counting.
- **Dinar-only valuation.** A team can complete the entire analysis in dinars if it uses a dinar discount rate consistent with the dollar rate, as in Question 3; it must still translate the result into dollars to compare with the price.
