# Chapter 11: Case: Nimbus Robotics, a Series B Term Sheet Negotiation

*All companies, people, funds, and figures in this case are fictional and illustrative. Any resemblance to real organizations is coincidental. The numbers are constructed for teaching and are not forecasts or statistics about any real market.*

## The Situation

On the morning of Tuesday, November 3, 2026, Priya Raman closed her laptop in the glass-walled conference room on the second floor of Nimbus Robotics' converted warehouse in Pittsburgh and looked at the two documents she had printed. One was a term sheet from Aldergate Ventures. The other was a term sheet from Brightwater Partners. Each offered to lead a $40 million Series B financing. Each would end the company's financing uncertainty for the next two years. And each would reshape who owned the company and who got paid first when it was eventually sold.

Priya is the cofounder and chief executive officer of Nimbus Robotics, Inc. She holds the chair of a four-person board. Her cofounder, Tomás Vidal, is the chief technology officer. The two founded the company in 2021, after Priya spent seven years running operations at a regional third-party logistics provider and Tomás spent six years in a university lab building grasping and manipulation systems. Their thesis was simple and, at the time, unfashionable: that warehouse picking robots would only reach mid-sized distributors if the customer did not have to buy them. Nimbus therefore sells its pick-and-place robot cells in two ways. A customer can buy the hardware outright, or, more commonly now, the customer can sign a three-to-five-year Robots-as-a-Service (RaaS) agreement and pay a monthly fee per deployed cell that bundles hardware, software, remote monitoring, and field service.

That choice of model explains the company's financing history and its current predicament. RaaS revenue is attractive: it is recurring, it has high lifetime value, and it makes customers sticky. But RaaS also means that Nimbus pays for the robot up front, builds it, ships it, installs it, and then collects the cost back over years. The faster the company grows, the more cash it consumes. By the end of September 2026, Nimbus had 41 customers, about 620 robot cells deployed, trailing twelve-month revenue of roughly $22 million, and $8.4 million in cash. The monthly net burn, including debt amortization, was running near $1.2 million and rising. Management's own model, which the board had reviewed in October, showed cash falling below the $3.0 million minimum-cash covenant in the company's venture debt facility in February 2027.

Nimbus was therefore not negotiating from a position of strength. It was, however, negotiating from a position of momentum. Revenue had grown 109 percent in fiscal 2025 and was on track to grow about 87 percent in fiscal 2026. Net revenue retention among RaaS customers was 128 percent. Two large customers had signed expansion orders in the third quarter. In September, Priya had allowed an introductory conversation with a growth-stage investor she had met at a logistics conference, and by mid-October two funds were competing.

### The people

**Priya Raman**, 39, CEO, cofounder. Holds 3,200,000 common shares. Fully vested.

**Tomás Vidal**, 41, CTO, cofounder. Holds 2,600,000 common shares. Fully vested.

**Dana Whitfield**, partner at Crestline Ventures, which led the $13 million Series A in 2024. Holds a board seat. Crestline would like to put money into the Series B to maintain its position (its pro rata right) but has told Priya that it will not lead.

**Marcus Oyelaran**, managing director at Lantern Seed Fund, lead of the $3 million seed round in 2022. Lantern has a board observer, not a seat, and is the smallest and most price-sensitive holder.

**Erik Lindqvist**, independent director, a former operating executive in industrial automation. Priya trusts his judgment on commercial questions and has asked him to review the legal terms with the company's counsel.

**Nadia Brandt**, Nimbus' vice president of finance, who built the model that underlies this case.

**Aldergate Ventures** ("Aldergate"), a growth-equity investor with a $900 million fund. Its partner, Julian Sato, has been warm, quick, and focused on the plan. Aldergate invests in industrial and logistics technology and usually leads with a clean, standard term sheet.

**Brightwater Partners** ("Brightwater"), a multi-stage fund with a related logistics operating affiliate. Its partner, Rosalind Teague, has offered a much higher headline valuation. She has also mentioned, in passing, that Brightwater's affiliate operates warehouses where Nimbus robots could be deployed, which could mean meaningful new customer volume.

### The decision

The board will meet on Monday, November 9, to choose which investor to pursue. Brightwater's term sheet expires at 5:00 p.m. on Wednesday, November 11. Aldergate's expires on Friday, November 13. Both ask for a 45-day exclusivity period, a "no-shop" clause that would stop Nimbus from soliciting other investors once signed. Priya has told both firms that she will sign at most one. She wants to close by the middle of December 2026 so that the company has cash well before the February covenant date.

Priya has to decide four things. First, which term sheet, if either, to accept. Second, whether to counter, and if so what to ask for and what to give up. Third, how big the option pool should be. And fourth, how to explain the decision to a board containing two investors who prefer different outcomes, a seed fund worried about its price, and a cofounder, Tomás, who has asked the pointed question that opens the case discussion: "If Brightwater's number is 28 percent higher, why would we ever take the lower one?"

The case rests on one idea that students of Volume 1, Chapter 13 (raising equity) and Volume 1, Chapter 15 (capital structure) will recognize in a new setting: the headline valuation in a venture term sheet is only one input, and often not the most important one, into what each class of holder will actually receive. The price per share tells you how many shares the new investor gets. The liquidation preference, the participation right, the option pool, and the anti-dilution protection tell you what those shares are worth in each possible future.

## Industry Context

### Warehouse robotics and the shift to RaaS

Distribution centers face rising labor costs, high turnover, and customer expectations of faster fulfillment. For a decade, automation vendors served the largest operators with fixed, engineered systems such as conveyors, sortation lines, and shuttle storage, costing tens of millions of dollars per facility and taking a year or more to install. Mid-sized distributors, those operating between one and ten facilities, were largely underserved: their volumes did not justify a fixed system, and their capital budgets were tight.

Flexible robots changed the economics. A mobile or arm-based robot cell can be deployed in days, redeployed when the layout changes, and scaled one unit at a time. Improvements in machine vision and grasp-planning software made it practical to handle the long tail of items that previously required human hands. Vendors responded with subscription pricing that converts a capital purchase into an operating expense. For the customer, RaaS aligns payment with benefit and removes technology risk. For the vendor, it creates a stream of contracted revenue but moves the capital burden onto the vendor's balance sheet.

This is why robotics companies as a group raise large amounts of capital relative to their revenue and why financing structure matters so much. A robot cell that costs $48,000 to build and install and that earns $2,300 per month gross of service costs pays back in roughly two years. The company that funds that deployment with equity at a high price gives up less of the business than one that funds it at a low price; the company that funds it with debt on the robots themselves preserves ownership but takes on fixed obligations.

### How exits are priced

Exits for companies like Nimbus have come in three forms: acquisition by a larger automation or logistics-technology vendor, acquisition by a financial sponsor once the company is profitable, or an initial public offering for the few that reach scale. Acquirers typically price on a multiple of trailing or forward revenue, adjusted for growth, gross margin, and the share of recurring revenue. The comparable-company and precedent-transaction tables in the next section are fictional and are used here only to anchor the exit scenarios that the board has asked the finance team to model. Students should not treat them as market data.

### Additional facts and open issues

**Crestline's position.** Under the Series A documents, issuing a new senior security requires Crestline's consent. Dana Whitfield has told Priya that Crestline will support whichever offer the board prefers but has noted that Brightwater's 1.5x participating preference reduces the proceeds of Series A in modest exits. Crestline also wants to exercise its pro rata right, which on a fully diluted basis is 22.41 percent of any new round; if it does, Aldergate or Brightwater will lead for a smaller dollar amount unless the round is upsized.

**Lantern's position.** Marcus Oyelaran has noted, politely, that Lantern's seed investment at $2.50 per share looks very good at either price, and that Lantern's main concern is that the company survive. Lantern would prefer the simpler terms.

**Brightwater's secondary suggestion.** In a phone call, Rosalind Teague suggested that Brightwater might buy up to $4.0 million of founder common stock at the Series B price alongside the primary investment. This is not in the term sheet and has not been modeled. Priya and Tomás would each have to decide whether to sell, and a secondary at the Series B price would be a taxable event for them. Students should treat it as an unsettled side proposal.

**Brightwater's commercial introduction.** The Brightwater affiliate operates warehouses that could become Nimbus customers. Rosalind has suggested that deployments could generate $6.0 million of RaaS annualized revenue by the end of 2028, but this is non-binding, not in the term sheet, and creates a related-party relationship that the board would have to monitor.

**Diligence speed.** Brightwater has said it can close in four weeks. Aldergate has said six to seven weeks, citing a full customer-reference program. Priya's target close is mid-December, about six weeks from now. A Brightwater close by mid-December is easier to achieve than an Aldergate close.

**Ambiguity in the pool language.** Both term sheets require an "unallocated" pool equal to a stated percentage of post-money fully diluted shares. Aldergate's counsel confirmed that the 600,000 existing unallocated shares count toward the target. Brightwater's term sheet is silent. Students should use the interpretation that the existing unallocated shares count toward the target in both cases, and discuss the other interpretation in class.

**Ambiguity in option data.** The option ledger shows 1,000,000 outstanding options with an average strike price of $1.40, but individual strikes range from $0.40 to $3.10, and about 35 percent are unvested. For the exit analysis, assume that all outstanding options vest and are exercised at the average strike price if, and only if, the common per-share value exceeds $1.40, and that exercise proceeds are added to the amount available to distribute. Unallocated pool shares are cancelled at exit and receive nothing. The 60,000 warrants held by the venture lender are ignored.

**Venture debt.** Harbor Lending must consent in a sale; management assumes it will, and Exhibit 9 values are net of debt.

**The founders' personal position.** Priya and Tomás have each taken salaries well below market for five years. Priya has said that a clearly profitable outcome of at least $10 million for each of them in a modest sale would change her willingness to take risk. Tomás is more focused on keeping control of the product roadmap.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Learning objectives

After working through this case, students should be able to:

1. Translate a venture term sheet into a capitalization table, including the effect of an option pool created before the investment.
2. Distinguish headline, effective, and economic pre-money valuations and explain why they differ.
3. Build a liquidation waterfall with senior preferences, non-participating preferred with a conversion decision, and capped participating preferred.
4. Locate the crossover exit value at which two term sheets deliver equal outcomes to common holders, and interpret it.
5. Evaluate term sheets under uncertainty using probability-weighted outcomes and a simple risk-aversion adjustment, and test conclusions against alternative probability assumptions.
6. Apply broad-based weighted-average and full-ratchet anti-dilution formulas and trace their effect on founder ownership.
7. Size an option pool from a hiring plan rather than from a convention.
8. Connect financing terms to the operating plan, in particular runway, covenants, and the probability of a down round.
9. Communicate a negotiation recommendation to a board with divided interests.

### Solution to Question 1: price and capitalization

**Step 1: the unchanging base.** Exclude the existing unallocated pool, because the new pool target replaces it. The other holders are Priya 3,200,000; Tomás 2,600,000; early employees 400,000; outstanding options 1,000,000; Seed 1,200,000; and Series A 2,600,000. Their sum is 11,000,000 shares.

**Step 2: the algebra of a pre-money pool.** Let T be the post-money fully diluted share count, p the pool percentage of T, and s the Series B percentage of T. The identity is

```
T = 11,000,000 + p × T + s × T
T = 11,000,000 / (1 - p - s)
price = post-money valuation / T
```

The Series B percentage s equals new money divided by post-money valuation, because the investor's ownership must equal the fraction of post-money value it contributes.

**Aldergate.** s = 40 / 140 = 28.5714 percent and p = 10 percent. Then 1 - 0.10 - 0.285714 = 0.614286, so T = 11,000,000 / 0.614286 = 17,906,977 shares. The price is $140,000,000 / 17,906,977 = $7.8182. The Series B receives 0.285714 × 17,906,977 = 5,116,279 shares (check: $40,000,000 / $7.8182 = 5,116,279). The pool is 0.10 × 17,906,977 = 1,790,698 shares, which is a top-up of 1,790,698 - 600,000 = 1,190,698 shares.

**Brightwater.** s = 40 / 168 = 23.8095 percent and p = 14 percent. Then 1 - 0.14 - 0.238095 = 0.621905, so T = 11,000,000 / 0.621905 = 17,687,596 shares. The price is $168,000,000 / 17,687,596 = $9.4982. The Series B receives 4,211,332 shares. The pool is 0.14 × 17,687,596 = 2,476,264 shares, a top-up of 1,876,264 shares.

**Post-money capitalization:**

| Holder | Shares (Aldergate) | % | Shares (Brightwater) | % |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Priya Raman | 3,200,000 | 17.87% | 3,200,000 | 18.09% |
| Tomás Vidal | 2,600,000 | 14.52% | 2,600,000 | 14.70% |
| Early employees | 400,000 | 2.23% | 400,000 | 2.26% |
| Options outstanding | 1,000,000 | 5.58% | 1,000,000 | 5.65% |

| Seed Preferred | 1,200,000 | 6.70% | 1,200,000 | 6.78% |

| Series B Preferred | 5,116,279 | 28.57% | 4,211,332 | 23.81% |

**Check.** For Aldergate, $7.8182 × 17,906,977 = $140.0 million. For Brightwater, $9.4982 × 17,687,596 = $168.0 million. The founders' combined ownership falls from 50.00 percent to 32.39 percent under Aldergate and to 32.79 percent under Brightwater. The founders end up with almost the same ownership under both term sheets, even though Brightwater's headline valuation is 28 percent higher. That near-equality is the first surprise of the case, and it comes entirely from the larger pool: Brightwater's higher price is offset by the larger number of shares in the pool and the smaller dollar share of the company it must buy.

### Solution to Question 2: the pool shuffle and effective valuation

The effective pre-money valuation values only the shares that existed before the round, including the existing unallocated pool of 600,000 shares, at the Series B price.

```
Aldergate: 7.8182 × 11,600,000 = $90.69 million
Brightwater: 9.4982 × 11,600,000 = $110.18 million
```

The same figures follow by subtracting the value of the pool top-up from the headline:

```
Aldergate: 100.00 - 1,190,698 × 7.8182 = 100.00 - 9.31 = $90.69 million
Brightwater: 128.00 - 1,876,264 × 9.4982 = 128.00 - 17.82 = $110.18 million
```

The headline premium of Brightwater over Aldergate is 28.0 percent. The effective premium is 110.18 / 90.69 - 1 = 21.5 percent, which also equals the ratio of the per-share prices, 9.4982 / 7.8182 - 1 = 21.5 percent. About a quarter of Brightwater's headline premium is consumed by its larger pool.

**Cost to existing holders.** The pool top-up costs existing holders $9.31 million under Aldergate and $17.82 million under Brightwater, in the sense that the new investor's price is set as if those shares were already outstanding. If we allocate that cost pro rata to the 11.6 million pre-round fully diluted shares, the founders, who hold 5.8 million of them (50 percent), bear $4.65 million and $8.91 million respectively.

**Interpretation.** If the company needs the options, someone must bear the dilution; a pre-money pool puts it all on existing holders, while a post-money pool shares it with the new investor.

The step-up from the Series A price of $5.00 is 1.56x for Aldergate and 1.90x for Brightwater. Both clear the seed and Series A investors' hurdles; neither triggers anti-dilution adjustments.

### Solution to Question 3: the waterfall

**Method.** The waterfall proceeds in four steps.

1. Add the option exercise proceeds (1,000,000 × $1.40 = $1.4 million) to the exit value if the common per-share price exceeds $1.40, and treat the options as outstanding common shares. Otherwise ignore them.
2. Pay the senior preference. Under Aldergate it is $40.0 million to the Series B. Under Brightwater it is 1.5 × $40.0 million = $60.0 million.
3. Pay the Series A ($13.0 million) and Seed ($3.0 million) preferences pari passu, but only for those series that do not convert.
4. Distribute the remainder per share among common stock, options, converted preferred, and (under Brightwater) the Series B on an as-converted basis, until the Series B reaches the participation cap of $120.0 million in total.

Each non-participating series converts if and only if the common per-share value exceeds its original issue price (Seed $2.50, Series A $5.00, Aldergate Series B $7.8182) when it converts. We solve for the consistent set of conversion decisions.

**Exit of $45 million.**
Aldergate: the Series B takes its $40.0 million preference. The remaining $5.0 million goes to Series A and Seed pari passu in proportion to their preferences ($13.0 million and $3.0 million): Series A gets $5.0 × 13/16 = $4.06 million and Seed gets $0.94 million. Common receives nothing.
Brightwater: the Series B preference is $60.0 million, which exceeds the exit value, so the Series B takes all $45.0 million. Everyone else receives nothing.
Founders receive $0 under both. The difference falls on the Series A and Seed, who receive $5.0 million under Aldergate and nothing under Brightwater.

**Exit of $100 million.**
Aldergate: the Series B takes $40.0 million. Test whether the Series A and Seed convert. If both convert, the amount to distribute is $100.0 + $1.4 (option proceeds) - $40.0 = $61.4 million, shared by 6,200,000 founder and employee shares, 1,000,000 options, 2,600,000 Series A shares, and 1,200,000 Seed shares, a total of 11,000,000 shares. The per-share value is $61.4 / 11.0 = $5.5818, which exceeds both $5.00 and $2.50, so both convert (and the Series B, whose conversion price is $7.8182, does not).
Priya receives 3,200,000 × $5.5818 = $17.86 million. Tomás receives 2,600,000 × $5.5818 = $14.51 million. Early employees receive $2.23 million. Option holders receive 1,000,000 × ($5.5818 - $1.40) = $4.18 million. Series A receives $14.51 million; Seed receives $6.70 million; Series B receives $40.00 million. The total is $100.0 million.
Brightwater: the Series B takes its $60.0 million preference first. Series A and Seed do not convert, because the amount left is small: they take $13.0 million and $3.0 million in preference. The remainder is $100.0 + $1.4 - $60.0 - $16.0 = $25.4 million, shared among common (6,200,000), options (1,000,000) and the participating Series B on an as-converted basis (4,211,332), a total of 11,411,332 shares: $2.2259 per share. Check that Seed would not prefer to convert: its $2.50 original price exceeds $2.2259. Priya receives 3,200,000 × $2.2259 = $7.12 million. Tomás receives $5.79 million. Early employees receive $0.89 million. Options receive 1,000,000 × ($2.2259 - $1.40) = $0.83 million. The Series B receives $60.0 + 4,211,332 × $2.2259 = $60.0 + $9.37 = $69.37 million. The total is $100.0 million.
The Series B receives 69 percent of the proceeds with 23.8 percent of the shares.

**Exit of $300 million.**
Aldergate: all series convert, since the per-share value, $301.4 / 16,116,279 = $18.70, exceeds every conversion price. (The shares are 11,000,000 plus 5,116,279 Series B.) Priya receives $59.85 million, Tomás $48.62 million, early employees $7.48 million, options $17.30 million, Series A $48.62 million, Seed $22.44 million, and the Series B $95.68 million.
Brightwater: the Series B takes its $60.0 million preference and participates. Participation would give the Series B $60.0 million plus 4,211,332 × the per-share value. The cap limits total proceeds to $120.0 million, so the participation is limited to $60.0 million. The cap binds if the per-share value would exceed $60.0 / 4.211332 = $14.25. With the cap binding, the Series B receives $120.0 million, and the remaining $301.4 - $120.0 = $181.4 million is shared by the other 11,000,000 as-converted shares, $16.49 per share. The Series B would convert only if its as-converted value exceeded $120.0 million, which requires a per-share value of $28.50. At $300 million, as-converted value is $301.4 / 15,211,332 × 4,211,332 = $83.4 million, far below $120.0 million, so it does not convert.
Priya receives 3,200,000 × $16.49 = $52.77 million, Tomás $42.88 million, early employees $6.60 million, options $15.09 million, Series A $42.88 million (converted), Seed $19.79 million (converted). Founders: $108.47 million under Aldergate versus $95.65 million under Brightwater.

**Exit of $520 million.**
Aldergate: all convert; per-share value is $521.4 / 16,116,279 = $32.35. Priya receives $103.53 million, Tomás $84.12 million, and the Series B $165.52 million.
Brightwater: the as-converted value of the Series B is per-share value × 4,211,332. If it converts, the per-share value is $521.4 / 15,211,332 = $34.28, and the Series B receives $144.35 million, which is more than the $120.0 million cap, so it converts. Priya receives $109.69 million, Tomás $89.12 million, and the Series B $144.35 million. Founders: $187.64 million under Aldergate versus $198.81 million under Brightwater.

### Solution to Question 4: breakeven

**Where the term sheets cross.** The founders do better under Brightwater only when the Series B's Brightwater payoff is small enough to offset its lower share count. The relevant region is the one in which Brightwater's Series B is capped at $120.0 million (exits between about $275 million and $432 million) and Aldergate's Series B has converted to common.

*Aldergate region (all series converted).* The founders hold 5,800,000 of 16,116,279 shares that participate at exit (the 11,000,000 other shares plus the 5,116,279 Series B shares; the unallocated pool is cancelled). Founder proceeds are

```
F_A(E) = 5,800,000 × (E + 1.4) / 16,116,279     (E in $ millions; 1.4 is option exercise cash)
```

*Brightwater region (Series B capped at $120.0 million).* The Series B receives $120.0 million, and the 11,000,000 other participating shares split the rest:

```
F_B(E) = 5,800,000 × (E + 1.4 - 120.0) / 11,000,000
```

Set the two equal and cancel the 5,800,000:

```
(E + 1.4) / 16.116279 = (E - 118.6) / 11.0
11.0 × (E + 1.4) = 16.116279 × (E - 118.6)
11.0 E + 15.4 = 16.116279 E - 1,911.4
5.116279 E = 1,926.8
E* = $376.6 million
```

At $376.6 million the founders receive $136.04 million under either term sheet (check: 5.8 × 378.0 / 16.116279 = 136.04). Remarkably, the Series B receives exactly $120.0 million under both at that point, so the other holders receive identical amounts. Above $376.6 million, Brightwater is better for the founders. The advantage is small: $3.9 million at $400 million, $11.2 million at $520 million, and $21.4 million at $1 billion.

**Conversion thresholds.** The Aldergate Series B converts when the common per-share value reaches its $7.8182 conversion price: with all series converted, $7.8182 × 16,116,279 = $126.0 million of total distributable value, or an exit of $126.0 - $1.4 = about $124.6 million. The Brightwater Series B converts when its as-converted share exceeds $120.0 million, which requires a per-share value of $120.0 / 4,211,332 = $28.50 and a distributable value of $28.50 × 15,211,332 = $433.5 million, or an exit of about $432 million.

**Where Aldergate's advantage peaks.** The founders' advantage under Aldergate is largest in the zone of modest outcomes. It is about $19.5 million at $100 million, peaks at about $21.0 million at an exit near $124.5 million (just where Aldergate's Series B converts), and is still $19.6 million at $150 million and $12.8 million at $300 million. The mid-range exits of $80 to $250 million cost the founders $17 to $21 million under Brightwater.

**Economics of the crossover.** Brightwater's structure is a bet by the founders that the exit will exceed about $377 million. Below that, the 1.5x senior preference plus participation transfers value from common holders to the Series B. The following table shows the return multiple of the Series B under each term sheet:

| Exit value ($ millions) | Aldergate Series B multiple | Brightwater Series B multiple |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 45 | 1.00x | 1.13x |
| 120 | 1.00x | 1.90x |
| 300 | 2.39x | 3.00x |
| 520 | 4.14x | 3.61x |
| 1,000 | 7.95x | 6.93x |

Brightwater guarantees itself a return of at least 1.5x in any exit above $60 million and a return of 3.0x in any exit between $275 million and $432 million. Aldergate guarantees only its money back. At very high exits Brightwater gives up a bit more because its lower ownership (23.8 versus 28.6 percent) yields less than Aldergate's. A structure with such a pattern is, economically, a downside protection product sold by the founders for the price of a higher headline valuation. The question is whether the headline price compensates for it, which Questions 5 and 6 answer.

### Solution to Question 5: expected value and risk

**Expected combined founder proceeds ($ millions):**

| Scenario | Probability | Aldergate | Brightwater |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1. Distress ($45) | 15% | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| 2. Underperform ($120) | 25% | 42.92 | 22.26 |

| 5. Breakout ($1,000) | 8% | 360.39 | 381.83 |
| **Expected value** | | **113.38** | **108.54** |

```
Aldergate: 0.25 × 42.92 + 0.30 × 108.47 + 0.22 × 187.64 + 0.08 × 360.39

The probability-weighted exit value is $321.15 million in both cases (0.15 × 45 + 0.25 × 120 + 0.30 × 300 + 0.22 × 520 + 0.08 × 1,000); the term sheets simply divide it differently. Priya's expected proceeds are $62.56 million (Aldergate) and $59.89 million (Brightwater); Tomás's are $50.83 million and $48.66 million.

The certainty-equivalent gap ($5.2 million) is larger than the expected-value gap ($2.7 million for Priya alone), because Brightwater's structure takes value away in the low and middle scenarios, where an additional dollar is worth more to a risk-averse founder, and adds it in the high scenarios.

In plain terms, Brightwater's term sheet is only better in expectation if the founders believe the chance of reaching the plan is nearly half.

### Solution to Question 6: the counters

The value of Brightwater's structure to the founders is therefore very sensitive to the scenario: it is a $20 million gift to Brightwater at moderate exits and a $10 million benefit to the founders at high exits.

### Solution to Question 7: option pool sizing

**Pool needed.** From Exhibit 10, 24 months of hiring requires 1,450,000 options. The existing unallocated pool is 600,000, so 850,000 new options are required. Solving for the pool percentage that provides exactly 1,450,000 unallocated shares:

```
T = (11,000,000 + 1,450,000) / (1 - s)
Aldergate: T = 12,450,000 / 0.714286 = 17,430,000;  p = 1,450,000 / 17,430,000 = 8.32%
Brightwater: T = 12,450,000 / 0.761905 = 16,340,625; p = 1,450,000 / 16,340,625 = 8.87%
```

With the right-sized pool, the Series B price would be $140.0 million / 17,430,000 = $8.0321 under Aldergate and $168.0 million / 16,340,625 = $10.2811 under Brightwater, and the founders' ownership would be 33.28 percent and 35.49 percent respectively.

**Cost of oversizing.** Measured in ownership points at closing:

| Term sheet | Pool as proposed | Founders' ownership | Right-sized pool | Founders' ownership | Difference |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Aldergate | 10% | 32.39% | 8.32% | 33.28% | 0.89 points |
| Brightwater | 14% | 32.79% | 8.87% | 35.49% | 2.70 points |

Brightwater's 14 percent pool is 5.1 points larger than needed. If Brightwater's pool were 10 percent, the founders' ownership would be 34.90 percent, 2.11 points higher than at 14 percent.

**Economic cost if the pool is unused.** Unallocated pool shares are cancelled at exit, so the true cost of an oversized pool is not the lost ownership points, but the lower Series B price, which gives the Series B more shares. In the plan scenario ($520 million, all series converted), the founders would receive $189.24 million under a right-sized Aldergate pool (8.32 percent), compared with $187.64 million with the 10 percent pool, a difference of only $1.60 million. Under Brightwater with a right-sized pool, founders would receive $203.09 million at $520 million versus $198.81 million, a difference of $4.28 million. The 14 percent pool costs real money mainly because it lowers the price at which Brightwater buys, not because the pool is wasted. Tomás's instinct to treat the pool as free if unused is therefore half right: unused pool shares disappear at exit, but the price paid for them does not come back.

**Smaller pool with a later top-up.** A 6 percent pool under Aldergate's structure (pool 1,008,734 shares; price $8.3273; founders 34.50 percent) covers about 70 percent of the 24-month need, roughly 17 months of hiring, and gives founders 2.11 more points at closing. The risk is a pre-money pool top-up demanded by the Series C investor when the company's leverage may be weaker. A reasonable position is a pool sized to 12 to 18 months of hiring (about 7 to 8 percent), with the plan documented for the investor.

### Solution to Question 8: anti-dilution

The hypothetical Series C is $20.0 million at $5.50, so C = $20,000,000 / $5.50 = 3,636,364 new shares. The broad-based weighted-average formula is

```
CP2 = CP1 × (A + B) / (A + C)
A = fully diluted shares before the issue; B = shares the new money would buy at CP1; C = shares actually issued
```

**Aldergate (A = 17,906,977; CP1 = $7.8182).** B = $20,000,000 / $7.8182 = 2,558,140. CP2 = 7.8182 × (17,906,977 + 2,558,140) / (17,906,977 + 3,636,364) = 7.8182 × 20,465,117 / 21,543,341 = 7.8182 × 0.94995 = **$7.4269**. The Series B converts into $40,000,000 / 7.4269 = 5,385,835 shares, an increase of 269,556 shares.

**Brightwater (A = 17,687,596; CP1 = $9.4982).** B = 2,105,666. CP2 = 9.4982 × 19,793,262 / 21,323,960 = **$8.8164**. The Series B converts into 4,537,013 shares, an increase of 325,681 shares. Under the weighted-average formula, this is mild.

**Full ratchet (Brightwater's first 24 months).** The conversion price resets to $5.50, so the Series B converts into $40,000,000 / 5.50 = 7,272,727 shares, an increase of **3,061,395 shares** (73 percent more than the 4,211,332 original shares). For comparison, a full ratchet applied to Aldergate's structure would add 2,156,448 shares.

**Founders' ownership after the Series C (total shares equal the original total plus extra Series B shares plus 3,636,364):**

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|

Before the Series C, the founders held 32.39 percent under Aldergate and 32.79 percent under Brightwater. After a down round at $5.50, the founders are left with 26.59 percent under Aldergate and 23.78 percent under Brightwater. The full ratchet costs the founders 3.0 ownership points relative to weighted average, and it makes the Series C investor's stake smaller as well (14.9 versus 16.8 percent), which raises the price the Series C investor will demand. The protection matters more in the Brightwater structure than the price alone suggests because it is paired with a 1.5x participating preference and board seats: in the very scenario where the ratchet triggers, Brightwater would hold nearly 30 percent of the company, a 1.5x senior claim, and two of five board seats.

### Solution to Question 10: recommendation

Pursue the Aldergate structure, using Brightwater's offer as leverage, and open with Counter C1. Priorities in order of value to common holders: (1) a 1.0x non-participating preference and no ratchet; (2) price, with an effective pre-money of about $98 million or more (a $105 million pre-money with an 8 percent pool); (3) a pool sized to the hiring plan; (4) a board with common and investor seats balanced and a jointly chosen independent; (5) no right of first offer. The walk-away position is Aldergate's original term sheet, whose expected founder value ($113.4 million) exceeds Brightwater's ($108.5 million). Brightwater can win only with C2 or better: non-participating, no ratchet, and a pre-money above $115 million.

### Common student errors

1. **Comparing headline pre-money valuations** and concluding that Brightwater is 28 percent better. The effective premium is 21.5 percent before considering the structure, and negative in expectation after.
2. **Computing the pool dilution on the wrong base.** The pool percentage is a share of the post-money fully diluted count, so the algebra must be solved simultaneously (T = base / (1 - p - s)). Applying 10 percent to the pre-round shares gives an incorrect price.
3. **Forgetting to count the existing unallocated pool** toward the target, and adding a full new pool on top.
4. **Treating the Series B as always converting** in the waterfall. Conversion is a choice that depends on exit value and is determined jointly for all series.
5. **Ignoring the participation cap**, or applying the cap to the participation amount rather than total proceeds ($120.0 million in total, not $120.0 million of participation).
6. **Mishandling options**: omitting the strike or the exercise proceeds, or forgetting that the unissued pool is cancelled at exit.
7. **Averaging founder proceeds across exits** instead of weighting by probability.
8. **Applying the ratchet price to the wrong share base.**
9. **Declaring a winner without a recommendation.** The case asks for a negotiation plan, not only a ranking.

### Alternative defensible answers

*Take Brightwater, with changes.* A group that gives weight to the affiliate's customer opportunity ($6.0 million of ARR, if real, would make the plan case more likely) can defend Brightwater, but only with the 1.5x participation removed or the cap reduced, the ratchet removed, and the board rebalanced. At 47.5 percent plan-or-better probability the two term sheets are equal; a team that believes in the plan could take Brightwater's structure at a price at a materially higher price than Brightwater has offered.

*Take Aldergate as offered.* Because the deadline of February is firm and Brightwater's 4-week close is not available from Aldergate, some groups will argue for speed. The case does not support that view: Aldergate's six to seven weeks still closes in mid-December or early January, before the covenant date, if signed within the week. The risk should be addressed by asking Harbor Lending for a short covenant waiver or an extension.

*Raise less or run a three-way process.* A $30 million round cuts dilution but the plan has only $6.6 million of low-point cushion, so it would require cutting about $10 million from the plan or accepting an earlier Series C. A third bidder, such as Corvane Industrial, is difficult under no-shop terms but might be feasible with a short extension.
