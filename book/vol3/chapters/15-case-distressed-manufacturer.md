# Chapter 15: Case: Rollan Industrial, a Distressed Manufacturer

*Type: Case. Rollan Industrial Holdings, Halden Partners, Brannoch Drive Systems and every other company, lender, adviser and individual in this chapter are fictional and illustrative. All figures are invented for teaching and are in US dollars millions unless stated. Nothing here is a statement about any real business, court or statute; legal mechanics are simplified to the level a finance leader needs for decision-making and should be confirmed with counsel in practice.*

## The Situation

On the morning of Wednesday 4 November 2026, Marta Elkind, chief executive of Rollan Industrial Holdings, Inc. ("Rollan"), asked her chief financial officer to close the door of the conference room at the company's headquarters in Marlow Falls and to bring only one document: the latest thirteen-week cash flow forecast. Dev Raghunathan, the CFO, had produced it the previous evening. He slid it across the table without comment. The bottom row showed cash falling from $14.2 million to a negative number in the seventh week.

Rollan designs and manufactures industrial power-transmission products: heavy-duty gearboxes, hydraulic pumps and motors, and the precision castings that go into them. It operates three plants. The Dunmere plant assembles gearboxes; the Carrick plant makes hydraulic components; the Teague foundry produces castings for both and for outside customers. About 2,900 people work for the company, of whom roughly 1,650 are represented by a union under a collective agreement that runs through 2028. The business was bought in a leveraged buyout in 2021 by Halden Partners, a private equity sponsor, which owns 82 percent of the equity; management and a handful of co-investors hold the remaining 18 percent. Revenue peaked at $468.0 million in FY2024. For the twelve months to 30 September 2026 it was $412.0 million, and EBITDA had fallen by more than half, from $62.4 million to $29.7 million.

The capital structure that looked comfortable in 2021 is now unmanageable:

- a $75 million asset-based revolving credit facility (the "ABL") provided by Pelion Commercial Finance, with $38.0 million drawn;
- a first-lien term loan (the "Term Loan") with $178.5 million outstanding, agented by Calder Agency Bank for a syndicate of fourteen lenders;
- $120.0 million of 9.5 percent senior unsecured notes due 2028 (the "Notes");
- an underfunded defined-benefit pension plan, carried on the balance sheet at a $45.0 million deficit; and
- trade payables of $58.0 million and accrued liabilities of $31.0 million.

Total funded debt is $336.5 million, which is 11.3 times reported EBITDA and 8.9 times the "Adjusted EBITDA" of $38.0 million that management presents to lenders. Cash interest is about $32.3 million a year against EBITDA minus capital expenditure of $14.7 million. The company is not merely tight on liquidity; it cannot service its debt even if its sales stabilise today.

Three events define the next six weeks.

1. **Covenant default and forbearance.** In September Rollan breached the first-lien leverage covenant (maximum 4.25 times) and the fixed charge coverage covenant (minimum 1.10 times). The Term Loan lenders and the ABL lender signed a forbearance agreement on 16 October. It expires on **4 December 2026**, the date on which $4.5 million of quarterly Term Loan interest is due. While the default continues, the ABL lender has reserved its rights and will not fund new draws, even though the borrowing base would support roughly $24 million of availability.
2. **The Notes coupon.** A $5.7 million semi-annual coupon is due on **15 December 2026**. Missing it starts a 30-day grace period; failing to pay by 14 January 2027 is an event of default under the Notes indenture.
3. **Pension funding.** A minimum required contribution of $6.0 million is due on **15 January 2027**. Missing it triggers a statutory lien in favour of the pension plan and a report to the plan's insurer.

The board has scheduled a special meeting for **Friday 20 November**. Management has been asked to bring one recommendation. The realistic choices are:

- **Option A, a consensual out-of-court restructuring.** Exchange part of the Term Loan and all of the Notes into equity and new debt, with $30 million of new super-senior money, all without a court filing.
- **Option B, a prepackaged Chapter 11 plan.** Solicit votes before filing, file around 7 December with a debtor-in-possession (DIP) financing commitment in hand, and emerge in about three months.
- **Option C, a Chapter 11 case built around a section 363 sale.** File with a stalking-horse bid from Brannoch Drive Systems, a strategic competitor, and run an auction.
- **Liquidation**, which nobody wants but which defines the floor against which every other option is tested.

The people in the room matter. Marta Elkind became CEO in 2023 after running operations; she is respected on the shop floor and is personally committed to keeping the plants open. Dev Raghunathan joined from an investment bank in 2022 and wrote the original credit agreement covenants into the financial model. Joseph Okonkwo-Hale chairs the board and represents Halden Partners, which wants to retain at least some equity and has hinted at new money if lenders cooperate. Fenwick Alder Advisors, the company's financial adviser, has built the forecasts in this chapter. An ad hoc group of five Term Loan lenders holds 58 percent of the loan and has hired its own advisers; an ad hoc group of noteholders led by Corvane Capital holds 46 percent of the Notes. Neither group trusts management's forecast.

Your task is to take the role of Fenwick Alder's engagement team. You must establish how much time Rollan has, what the business is worth, who gets what under each path, whether a deal can be done without a court, how a DIP financing should be sized and priced, and what the board should do on 20 November.

## Industry Context

Rollan competes in the market for engineered power-transmission and fluid-power components. This is a long-cycle, capital-goods-adjacent industry. Customers are original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) in mining, agricultural machinery, material handling, wind-turbine auxiliary systems and construction equipment, plus an aftermarket channel that sells replacement gearboxes and pumps through distributors. Roughly 64 percent of Rollan's revenue is OEM and 36 percent aftermarket. The aftermarket carries gross margins about ten points higher than OEM sales and is the more stable stream.

Several features of the industry shape the restructuring analysis.

**Operating leverage is high.** Plants carry heavy fixed costs: skilled labour that is expensive to lay off, foundry furnaces that must run near capacity to be efficient, and machining centres with long depreciation lives. Between FY2024 and the twelve months to September 2026 revenue fell 12.0 percent while EBITDA fell 52.4 percent. The decremental margin (the fraction of lost revenue that fell through to EBITDA) was 58.4 percent. Cost take-out is possible, but it takes time and cash, and the company has been short of both.

**Demand is cyclical and currently weak.** Mining equipment orders softened as commodity prices fell; agricultural machinery demand has been depressed by farm incomes and high interest rates; OEM customers have been destocking. Industry analysts (illustrative) expect volumes to bottom in the first half of 2027 and recover gradually. The pace of recovery is the largest source of valuation uncertainty in this case.

**Switching costs protect the franchise.** A gearbox specified into a haul truck or a baler is qualified over twelve to twenty-four months. OEMs rarely change suppliers mid-programme. That gives a distressed supplier a degree of franchise protection, but only as long as customers believe the supplier will survive to deliver warranty parts for fifteen years. A court filing can therefore damage revenue even where it improves the balance sheet. This is the central trade-off between the out-of-court and court-supervised options.

**Supplier concentration.** The company buys specialty steel forgings from a small number of mills. One sole-source forging supplier, Harlan Forge, is owed $6.5 million and has threatened to move to cash-in-advance terms. Credit insurers that previously covered Rollan's suppliers have cut or withdrawn limits. Trade terms have therefore been shortening, which is the main driver of the rising vendor payments in the thirteen-week forecast.

**The restructuring market.** Distressed industrial credits with a viable core business tend to be restructured through debt-for-equity exchanges: lenders who hold first-lien paper end up owning the reorganised business. Strategic buyers appear opportunistically when assets are cheap. Sponsors who are deeply out of the money typically retain nothing unless they contribute new money or the lenders agree to a negotiated "gift".

With that background, the next section sets out the numbers.

## Complications and Exhibits

The following facts surfaced in the two weeks before the board meeting. Some sharpen the analysis; others are deliberately ambiguous. Students should say explicitly how each affects their answer, and when they choose to ignore one, why.

**Exhibit A: The Adjusted EBITDA add-backs.** The credit agreement allows add-backs for restructuring costs and pro forma savings, capped at 25 percent of reported EBITDA (that is $7.4 million at the current level). The inventory write-down is not obviously permitted. The lenders' advisers accept $2.9 million of restructuring costs but only half of the pro forma savings and none of the inventory write-down, arriving at $34.7 million. Management's figure of $38.0 million is therefore a negotiating position, not a fact. For covenant purposes the answer matters; for valuation, the relevant measure is the earnings the business will actually produce, which is what the forecast cases attempt to capture.

**Exhibit B: Customer concentration and change-of-control clauses.** Three OEM customers account for 31 percent of revenue. Two of them have contracts that allow termination on a "material adverse change in financial condition", and one requires a supplier to maintain a minimum net worth, which Rollan has already breached. In a free-fall filing, one of these customers has told the sales team it would begin qualifying a second source immediately. In a prepackaged filing with a short, well-explained timeline, the same customer has indicated it would "continue to monitor". A sale to Brannoch, which is a competitor of two of the three customers' other suppliers, might trigger the opposite concern. These statements are the origin of the EV haircuts in Table 10 and are, like all such haircuts, judgemental.

**Exhibit C: Pension valuation basis.** The $45.0 million deficit is measured under the accounting standard using a high-quality bond discount rate. If the plan were terminated, the insurer would compute the claim on a termination basis that is more conservative: $62.0 million. The plan is frozen to new entrants but covers 1,100 current and former employees. The union has made clear that termination would be treated as a breach of the collective agreement. Fenwick Alder's draft plan assumes the pension is retained and funded at the minimum required level of $6.0 million a year for seven years, after which the schedule is reassessed, but the lenders' advisers argue that the pension claim should be allowed in the unsecured pool at $62.0 million and be rejected as a priority.

**Exhibit D: Critical vendors and statutory priorities.** Of the $58.0 million of accounts payable, $4.0 million represents goods delivered within 20 days of a filing and would be entitled to administrative priority. A further $18.0 million is owed to vendors that management designates as critical (including Harlan Forge, owed $6.5 million). Management proposes paying critical vendors in full, partly in cash during the case ($10.0 million) and partly through reinstated terms ($8.0 million). The remaining $36.0 million of trade debt is general unsecured. Other unsecured claims, principally lease rejection and contract damages, are estimated at $14.0 million.

**Exhibit E: The sponsor's proposal.** Halden Partners has offered to contribute $15 million of new equity in an out-of-court deal if, in return, it retains 15 percent of the new equity. The Term Loan group has countered that it would give the sponsor 5 percent and no more, and only if the sponsor also supports the deal publicly. The sponsor has not said what it would do in a Chapter 11, but its fund documents restrict further investment into a company that has filed.

**Exhibit F: The Term Loan syndicate.** The five lenders in the ad hoc group hold $103.5 million (58 percent). They have said they will support a deal in which they receive control of the equity. The other nine lenders hold $75.0 million. Two of those are collateralised loan obligation vehicles holding $25.0 million (14 percent) whose documents limit them to receiving "like-kind" securities and who have already said they cannot accept equity. The credit agreement allows the "Required Lenders" (more than 50 percent in amount) to waive covenants, but any reduction of principal or release of substantially all collateral requires every affected lender. A deal in which principal is exchanged for equity therefore needs unanimity out of court.

**Exhibit G: The Brannoch bid.** Brannoch Drive Systems has offered $185 million in cash for substantially all of Rollan's assets, assuming trade payables incurred in the ordinary course after filing and the collective agreement, but not the pension liability or prepetition unsecured debt. Brannoch has asked for a break-up fee of 3 percent of the price and expense reimbursement of up to $1.5 million if it is outbid. Management believes the likelihood of an overbid is 40 percent and that an overbid would clear at about $210 million.

**Exhibit H: Environmental and tax matters.** The Teague foundry has a historic soil contamination issue. A consultant estimates remediation at $3 million to $9 million. This is included in "other long-term liabilities" at $4.0 million and is not a priority claim. Separately, the company has about $60 million of federal net operating losses, but a debt-for-equity exchange that transfers more than half of the equity may limit their use; the tax value is excluded from all valuations in this chapter.

**Exhibit I: Potential preference claims.** In the 90 days before a filing, Rollan paid $9.2 million to unsecured creditors outside the ordinary course, including a $2.1 million prepayment to a captive insurer. Counsel believes about half is recoverable. Recoveries would flow to the estate and are not included in the base waterfalls.

**Exhibit J: Management incentives.** The compensation committee has proposed a key employee retention plan costing $1.5 million and a management incentive plan of 8 percent of new equity, to be granted after emergence. Both are likely to be challenged by the unsecured creditors' committee.

**Ambiguities students must handle.**

- Whether the mid-point of the valuation range is a fair basis for negotiation when the Term Loan trades at 62, which, together with the other debt prices in Table 4, implies about $142 million of enterprise value net of cash.
- Whether the pension deficit is a financial liability or a quasi-operating one.
- Whether the sponsor's equity has option value that justifies a gift.
- Whether a prepackaged case can achieve a three-month timetable when the unsecured creditors' committee has not yet been formed.

## Assignment Questions

Answer each question in turn and show every calculation. Where you make an assumption, state it. Unless told otherwise, use the data in Tables 1 to 10 and Exhibits A to J.
