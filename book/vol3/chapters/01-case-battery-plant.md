# Chapter 1: Case: Helios Cell, a Battery Gigafactory Decision

*Illustrative case. Helios Cell Technologies, its people, its lenders, the Province of Ardmore, and every number in this chapter are fictional and were constructed for teaching. No figure should be read as an estimate of any real company, market, or government program. Units: $M means millions of US dollars; GWh means gigawatt-hours of cell capacity; because one GWh equals one million kWh, an output in GWh multiplied by a price in $/kWh gives revenue in $M directly.*

*This case draws on Volume 1, Chapter 6 (NPV and investment criteria), Chapter 7 (capital budgeting), Chapter 8 (uncertainty and real options), Chapter 11 (cost of capital) and Chapter 14 (debt financing), and on Volume 2 where it treats adjusted present value and option valuation.*

## The Situation

On the morning of 4 November 2026, Dev Raman, the chief financial officer of Helios Cell Technologies Inc., opened a folder labelled "Project Meridian" and found, at the top, a two-page term sheet from the Province of Ardmore. It was the third revision. The Province would support a new lithium-ion cell plant at Port Alder, a deep-water industrial site on its northern coast, with a capital grant, a production credit, a property-tax abatement and a low-interest development loan. The offer expired on 31 January 2027. The Helios board would meet on 15 December 2026, and Raman had to bring it a recommendation.

Helios Cell Technologies is a mid-sized cell manufacturer headquartered in Marlow Bay. It makes prismatic lithium-ion cells for two markets: stationary energy storage (containers of batteries used by electricity utilities and large commercial sites) and commercial electric vehicles such as buses, delivery vans and port equipment. Its two existing plants, Brightwater (8 GWh a year, commissioned eight years ago) and Corrin Valley (5 GWh a year, commissioned four years ago), ran at high utilisation through 2026. The company reported revenue of $3,210M and EBITDA of $545M for the financial year ending 31 December 2026 (an estimate, since the books had not closed), and its shares traded at $20.00, giving an equity market value of about $6,100M on 305 million shares.

Project Meridian would be larger than anything Helios had done. The engineering study called for a 15 GWh cell plant at Port Alder in two phases. Phase 1 was a 15 GWh plant costing $1,500M, spent $900M in the first year of construction and $600M in the second. Cells would start to come off the line in the third calendar year of the project. Phase 2 was a further 15 GWh of capacity, bringing the site to 30 GWh, at an estimated cost of $1,300M if started three years after Phase 1 and $1,248M (a 4% discount for placing both orders together) if committed with Phase 1. Helios did not need to decide Phase 2 now, but it had to decide whether to reserve the land, power connection and permits for it, and whether to build it up front.

### The people

Marguerite Okonkwo-Hale, the chief executive, was an engineer by training and a believer in scale. She had joined Helios when it had one plant and she liked to say that "cell manufacturing is a learning business: the company that makes the most cells learns the fastest, and the company that learns the fastest wins". She wanted to approve the full 30 GWh site in December.

Dev Raman, the CFO, was more cautious. He had noticed that every previous Helios plant had come in over budget and behind schedule, and that the first plant's cell prices had fallen by roughly a third in its first five years of operation. He had asked his team to build a proper discounted cash flow model before anyone spoke about strategy.

Lena Bergström, senior vice president for strategy, had negotiated the Ardmore term sheet and favoured Phase 1 now with an option on Phase 2. Tomasz Wierzba, vice president of manufacturing, owned the learning-curve assumptions and the ramp-up schedule. Priya Anand, the treasurer, was responsible for the funding plan and for the covenants in the company's bank facilities. Dr. Nasrin Kaveh, chair of the audit committee, had told Raman that she would not support any plan that put the company's leverage covenant at risk.

### The decision

The board had to answer three connected questions.

1. Should Helios commit to Phase 1 of Project Meridian on the terms offered by the Province?
2. If so, should it also build Phase 2 now, or hold Phase 2 as an option to be exercised in three years?
3. What conditions, if any, should the board attach to the commitment?

The board had a fourth, quieter question, which Raman felt he owed the directors: how much of the apparent value of the project came from subsidies, and how secure were those subsidies?

## Industry Context

### Demand

Demand for lithium-ion cells is driven by two forces that are both policy-sensitive. The first is the transition of road vehicles from combustion engines to electric drive, which has been fastest in cars and is now spreading to buses and trucks. The second is the growth of stationary storage, which electricity grids use to store solar and wind output and to relieve congestion. Both markets have been growing quickly from a small base, but forecasts differ widely. The consultants Helios commissioned (a fictional firm, Calder & Voss) gave a central view, a low view and a high view of cell demand, and the spread between low and high was larger than the central growth rate. Any model that treats future demand as known is deceptive, and the case asks students to resist that temptation.

### Price dynamics

Cell prices have fallen for a decade, driven by three things: lower raw-material prices after an early shortage, process improvements and larger plants, and the entry of new capacity, especially in Asia. Price declines are not a smooth line. They arrive in steps when a surplus of capacity coincides with a fall in metal prices, and they pause when a shortage appears. A manufacturer's margin is the gap between a falling price and a falling cost, and the survivors are those whose costs fall faster. Helios's own history shows the pattern: its Brightwater plant earned a high margin in its third year and a modest one by its eighth.

### Cost structure and the learning curve

A cell plant has three cost layers. Materials (cathode and anode active material, electrolyte, separators, foils) are about half of the selling price in the early years and are largely passed through from commodity markets, though the plant's yield (the proportion of cells that pass quality tests) determines how much material is wasted. Conversion costs (labour, energy, consumables, maintenance and the scrap cost of poor yield) fall with experience as operators learn. Fixed cash operating costs (supervision, engineering, plant overheads, insurance) do not vary with volume and are the reason utilisation matters so much.

The manufacturing literature describes conversion cost with an experience curve: each time cumulative output doubles, unit conversion cost falls by a fixed fraction. A learning rate of 85% means that cost after a doubling is 85% of the cost before. Helios's manufacturing group estimates 85% for the Meridian technology, and notes that the estimate came from its own two plants plus industry anecdote. Because the curve is driven by cumulative output across the whole company site, a second phase at Port Alder would benefit from experience gained in Phase 1, and Phase 1 would benefit from any early volume of Phase 2.

### Policy support

Governments competing for battery plants have offered four main instruments: grants toward construction cost, production credits paid per kWh of cells sold, tax abatements and subsidised loans. Their value to the company is large relative to the plant's margin, which is why the case treats them carefully. They also carry a political risk that private contracts do not: a production credit is legislated, and legislatures can change course. The Province's term sheet promises its credit for eight years of production, but the enabling statute is subject to review in 2031.

### Competition

Three competitor groups matter to Helios. Large vertically integrated Asian producers set the global price trend. Two established North American producers are expanding. A number of start-ups have announced plants but not all will be financed. Helios's advantage is a technology licence for a high-energy-density chemistry, an anchor customer commitment from a bus manufacturer (described under Complications) and a relationship with the Province. Its disadvantage is scale: at 15 GWh Meridian would still be small by global standards, and at 30 GWh it would be mid-sized.

### Capital intensity

A gigafactory is one of the most capital-intensive manufacturing assets. The Meridian Phase 1 cost per unit of annual capacity is $100M per GWh. Plants typically take about two years to build, then ramp up over three to four years, so the project spends heavily for two years and earns little for the next two or three. This shape, a long dip in cash flow followed by a long plateau, makes the net present value of a cell plant very sensitive to the discount rate, to the speed of ramp-up and to the price path in the plateau years.

## Complications and Exhibits

Real decisions arrive with untidy data. The following exhibits, assembled by Raman's staff over the last week of October, raise problems that students must decide how to handle. None of them has a single correct answer, and the Teaching Note says how each is treated in the model solution.

### Exhibit A: Costs that may or may not be relevant

The team has recorded the following items against Project Meridian. Helios has already paid a consultant $22M for site studies, geotechnical work and permit applications. The corporate finance department allocates $14M a year of head-office cost to every business unit in proportion to revenue; the Meridian share would be $14M a year once the plant is running, but the head-office headcount would not change. The land at Port Alder will be bought for $40M; the Province has said it would repurchase the land at cost at any time, which management has taken to mean that land can be recovered in Year 15 at cost. Brightwater, Helios's existing plant, serves some of the same customers, and the marketing director thinks Meridian might take $18M a year of Brightwater's contribution between Years 2 and 6, but Brightwater is full and he agrees the sales could be replaced from a waiting list.

### Exhibit B: Learning data from Brightwater

Wierzba's group supplied the observed conversion cost at Brightwater during its first four years of production. Meridian's technology is newer and its first-GWh cost is lower, but he believes the rate of learning is comparable.

Management's 85% learning rate was rounded down by Wierzba from a first look at these data. Students may want to estimate the rate themselves.

### Exhibit C: Tax counsel's two views

Helios's tax counsel gave a written opinion with two open questions. First, whether the production credit is taxable income to Helios when received (counsel thinks "probably not", with 60% confidence). Second, whether the capital grant reduces the depreciable basis of the plant (counsel thinks "yes" under general principles but notes that the enabling statute is silent). The base-case model assumes the credit is not taxable and the grant does reduce the depreciable basis, and the CFO would like to know how much this matters.

### Exhibit D: Policy risk

The Province's production credit statute comes up for legislative review in 2031, which is Year 4 of the project. The opposition party has said it would "review the cost-effectiveness" of the credit. Bergström's view is that the credit is more likely than not to survive, and she gives a 30% chance that it is repealed or sharply curtailed after the review for production from Year 5 onward. Grants and the abatement are contractual and are not subject to the review, and the development loan is a contract with the provincial development bank. Students should model repeal as a loss of the credit for all production from Year 5 onward and use the 30% probability, while recognising that no one can know it.

### Exhibit E: Anchor customer

Northgate Coachworks, a bus manufacturer, has signed a non-binding letter of intent to buy 4 GWh a year from Meridian for five years at a price no lower than $90 per kWh (flat). The letter has no penalty for either party. The sales team treats it as validation of demand. The base-case model does not include it. Students should consider its value but should not simply add it to the cash flows.

### Exhibit F: The covenant

Priya Anand reports that pro forma net debt after the Phase 1 funding plan would be above today's level, while EBITDA from the plant would be negative in Year 2. She has asked the banks for a construction holiday on the covenant but has no written commitment. Students should compute the pro forma leverage ratio under Phase 1 and under a decision to build all 30 GWh immediately.

### Exhibit G: Volatility estimates

For the real-option analysis the consultants offered 25% as a low estimate (based on the long-run volatility of battery pack prices), 35% as a point estimate (based on listed battery manufacturers' equity volatility, adjusted for leverage) and 45% as a high estimate (based on spot cell prices during the last supply shortage). Raman has chosen 35% but wants to know how the answer changes.

### Exhibit H: Capacity commitments from rivals

Calder & Voss note that three announced plants in the region would, if all built, add roughly twice the demand growth expected in the next three years. This is the main reason Phase 2 output is assumed to sell on spot contracts at a discount to Phase 1 prices. If the rivals' plants are delayed, prices might hold; if they are all built, prices may fall faster than the base 3% a year.

## Assignment Questions

Answer all ten questions. Use year-end cash flows, the data in the tables and the treatment of ambiguous items you can defend. State every assumption you add. Where a question asks for a number, give the calculation, not only the result. Use the project discount rate you compute in Question 1 (rounded to one decimal place) in all later questions unless a question says otherwise.

**Question 1: Cost of capital.** Unlever each comparable company's equity beta using its own debt/equity ratio and tax rate. Take the average as the asset beta of a cell manufacturer, relever it at the target capital structure, and estimate the project's cost of equity and its weighted average cost of capital. Explain why Helios's own beta and its current capital structure are not the right inputs, and why the unexplained 10% hurdle rate should be retired.

**Question 2: Base-case free cash flows without subsidies.** Build the Phase 1 free cash flows for Years 0 to 15 with no grant, no production credit and no abatement, including the land at Port Alder and the tax-loss carry-forward. Identify which costs in Exhibit A you include and which you exclude, and why. Compute NPV, IRR, modified IRR (reinvesting at the project rate), payback, discounted payback and profitability index. What does the answer say about the project as an unsubsidised business?

**Question 3: The learning curve.** (a) Using the five observations in Exhibit B, estimate the learning rate by regressing the log of cost on the log of cumulative output. How precise is the estimate, and how does it compare with management's 85%? (b) Show how the average conversion cost for Years 2 and 3 is derived from the experience curve for an 85% rate and a first-GWh cost of $36 per kWh. (c) Tabulate project NPV (with and without subsidies) for learning rates from 80% to 100%. (d) Explain why a manager might be tempted to overstate the learning rate, and how the board should use the sensitivity.

**Question 4: Subsidies.** (a) Value the capital grant, the production credit and the abatement individually and together, as increments to the unsubsidised NPV. Why do the individual values not sum exactly to the total? (b) Compute the NPV if the production credit is taxable and, separately, if the grant does not reduce the depreciable basis. (c) Compute the NPV if the credit is lost for production from Year 5 onward, and the expected NPV using the 30% probability of repeal. At what probability of repeal does the expected NPV become zero? (d) What is the share of the project's total value, relative to the unsubsidised result, that comes from subsidies?

**Question 5: Tax effects.** (a) Show the tax-loss schedule for the first six years of operations. (b) Compare NPV under tax depreciation over 5, 10 and 15 years and under immediate expensing, and explain why immediate expensing is worth almost nothing relative to five-year depreciation for this project. (c) How much would the NPV change if the loss carry-forward were not limited to 80% of taxable income?

**Question 6: Sensitivity and scenarios.** (a) Produce a one-variable-at-a-time sensitivity table (a tornado) for eight inputs: initial price, price decline, utilisation, learning rate, Phase 1 capex, materials cost, fixed operating cost and discount rate. (b) Compute break-even values for the initial price and for steady-state utilisation. (c) Compute the NPV in each of the three scenarios in Table 8 and the probability-weighted NPV and standard deviation. Which inputs should the board worry most about, and which can be managed?

**Question 7: The Phase 2 option.** (a) Compute the incremental cash flows of Phase 2 if it is committed at the end of Year 3, and its static NPV. (b) Value the option to build Phase 2 using the Black–Scholes formula with the inputs in Table 9, and with a three-step binomial lattice. (c) Show how the option value changes with volatility. (d) Interpret: why can an option be valuable when the static NPV is negative?

**Question 8: Phase it or build it all now?** (a) Compute the NPV of building both phases immediately (with the 4% discount on Phase 2). (b) Compare it with Phase 1 plus the Phase 2 option, and find the volatility at which the two are equal. (c) Compute pro forma leverage under both plans against the 3.75x covenant. (d) Compare the plans under four price outcomes and say what you would recommend, and under what circumstances your recommendation would change.

**Question 9: Financing and adjusted present value.** (a) Value the provincial development loan: what is the NPV of borrowing $500M at 2.5% when the market rate is 7%? Use Myers's method, discounting after-tax payments at the pre-tax market rate. (b) Compute the base-case NPV at the unlevered cost of capital, add the value of the loan, and report the adjusted present value. (c) Should the loan be counted in the investment decision? ## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Case synopsis and positioning

Helios Cell is a capital-budgeting case in which the arithmetic of discounted cash flow is straightforward and the judgement is not. A cell plant has a deep early cash dip, a long plateau, a falling price, an experience curve that rewards volume, and a package of government support worth more than the project's unsubsidised value. The interesting questions are how to value each of these features, how to keep subsidy value separate from operating value, and how to decide whether to buy flexibility by phasing. The case is best used after students have studied net present value, project cash flows and the basics of real options (Volume 1, Chapters 6 to 8), and it works well as a capstone to a capital-budgeting module. The numerical results below were produced by a single spreadsheet-style model written in Python and checked by hand on selected years.

All solution figures use year-end cash flows, the Table 3 timing, a project discount rate of 10.2% (from Question 1) and the base-case inputs. Where a result is described as "rounded", the underlying calculation used unrounded inputs, so students replicating with rounded intermediate numbers may differ by a unit or two.

### Learning objectives

After working through the case, students should be able to:

1. Estimate a project cost of capital from comparable companies by unlevering and relevering betas, and explain why the project's risk and financing, not the sponsor's, set the discount rate.
2. Build incremental after-tax free cash flows for a long-lived industrial investment, including working capital, salvage, land, and a tax-loss carry-forward, and decide which costs are relevant.
3. Model a learning (experience) curve correctly, using cumulative output, and assess how sensitive value is to the learning rate.
4. Separate and value government support instruments, treat ambiguity in their tax treatment, and price political risk into an expected NPV.
5. Run and interpret sensitivity, break-even and scenario analyses, and identify the few variables that dominate the decision.
6. Value a staged-investment option with the Black–Scholes formula and a binomial lattice, and weigh the value of flexibility against the cost of delay.
7. Use adjusted present value to value a subsidised loan, and recognise when financing side effects should and should not drive an investment decision.
8. Write a board recommendation that states a decision, the conditions attached to it and the evidence for it.

### Question 1 solution: Cost of capital

**Step 1: unlever each beta.** With zero debt beta, the asset beta is the equity beta divided by one plus (1 minus tax rate) times debt/equity.

| Company | Levered beta | 1 + (1 − t) × D/E | Unlevered beta |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Aurelia Cells | 1.69 | 1 + 0.79 × 0.45 = 1.3555 | 1.2468 |
| Kestrel Power Systems | 1.51 | 1 + 0.75 × 0.30 = 1.2250 | 1.2327 |
| Nordvik Energy Storage | 1.34 | 1 + 0.78 × 0.20 = 1.1560 | 1.1592 |
| Orrin Electrochem | 1.61 | 1 + 0.76 × 0.55 = 1.4180 | 1.1354 |
| Tamsin Materials Group | 1.09 | 1 + 0.74 × 0.25 = 1.1850 | 0.9198 |

The simple average is 1.14 and the median is 1.16. Tamsin is a materials supplier, with less operating leverage than the pure cell makers, so a reader might drop it or weight it less; doing so lifts the average to about 1.19. The solutions use the average of all five, 1.14.

**Step 2: relever.** The target debt/(debt + equity) is 30%, so debt/equity is 0.30 / 0.70 = 0.4286.

```
βL = βU × [1 + (1 − t) × D/E]
   = 1.1388 × [1 + 0.75 × 0.4286]
   = 1.1388 × 1.3214
   = 1.505
```

**Step 3: cost of equity (CAPM).**

```
ke = rf + βL × ERP = 4.0% + 1.505 × 5.5% = 12.28%
```

**Step 4: WACC.**

```
WACC = E/V × ke + D/V × kd × (1 − t)
     = 0.70 × 12.28% + 0.30 × 7.0% × 0.75
     = 8.59% + 1.58%
     = 10.17%, rounded to 10.2%
```

The equity part contributes 8.59 points and the after-tax debt part 1.57 points. For use in Question 9, the unlevered cost of capital is 4.0% + 1.1388 × 5.5% = 10.26%.

**Why not Helios's own inputs?** Helios's present structure is about 13% debt to total capital (930 / (6,100 + 930)), and its beta reflects mature plants at Brightwater and Corrin Valley with regulated-looking utility customers and contracted volumes. Meridian is a single-site greenfield plant selling into a falling-price market. The right discount rate depends on the risk of the project's cash flows, not on the name of the company that undertakes it (Volume 1, Chapter 11). The target 30% leverage is a project-appropriate structure, not Helios's current one. **The 10% hurdle** has no traceable derivation, and a 0.2-point difference is small here, but an untraced hurdle rate gives no guidance when risk differs across projects, and it hides the fact that this project is riskier than average. A more careful instructor will note that 10.2% might even be low for a greenfield cell plant, and that raising it by a point lowers NPV by about $90M (Question 6).

### Question 2 solution: Base-case cash flows without subsidies

**Relevant versus irrelevant costs.** Only incremental cash flows count.

| Item | Treatment | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |

| $14M allocated head-office cost | Excluded | Not incremental: headcount does not change |
| $18M Brightwater cannibalisation | Excluded | Brightwater is full and the sales can be replaced from the waiting list; if the waiting list were empty the item would be a real opportunity cost |
| $40M land | Included (Year 0 outflow, Year 15 recovery) | Real cash; recoverable at cost |
| Working capital at 12% of revenue | Included | Real cash tied up |
| Salvage 8% of plant cost | Included, taxed as a gain | Book value is zero after ten years |

**Year-by-year mechanics.** For each year:

```
Revenue            = output (GWh) × price ($/kWh)                    [$M]
EBITDA             = revenue − materials − conversion − fixed − SG&A
Taxable income     = EBITDA − tax depreciation (+ gain on sale in Year 15)
Tax                = 25% × (taxable income − NOL used)
NOL used           = min(NOL balance, 80% × taxable income)
FCF                = EBITDA − tax − capex − ΔNWC + salvage and land recovery
```

**Worked example, Year 6 (no subsidies).** Output is 15 × 85% = 12.75 GWh and price is 97.0 × 0.97⁴ = $85.87 per kWh, so revenue is 12.75 × 85.87 = $1,094.9M. Materials are 12.75 × $41.62 = $530.6M. The conversion cost (Question 3) is $14.75 per kWh, so $188.1M. Fixed opex is $119.1M and SG&A is 3% × 1,094.9 = $32.8M. EBITDA is 1,094.9 − 530.6 − 188.1 − 119.1 − 32.8 = $224.2M. Tax depreciation is 1,500 / 10 = $150.0M, so taxable income before losses is $74.2M. The loss balance at the end of Year 5 is $162M, and 80% × 74.2 = $59.4M can be used, leaving taxable income of $14.8M and tax of $3.7M. Working capital falls from $135.4M to $131.4M because revenue falls, releasing $4.1M. Free cash flow is 224.2 − 3.7 + 4.1 = $224.6M.

### Question 3 solution: The learning curve

**(a) Estimating the learning rate.** The experience-curve model is cost = a × Qᵇ, so log(cost) = log(a) + b × log(Q). Regressing the five points of Exhibit B:

| Q (GWh) | ln Q | Cost ($/kWh) | ln cost |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.5 | 0.4055 | 41.0 | 3.7136 |
| 3.0 | 1.0986 | 35.6 | 3.5722 |
| 6.0 | 1.7918 | 30.2 | 3.4078 |
| 12.0 | 2.4849 | 26.9 | 3.2921 |
| 24.0 | 3.1781 | 22.6 | 3.1179 |

The means are ln Q = 1.7918 and ln cost = 3.4208. The sum of squares of ln Q about its mean is 4.8045 and the cross-product sum is −1.0199, so the slope is b = −1.0199 / 4.8045 = −0.2123. The learning rate is 2ᵇ = 2^(−0.2123) = **86.3%**, meaning each doubling of cumulative output reduces cost by 13.7%. The intercept gives a cost of $44.75 per kWh at Q = 1 GWh for Brightwater. R² is 0.997. The standard error of the slope is 0.0065, so with three degrees of freedom (t = 3.18) the 95% confidence interval for b is −0.2329 to −0.1917, which corresponds to learning rates of 85.1% to 87.6%. Management's 85% is just outside the favourable end of that interval. The four successive doubling ratios (86.8%, 84.8%, 89.1% and 84.0%) show how noisy a single estimate is, and with only five observations from one plant the interval is probably too narrow.

**(b) Deriving the average conversion cost.** The exponent for an 85% curve is b = ln(0.85) / ln(2) = -0.2345. If the cost of the Q-th GWh is 36 × Qᵇ, the total conversion cost of the output between cumulative volumes Q₁ and Q₂ is the integral of 36 × Qᵇ from Q₁ to Q₂, which equals 36 × (Q₂ᵇ⁺¹ − Q₁ᵇ⁺¹) / (b + 1), with b + 1 = 0.7655. The average cost per kWh is that total divided by the output Q₂ − Q₁.

```
Year 2: Q1 = 0, Q2 = 5.25 GWh
  5.25^0.7655 = 3.5588
  total = 36 × 3.5588 / 0.7655 = 167.4  ($M)
  average = 167.4 / 5.25 = $31.88 per kWh

Year 3: Q1 = 5.25, Q2 = 14.25 GWh
  14.25^0.7655 = 7.6434
  total = 36 × (7.6434 − 3.5588) / 0.7655 = 192.1  ($M)
  average = 192.1 / 9.00 = $21.34 per kWh
```

The average cost falls from $31.88 in Year 2 to $21.34 in Year 3 and $14.75 in Year 6, as cumulative output rises from 5.3 GWh to 51.5 GWh. A common error is to apply the curve to each year's output separately, which resets cost every year. The correct unit of account is cumulative output over the plant's life, which is also why Phase 2 inherits Phase 1's learning.

**(c) NPV by learning rate** (project discount rate 10.2%).

| Learning rate | Exponent b | Conv. cost yr 2 | Yr 6 | Yr 15 | NPV no subsidy | NPV with subsidy |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 80% | 0.32 | 31.1 | 10.6 | 7.0 | (315) | 334 |
| 85% | 0.23 | 31.9 | 14.8 | 11.0 | (537) | 115 |
| 90% | 0.15 | 33.0 | 20.2 | 16.6 | (851) | (186) |
| 95% | 0.07 | 34.4 | 27.2 | 24.7 | (1,342) | (660) |
| 100% | 0.00 | 36.0 | 36.0 | 36.0 | (1,987) | (1,305) |

At the base rate of 85%, NPV with subsidies is $115M. Each one-point worsening of the learning rate removes about $(52)M of NPV (reading the sign as a loss). At the regression estimate of 86.3% the NPV is $45M, and at 87.1% the NPV is about $1M, so the subsidised project breaks even at a learning rate of roughly 87%. Without subsidies, even an 80% learning rate leaves NPV at $(537)M less than zero.

**(d) Why the learning rate may be overstated and how the board should use it.** Those who sponsor a project own its assumptions, and a faster learning rate is the cheapest way to improve the numbers. The estimate comes from one plant, in an earlier technology, in a period when yields improved for reasons unrelated to cumulative volume. Learning also requires stable operations, a labour force that stays and a technology that does not change; a switch of chemistry resets the curve. The board should treat 85% as the optimistic end of a range of 85% to 90% and insist on milestones (yield and conversion cost at fixed cumulative volumes) that would reveal slippage early. The sensitivity also supports a management incentive tied to learning milestones, not just to commissioning dates.

### Question 4 solution: Subsidies

**(a) Value of each instrument.** Each case is the base model with one instrument switched on:

| Case | NPV ($M) | Increment vs none |
| :--- | ---: | ---: |
| No subsidies | (537) | – |
| Capital grant only (12%) | (395) | 143 |
| Production credit only ($10/kWh, 92% monetised) | (63) | 475 |
| Property-tax abatement only | (502) | 35 |
| Sum of individual effects |  | 653 |
| All three together | 115 | 652 |
| Interaction (all - sum) |  | (1) |

Reading the table: the capital grant adds $143M. The production credit adds $475M (the present value of the credit cash flows is $475M; the NPV increment is the same to the nearest million because the credit is not taxed and its timing interacts only slightly with the loss carry-forward). The abatement adds $35M (its after-tax present value is lower than the $44M pre-tax-flow figure, because the abated property tax is a deductible expense whose saving is taxed). The three instruments together are worth $652M. The sum of the individual effects is $653M, which differs from the combined effect by $1M. This interaction arises because taxable income changes when more than one instrument is on, and so the timing of loss utilisation changes. The capital grant reduces the depreciable basis, which lowers depreciation and slightly increases tax; the abatement increases taxable income; both effects change how quickly the loss carry-forward is used.

**(b) Tax ambiguities.** If the production credit is taxable, NPV falls from $115M to $2M, a loss of about $113M; the tax treatment of one line item is the difference between a project that creates value and one that merely breaks even. If the grant does not reduce depreciable basis, the NPV rises to $136M, a gain of about $21M. The board should therefore not approve the project until tax counsel has resolved the credit question, ideally through an advance ruling or a statutory clarification.

**(c) Repeal risk.** If the credit is lost for production from Year 5 onward, the NPV is $(185)M, a loss of $300M relative to the intact case. The expected NPV with a 30% chance of repeal is:

```
E[NPV] = 0.70 × 114.7 + 0.30 × (−185.3) = 80.3 − 55.6 = $24.7M
```

The break-even probability of repeal is 114.7 / (114.7 + 185.3) = 38.2%, so any probability of repeal above about 38% makes the expected NPV negative. This is above Bergström's 30% but not by a wide margin, and the 30% is a judgement that nobody can verify. The credit is also an asymmetric risk: it only matters if the plant is in operation when the statute is reviewed, which is exactly when Helios will have the least flexibility.

**(d) Share of value from subsidies.** The unsubsidised NPV is −$537M and the subsidised NPV is +$115M. Subsidies contribute $652M. Without them, the project would destroy $537M of value. The subsidised value is just 18% of the subsidy value (115 / 652). In other words, **the whole of the project's positive NPV comes from government support, and 73% of that support comes from one instrument, the production credit, that is subject to legislative review.** Students should resist the conclusion that subsidies are "free": they are payments the Province makes in return for the commitment, and a rational firm accepts them only while the project remains worth building, but a board should know how much of the value rests on a statute.
