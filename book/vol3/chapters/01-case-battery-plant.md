# Chapter 1: Case: Helios Cell, a Battery Gigafactory Decision

*Illustrative case. Helios Cell Technologies, its people, its lenders, the Province of Ardmore and every number in this chapter are fictional and were constructed for teaching. Units: $M means millions of US dollars; GWh means gigawatt-hours of cell capacity. Because one GWh is one million kWh, output in GWh multiplied by a price in $/kWh gives revenue in $M directly. The case draws on Volume 1, Chapters 6 to 8, 11 and 14, and on Volume 2 for adjusted present value and options.*

## The Situation

On 4 November 2026, Dev Raman, chief financial officer of Helios Cell Technologies Inc., opened a folder labelled "Project Meridian" and found at the top the third revision of a term sheet from the Province of Ardmore. The Province would support a new lithium-ion cell plant at Port Alder, a deep-water industrial site on its northern coast, with a capital grant, a production credit, a property-tax abatement and a low-interest development loan. The offer expired on 31 January 2027. The Helios board would meet on 15 December 2026, and Raman had to bring it a recommendation.

Helios, headquartered in Marlow Bay, makes prismatic lithium-ion cells for stationary energy storage (battery containers for utilities and large commercial sites) and for commercial electric vehicles such as buses, delivery vans and port equipment. Its two plants, Brightwater (8 GWh a year, eight years old) and Corrin Valley (5 GWh, four years old), ran at high utilisation through 2026. The company expected revenue of $3,210M and EBITDA of $545M for 2026, and its shares traded at $20.00, an equity value of about $6,100M on 305 million shares.

Meridian would be larger than anything Helios had attempted. The engineering study called for a 15 GWh plant (Phase 1) costing $1,500M, spent $900M in the first year of construction and $600M in the second, with the first cells off the line in the third year. Phase 2 was a further 15 GWh, taking the site to 30 GWh, at $1,300M if started three years after Phase 1 or $1,248M (a 4% discount for ordering together) if committed with Phase 1. Helios did not have to decide Phase 2 now, but it did have to decide whether to reserve the land, power connection and permits for it, and whether to build it at once.

### The people

Marguerite Okonkwo-Hale, the chief executive, was an engineer and a believer in scale: "cell manufacturing is a learning business, and whoever makes the most cells learns fastest." She wanted to approve all 30 GWh in December. Raman was more cautious: every earlier Helios plant had run over budget and late, and the first plant's cell price had fallen by about a third in five years. Lena Bergström, senior vice president for strategy, had negotiated the term sheet and favoured Phase 1 now with an option on Phase 2. Tomasz Wierzba, vice president of manufacturing, owned the learning-curve and ramp-up assumptions. Priya Anand, the treasurer, owned the funding plan and the bank covenant. Dr. Nasrin Kaveh, who chaired the audit committee, had told Raman she would oppose any plan that endangered the covenant.

### The decision

The board had to answer three connected questions: whether to commit to Phase 1 on the terms offered; whether to build Phase 2 now or hold it as an option to be exercised in three years; and what conditions to attach. Beneath them lay a fourth, which Raman felt he owed the directors: how much of the project's apparent value came from subsidies, and how secure were they?

"Year 0" is the date of commitment, January 2027. Later cash flows occur at year end. Construction ends in Year 1 (2028) and first production comes in Year 2 (2029).

## Industry Context

**Demand.** Cell demand is driven by the shift of road vehicles to electric drive, now spreading from cars to buses and trucks, and by stationary storage used by grids to absorb solar and wind output. Both are growing quickly and both are policy-sensitive. The consultants Helios hired (a fictional firm, Calder & Voss) gave a low, central and high demand view, and the gap between low and high was larger than the central growth rate. A model that treats demand as known misleads.

**Price.** Cell prices have fallen for a decade because of lower raw-material prices, process improvement, larger plants and new capacity. Declines arrive in steps, when a capacity surplus coincides with a fall in metal prices, and pause in shortages. A manufacturer's margin is the gap between a falling price and a falling cost, and survivors are those whose costs fall faster. Brightwater earned a high margin in its third year and a modest one in its eighth.

**Costs and learning.** A cell plant has three cost layers. Materials (active materials, electrolyte, separators, foils) are about half the early selling price and largely pass through from commodity markets. Conversion costs (labour, energy, consumables, scrap) fall as operators learn. Fixed cash costs (supervision, engineering, insurance) do not vary with volume, which is why utilisation matters so much. The manufacturing literature describes conversion cost with an experience curve: each doubling of cumulative output cuts unit cost by a fixed fraction. A learning rate of 85% means cost after a doubling is 85% of cost before. The curve runs on cumulative output across the site, so Phase 2 would inherit Phase 1's experience.

**Policy support.** Governments competing for plants offer grants, production credits, tax abatements and subsidised loans. Their value is large relative to the plant's margin and they carry political risk that private contracts do not: a credit is legislated, and legislatures change course. The Ardmore credit statute is subject to review in 2031.

**Capital intensity.** Phase 1 costs $100M per GWh of annual capacity. A plant takes two years to build and three to four to ramp, so cash flow dips for years before a long plateau. That shape makes NPV very sensitive to the discount rate, the speed of ramp-up and the price path in the plateau years. Helios's edge is a licence for a high-density chemistry, an anchor-customer letter and the Province's goodwill; its weakness is scale, since 15 GWh is small by global standards.

### Exhibit C: Tax counsel's open questions

Counsel could not say whether the production credit is taxable income when received ("probably not", 60% confidence), or whether the capital grant reduces the depreciable basis of the plant ("yes" under general principles, but the statute is silent). The base case assumes the credit is not taxable and the grant does reduce basis. The CFO wants to know how much this matters.

### Exhibit D: Policy risk

The credit statute is reviewed in 2031, Year 4 of the project, and the opposition has promised to "review the cost-effectiveness" of the credit. Bergström gives a 30% chance that the credit is repealed or sharply curtailed for production from Year 5 onward. The grant, abatement and loan are contractual and not subject to review. Students should model repeal as loss of the credit for all production from Year 5 on, with probability 30%, while recognising that no one can know it.

### Exhibit E: Anchor customer

Northgate Coachworks, a bus maker, has signed a non-binding letter of intent to buy 4 GWh a year for five years at a price no lower than $90 per kWh, with no penalty to either side. Sales treats it as validation. The base case ignores it.

### Exhibit F: The covenant

Net debt after the Phase 1 funding plan would exceed today's level while plant EBITDA is negative in Year 2. Anand has asked the banks for a construction holiday on the covenant but has no written commitment.

### Exhibit G: Volatility and rivals

For the Phase 2 option the consultants offered volatility of 25% (long-run pack-price volatility), 35% (listed manufacturers' equity volatility, adjusted for leverage) and 45% (spot cell prices in the last shortage). Raman chose 35%. Calder & Voss also note that announced rival plants would, if all built, add roughly twice the demand growth of the next three years, which is why Phase 2 output is assumed to sell at spot at a discount to Phase 1.

## Teaching Note and Full Solutions

### Case synopsis

Helios Cell is a capital-budgeting case in which the arithmetic is straightforward and the judgement is not. A cell plant has a deep early cash dip, a long plateau, a falling price, an experience curve that rewards volume, and a subsidy package worth more than the project's unsubsidised value. The questions are how to value each feature, how to separate subsidy value from operating value, and whether to pay for flexibility by phasing. It works best after Volume 1, Chapters 6 to 8. All results below come from one Python model of the cash flows, checked by hand on selected years. They use year-end flows, a 10.2% discount rate (Question 1) and the base-case inputs; students working with rounded intermediate numbers may differ by a unit or two.

### Learning objectives

Students should be able to (1) estimate a project cost of capital by unlevering and relevering comparable betas; (2) build incremental after-tax cash flows including working capital, land, salvage and a loss carry-forward; (3) model a learning curve on cumulative output; (4) separate and value subsidies and price political risk; (5) run sensitivity, break-even and scenario analysis; (6) value a staged-investment option by Black-Scholes and a lattice and weigh it against the cost of delay; (7) use APV for a subsidised loan; and (8) write a board recommendation with conditions.

### Question 1 solution: Cost of capital

**Step 1: unlever each beta.** With zero debt beta, the asset beta is the equity beta divided by one plus (1 minus tax rate) times debt/equity.

| Company | Levered beta | 1 + (1 − t) × D/E | Unlevered beta |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Aurelia Cells | 1.69 | 1 + 0.79 × 0.45 = 1.3555 | 1.2468 |
| Kestrel Power Systems | 1.51 | 1 + 0.75 × 0.30 = 1.2250 | 1.2327 |
| Nordvik Energy Storage | 1.34 | 1 + 0.78 × 0.20 = 1.1560 | 1.1592 |
| Orrin Electrochem | 1.61 | 1 + 0.76 × 0.55 = 1.4180 | 1.1354 |
| Tamsin Materials Group | 1.09 | 1 + 0.74 × 0.25 = 1.1850 | 0.9198 |

The simple average is 1.14 and the median is 1.16. Tamsin is a materials supplier, with less operating leverage than the pure cell makers, so a reader might drop it or weight it less; doing so lifts the average to about 1.19. The solutions use the average of all five, 1.14.

**Step 2: relever.** The target debt/(debt + equity) is 30%, so debt/equity is 0.30 / 0.70 = 0.4286.

```
βL = βU × [1 + (1 − t) × D/E]
   = 1.1388 × [1 + 0.75 × 0.4286]
   = 1.1388 × 1.3214
   = 1.505
```

**Step 3: cost of equity (CAPM).**

```
ke = rf + βL × ERP = 4.0% + 1.505 × 5.5% = 12.28%
```

**Step 4: WACC.**

```
WACC = E/V × ke + D/V × kd × (1 − t)
     = 0.70 × 12.28% + 0.30 × 7.0% × 0.75
     = 8.59% + 1.58%
     = 10.17%, rounded to 10.2%
```

The equity part contributes 8.59 points and the after-tax debt part 1.57 points. For use in Question 9, the unlevered cost of capital is 4.0% + 1.1388 × 5.5% = 10.26%.

**Why not Helios's own inputs?** Helios is about 13% debt-financed, and its beta reflects mature plants with contracted volumes. Meridian is a greenfield single-site plant in a falling-price market. The discount rate depends on the risk of the project's cash flows, not on the sponsor's name (Volume 1, Chapter 11), and 30% is a project-appropriate structure. The 10% hurdle has no traceable derivation. It is close here, but an untraced hurdle gives no guidance when risk differs across projects, and each added point of discount rate costs about $90M of NPV (Question 6).

### Question 2 solution: Base-case cash flows without subsidies

**Relevant versus irrelevant costs.** Only incremental cash flows count.

| Item | Treatment | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $22M site studies already paid | Excluded | Sunk; paid whatever the decision |
| $14M allocated head-office cost | Excluded | Not incremental: headcount does not change |
| $18M Brightwater cannibalisation | Excluded | Brightwater is full and the sales can be replaced from the waiting list; if the waiting list were empty the item would be a real opportunity cost |
| $40M land | Included (Year 0 outflow, Year 15 recovery) | Real cash; recoverable at cost |
| Working capital at 12% of revenue | Included | Real cash tied up |
| Salvage 8% of plant cost | Included, taxed as a gain | Book value is zero after ten years |

**Year-by-year mechanics.** For each year:

```
Revenue            = output (GWh) × price ($/kWh)                    [$M]
EBITDA             = revenue − materials − conversion − fixed − SG&A
Taxable income     = EBITDA − tax depreciation (+ gain on sale in Year 15)
Tax                = 25% × (taxable income − NOL used)
NOL used           = min(NOL balance, 80% × taxable income)
FCF                = EBITDA − tax − capex − ΔNWC + salvage and land recovery
```

**Worked example, Year 6 (no subsidies).** Output is 15 × 85% = 12.75 GWh and price is 97.0 × 0.97⁴ = $85.87 per kWh, so revenue is 12.75 × 85.87 = $1,094.9M. Materials are 12.75 × $41.62 = $530.6M. The conversion cost (Question 3) is $14.75 per kWh, so $188.1M. Fixed opex is $119.1M and SG&A is 3% × 1,094.9 = $32.8M. EBITDA is 1,094.9 − 530.6 − 188.1 − 119.1 − 32.8 = $224.2M. Tax depreciation is 1,500 / 10 = $150.0M, so taxable income before losses is $74.2M. The loss balance at the end of Year 5 is $162M, and 80% × 74.2 = $59.4M can be used, leaving taxable income of $14.8M and tax of $3.7M. Working capital falls from $135.4M to $131.4M because revenue falls, releasing $4.1M. Free cash flow is 224.2 − 3.7 + 4.1 = $224.6M.

### Question 3 solution: The learning curve

**(a) Estimating the learning rate.** The experience-curve model is cost = a × Qᵇ, so log(cost) = log(a) + b × log(Q). Regressing the five points of Exhibit B:

| Q (GWh) | ln Q | Cost ($/kWh) | ln cost |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1.5 | 0.4055 | 41.0 | 3.7136 |
| 3.0 | 1.0986 | 35.6 | 3.5722 |
| 6.0 | 1.7918 | 30.2 | 3.4078 |
| 12.0 | 2.4849 | 26.9 | 3.2921 |
| 24.0 | 3.1781 | 22.6 | 3.1179 |

The means are ln Q = 1.7918 and ln cost = 3.4208. The sum of squares of ln Q about its mean is 4.8045 and the cross-product sum is −1.0199, so the slope is b = −1.0199 / 4.8045 = −0.2123. The learning rate is 2ᵇ = 2^(−0.2123) = **86.3%**, meaning each doubling of cumulative output reduces cost by 13.7%. The intercept gives a cost of $44.75 per kWh at Q = 1 GWh for Brightwater. R² is 0.997. The standard error of the slope is 0.0065, so with three degrees of freedom (t = 3.18) the 95% confidence interval for b is −0.2329 to −0.1917, which corresponds to learning rates of 85.1% to 87.6%. Management's 85% is just outside the favourable end of that interval. The four successive doubling ratios (86.8%, 84.8%, 89.1% and 84.0%) show how noisy a single estimate is, and with only five observations from one plant the interval is probably too narrow.

**(b) Deriving the average conversion cost.** The exponent for an 85% curve is b = ln(0.85) / ln(2) = -0.2345. If the cost of the Q-th GWh is 36 × Qᵇ, the total conversion cost of the output between cumulative volumes Q₁ and Q₂ is the integral of 36 × Qᵇ from Q₁ to Q₂, which equals 36 × (Q₂ᵇ⁺¹ − Q₁ᵇ⁺¹) / (b + 1), with b + 1 = 0.7655. The average cost per kWh is that total divided by the output Q₂ − Q₁.

```
Year 2: Q1 = 0, Q2 = 5.25 GWh
  5.25^0.7655 = 3.5588
  total = 36 × 3.5588 / 0.7655 = 167.4  ($M)
  average = 167.4 / 5.25 = $31.88 per kWh

Year 3: Q1 = 5.25, Q2 = 14.25 GWh
  14.25^0.7655 = 7.6434
  total = 36 × (7.6434 − 3.5588) / 0.7655 = 192.1  ($M)
  average = 192.1 / 9.00 = $21.34 per kWh
```

The average cost falls from $31.88 in Year 2 to $21.34 in Year 3 and $14.75 in Year 6, as cumulative output rises from 5.3 GWh to 51.5 GWh. A common error is to apply the curve to each year's output separately, which resets cost every year. The correct unit of account is cumulative output over the plant's life, which is also why Phase 2 inherits Phase 1's learning.

**(c) NPV by learning rate** (project discount rate 10.2%).

| Learning rate | Exponent b | Conv. cost yr 2 | Yr 6 | Yr 15 | NPV no subsidy | NPV with subsidy |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 80% | 0.32 | 31.1 | 10.6 | 7.0 | (315) | 334 |
| 85% | 0.23 | 31.9 | 14.8 | 11.0 | (537) | 115 |
| 90% | 0.15 | 33.0 | 20.2 | 16.6 | (851) | (186) |
| 95% | 0.07 | 34.4 | 27.2 | 24.7 | (1,342) | (660) |
| 100% | 0.00 | 36.0 | 36.0 | 36.0 | (1,987) | (1,305) |

At the base rate of 85%, NPV with subsidies is $115M. Each one-point worsening of the learning rate removes about $(52)M of NPV (reading the sign as a loss). At the regression estimate of 86.3% the NPV is $45M, and at 87.1% the NPV is about $1M, so the subsidised project breaks even at a learning rate of roughly 87%. Without subsidies, even an 80% learning rate leaves NPV at $(537)M less than zero.

**(d) Overstatement and use.** Sponsors own their assumptions, and a faster learning rate is the cheapest way to improve the numbers. The estimate comes from one plant, an earlier technology and a period when yields improved for other reasons; a change of chemistry would reset the curve. The board should treat 85% as the optimistic end of a range of 85% to 90% and tie management incentives to yield and conversion-cost milestones at fixed cumulative volumes.

The grant adds $143M, the production credit $475M (equal to its present value of $475M, since it is untaxed) and the abatement $35M (less than the $44M pre-tax present value because the saved property tax is deductible). Together they add $652M. The $1M interaction arises because the grant lowers depreciation and the abatement raises taxable income, which changes how fast losses are used.

**(b) Tax ambiguities.** If the credit is taxable, NPV falls from $115M to $2M, a loss of $113M: one line of tax law separates value creation from break-even. If the grant does not reduce depreciable basis, NPV rises to $136M. The board should require tax counsel's resolution of the credit question, ideally an advance ruling, before approval.

**(c) Repeal risk.** If the credit is lost from Year 5, NPV is $(185)M, $300M lower. The expected NPV at 30% is

```
E[NPV] = 0.70 × 114.7 + 0.30 × (−185.3) = 80.3 − 55.6 = $24.7M
```

The break-even probability is 114.7 / 300.0 = 38%, not far above Bergström's unverifiable 30%. The risk bites when the plant is operating and Helios has least flexibility.

**(d) Share from subsidies.** Unsubsidised NPV is −$537M and subsidised NPV +$115M, so subsidies contribute $652M and **all of the positive NPV comes from government support**; 73% of that support (475 / 652) is the production credit, which is subject to review. Subsidies are not free: they are paid for the commitment, but the board should know how much value rests on a statute.

### Question 8 solution: Phase it or build it all now?

**(a) All now.** With Phase 2 capex of $1,248M at the end of Year 1 and output from Year 2, NPV is **$302M** and IRR 12.13%, $187M above Phase 1 alone.

**(b) Comparison.** Phase 1 plus the option is worth $275M (formula) or $260M (three-step lattice), so building now is worth $27M to $42M more. The cost of waiting (two years of margin, the lost 4% discount, delayed learning) nearly matches the value of flexibility; the two are equal at a volatility of **39%**, inside the 25% to 45% range.

**(c) Covenant.** After Phase 1, net debt is 520 + 500 + 350 + 300 = $1,670M, or 3.06 times EBITDA of $545M. Building Phase 2 now adds $1,248M, giving $2,918M or **5.35 times**. ### Question 9 solution: Financing and APV

**(a) The development loan.** Myers's method values a loan as proceeds minus the PV of after-tax payments discounted at the pre-tax market rate (7%). Interest after tax is 500 × 2.5% × 0.75 = $9.375M a year in Years 2 to 11; principal is repaid at the end of Year 11.

```
PV of proceeds (Year 1)      = 500 / 1.07                    = 467.3
PV of after-tax interest     = 9.375 × annuity, Years 2-11   =  61.5
PV of principal              = 500 / 1.07^11                 = 237.5
NPV of loan                  = 467.3 − 61.5 − 237.5          = $168.3M
```

A market-rate loan would be worth $57M (tax shield only), so $111M is the subsidy in the 4.5-point rate gap.

**(b) APV.**

| Component | $M |
| :--- | ---: |
| Base-case NPV at WACC 10.2% (Question 4) | 115 |
| Base-case NPV at unlevered cost of capital 10.26% | 109 |

The unlevered NPV is slightly below the WACC figure because the unlevered rate (10.26%) exceeds the WACC (10.17%), which embeds a debt tax shield. The APV is **$277M**.

**(c) Should it count?** Yes in principle, since the loan exists only because Meridian is built. But the unlevered NPV is already positive, so the loan is not what makes the project acceptable and must not rescue a negative one. ### Sensitivity discussion

Three results organise the discussion. First, value is concentrated in a few drivers: a 2.5% price shortfall or a learning rate of 87% instead of 85% erases the subsidised NPV, and a 30% repeal probability brings the expected value to about zero. Second, the drivers are correlated in practice: a price collapse driven by oversupply also tends to coincide with weak utilisation and slower learning, so the downside scenario is more plausible than independent one-at-a-time swings suggest. Third, flexibility is worth most when value is near the strike: Phase 2 is almost at the money, so its option value responds strongly to volatility, which is the one input that cannot be observed. Instructors should push students to say which of the 25%, 35% and 45% estimates they trust and why.

### Common student errors

1. **Applying the learning curve to each year's output** instead of cumulative output, which resets cost every year (Question 3).
2. **Using Helios's capital structure or beta,** or leaving the 10% hurdle in place (Question 1).
3. **Including sunk, allocated or cannibalisation costs** (Exhibit A), or omitting land and working capital recovery.
4. **Ignoring the tax-loss mechanics,** either giving the project an immediate tax refund or forgetting that salvage is taxed in full.
5. **Treating subsidies as certain,** or as additive without checking taxability; presenting the subsidised NPV without the unsubsidised one.
6. **Reporting payback as evidence of value** when discounted payback never occurs.
7. **Discounting Phase 2 capex at the risk-free rate in the DCF but at the project rate in the option,** or computing the option on the NPV (−23) instead of on the PV of the operating flows (949).
8. **Counting the loan twice,** once through a lower WACC and again as a subsidy.
9. **Choosing between phasing and building now by comparing NPVs alone,** ignoring the covenant.

### Alternative defensible answers

The model solution recommends Phase 1 with an option, but a well-argued case can be made for other answers. **Build all 30 GWh now** is defensible on pure value (it exceeds phasing by $27M to $42M at 35% volatility and by more if volatility is below 39%), but only if the covenant can be amended and about $874M of equity found. **Decline** is defensible if the board regards the 30% repeal probability as too low, since at 38% the expected NPV is negative, or if it will not rely on a learning rate that sits at the edge of the confidence interval. **Use a different discount rate**: a student who removes Tamsin from the comparables obtains an asset beta near 1.19 and a WACC near 10.5%, which lowers NPV by about $60M but does not change the ranking of alternatives. A student who treats the credit as taxable (the 40% case in counsel's opinion) finds a project worth about $2M, so the decision becomes one of negotiation, not valuation. Another defensible route values Phase 2 with a decision tree and scenario probabilities instead of option pricing; this gives similar answers when probabilities are calibrated to the lattice. The grading standard should reward a clear decision rule, correct mechanics and honest treatment of what is uncertain, not agreement with any single figure.

**To:** The Board of Directors, Helios Cell Technologies Inc.
**From:** Dev Raman, Chief Financial Officer
**Date:** 1 December 2026
