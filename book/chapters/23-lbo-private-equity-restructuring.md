## Opening Vignette

*(Illustrative scenario; the company and all figures are fictional.)*

Marlow Packaging is a family-founded maker of corrugated and specialty packaging with revenue of $500 million and EBITDA of $100 million. Its shares trade thinly on a regional exchange at roughly 7 times EBITDA, a discount to the 9 times that larger peers command. The founder's children want liquidity; the board is tired of quarterly scrutiny over a business that earns steady, unexciting cash flows.

Northgate Capital, a mid-sized private equity firm, sees an opportunity. Its analysts note that Marlow's plants run at 71 percent utilization, that purchasing is fragmented across six regional buyers, and that the company has never used more than 1.5 times leverage. Northgate proposes to acquire Marlow for 9 times EBITDA, financing more than half the price with debt that will be serviced from Marlow's own cash flow. The sponsor will contribute roughly 41 percent of the capital in equity, install a new chief operating officer, consolidate procurement, close two inefficient plants and sell a non-core labeling division.

The founders' advisers ask the question every board faces in such a transaction: if the buyer can pay a premium of roughly 29 percent and still earn a high return, where does the value come from? Is it tax shields on the new debt? Better management? A cheap entry price relative to the exit price? Or simply risk transferred to lenders and to employees? Northgate's investment committee asks the mirror-image question: what happens if earnings fall 20 percent in a recession while $550 million of debt is outstanding?

This chapter answers both. We build the mechanics of a leveraged buyout from first principles, test where returns come from, examine what happens when leverage turns from friend to foe, and study the legal and financial machinery of restructuring and separation that sits at the other end of the corporate life cycle.

## 23.1 The Anatomy of a Leveraged Buyout

### 23.1.1 What an LBO is

A leveraged buyout (LBO) is the acquisition of a company, or a division, financed predominantly with borrowed money, where the target's own assets and cash flows support the debt. A private equity (PE) sponsor, usually a limited partnership fund, supplies a minority of the purchase price as equity, lenders supply the rest, and the sponsor takes control. The sponsor's goal is to improve the business, repay part of the debt from operating cash flow, and sell or list the company within roughly four to seven years at a higher equity value.

Three variants matter in practice. In a classic sponsor LBO, an outside fund buys the company. In a management buyout (MBO), the existing management team, usually backed by a PE fund, acquires the company. In a take-private, a listed company is acquired and delisted, typically at a premium of 20 to 40 percent over the undisturbed share price. Each shares the same engine: high leverage, concentrated ownership, strong incentives and a defined exit.

### 23.1.2 Why debt works here, and where it stops working

The theoretical case for high leverage draws on three strands of corporate finance covered earlier in this book.

First, the tax shield. Interest is deductible, so debt lowers the after-tax cost of capital as in Modigliani-Miller with taxes. For a firm paying a 25 percent tax rate, each dollar of interest saves 25 cents of tax, subject to the interest-deductibility limits that many jurisdictions impose (often a cap tied to a percentage of EBITDA or EBIT).

Second, agency costs of free cash flow, per Jensen. Mature companies with stable cash flows and few high-return projects tend to hoard cash or reinvest it in low-return expansion. A heavy debt load forces managers to disgorge cash through mandatory interest and principal payments. Debt acts as a disciplinary device.

Third, incentive alignment. The sponsor and management hold concentrated equity stakes, often with management investing meaningfully from personal wealth plus a pool of options, so they gain a large share of every dollar of value created.

Against these benefits stand the costs of financial distress: lost customers, supplier tightening, forced asset sales, management distraction and legal fees. The trade-off theory says the optimum lies where the marginal tax shield equals the marginal expected distress cost. LBO targets are chosen because their distress costs are lower than average, not because they are immune to distress. The ideal target has stable, predictable cash flow, modest capital expenditure needs, a strong market position, tangible assets that can serve as collateral, identifiable costs to cut and clear exit routes. Cyclical, capital-hungry or fast-changing businesses make poor LBO candidates.

### 23.1.3 The financing stack

LBO debt is layered by priority and cost, so each tranche appeals to a different investor.

| Tranche | Typical seniority | Typical features | Indicative cost | Holders |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revolving credit facility | First lien | Undrawn at close; liquidity backstop | Floating rate + low spread | Banks |
| Term loan A / B | First lien | Amortizing (A) or bullet-like (B); secured on all assets; covenants (A) or covenant-lite (B) | Floating rate + moderate spread | Banks, institutional loan funds |
| Second lien / unitranche | Second lien or blended | Secured but junior; higher spread | Floating rate + high spread | Credit funds |
| Senior unsecured notes | Unsecured | Fixed coupon, 7 to 10 years, call protection | Fixed, high-yield | Bond investors |
| Subordinated / mezzanine / PIK | Subordinated | Cash or payment-in-kind interest, warrants | Highest debt cost | Mezzanine funds |
| Sponsor equity and management rollover | Residual | Common or preferred equity | Target return 20 percent plus | Fund, managers |

Leverage is measured against EBITDA. Total debt of 5 to 6 times EBITDA was common in buoyant markets, and senior secured leverage of 3 to 4 times. Lenders also test coverage: EBITDA divided by cash interest, and fixed charge coverage including capex and mandatory amortization. Debt markets move in cycles, and the available leverage and the multiples sponsors can pay move with them. An important managerial implication: purchase multiples and returns are not independent of credit conditions, so a deal that works at 6 times leverage and a 4 percent base rate may fail at 4 times leverage and 8 percent.

### 23.1.4 Sources and uses

Every LBO analysis starts with a sources-and-uses table. Uses are what must be paid at closing: the equity purchase price (or enterprise value if existing debt is refinanced), fees (advisory, financing, legal) and any cash placed on the balance sheet. Sources are the debt tranches and the sponsor equity, which is the plug.

**Example 23.1 – Sources and uses for the Marlow buyout.** Northgate agrees to acquire Marlow at 9.0 times trailing EBITDA of $100 million. Existing debt is refinanced as part of the purchase, so the purchase price is the enterprise value. Transaction and financing fees total $27 million, and Northgate wants $10 million of minimum cash left on the balance sheet. Total debt is set at 5.5 times EBITDA, split into a $400 million term loan B and $150 million of senior notes.

Step 1: Enterprise value = 9.0 × $100m = $900m.

Step 2: Total uses = EV + fees + minimum cash = 900 + 27 + 10 = $937m.

Step 3: Total debt = 5.5 × 100 = $550m (TLB $400m + notes $150m).

Step 4: Sponsor equity (plug) = 937 − 550 = $387m, which is 387 / 937 = 41.3 percent of capitalization.

| Uses | $m | Sources | $m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Purchase of enterprise | 900 | Term loan B | 400 |
| Fees and expenses | 27 | Senior notes | 150 |
| Cash to balance sheet | 10 | Sponsor equity | 387 |
| **Total uses** | **937** | **Total sources** | **937** |

The equity cushion of 41 percent is typical of mid-cycle deals; in earlier eras equity was often 10 to 20 percent. Higher purchase multiples and tighter lending standards have raised the equity share. Notice that the sponsor's equity cheque is larger than the "price" of the business net of debt (900 − 550 = 350) because it also funds fees and cash. Fees are real value leakage, and they matter more at lower returns.

## 23.2 Projecting Cash Flow and Paying Down Debt

### 23.2.1 The cash sweep

The central mechanism of an LBO is the debt paydown. After servicing interest, paying taxes and funding capex and working capital, free cash flow is swept to repay the prepayable term loan. Each dollar repaid transfers a dollar of enterprise value from creditors to equity holders without any change in the enterprise value itself. Deleveraging is therefore a source of return even if the business does not grow, provided the exit multiple does not fall.

The free cash flow available for debt repayment each year is:

```
FCF to debt paydown = EBITDA − Cash interest − Cash taxes − Capex − ΔNWC
Cash taxes = tax rate × (EBITDA − D&A − Interest)
```

Interest in a simple model is computed on the beginning-of-year balance to avoid circularity; a more refined model uses average balances and iterates. The key behavioral feature is that interest falls as debt falls, so free cash flow accelerates even if EBITDA grows modestly.

**Example 23.2 – Five-year debt schedule for Marlow.** Revenue is $500m and EBITDA margin is 20 percent. EBITDA grows at 8, 8, 7, 6 and 5 percent in years 1 to 5, and the margin is held at 20 percent so revenue is EBITDA / 0.20. D&A is 3 percent of revenue, capex is 4 percent of revenue, and net working capital investment is 10 percent of the change in revenue. The tax rate is 25 percent. The term loan B ($400m) bears 9 percent and the notes ($150m) bear 11 percent fixed; the notes are non-callable and stay outstanding, while all free cash flow sweeps the term loan.

Year 1 detail:

- EBITDA = 100 × 1.08 = $108.0m; revenue = 108 / 0.20 = $540.0m.
- D&A = 3% × 540 = $16.2m; capex = 4% × 540 = $21.6m.
- ΔNWC = 10% × (540 − 500) = $4.0m.
- Interest = 400 × 9% + 150 × 11% = 36.0 + 16.5 = $52.5m.
- Taxable income = 108.0 − 16.2 − 52.5 = $39.3m; tax = 25% × 39.3 = $9.8m.
- FCF = 108.0 − 21.6 − 4.0 − 52.5 − 9.8 = $20.1m.
- Closing term loan = 400 − 20.1 = $379.9m.

Repeating for each year gives:

| Year | EBITDA | Interest | Taxes | Capex | ΔNWC | FCF swept | Term loan (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100.0 | | | | | | 400.0 |
| 1 | 108.0 | 52.5 | 9.8 | 21.6 | 4.0 | 20.1 | 379.9 |
| 2 | 116.6 | 50.7 | 12.1 | 23.3 | 4.3 | 26.2 | 353.7 |
| 3 | 124.8 | 48.3 | 14.4 | 25.0 | 4.1 | 33.0 | 320.8 |
| 4 | 132.3 | 45.4 | 16.8 | 26.5 | 3.7 | 40.0 | 280.8 |
| 5 | 138.9 | 41.8 | 19.1 | 27.8 | 3.3 | 47.0 | 233.8 |

Total debt falls from $550m to 233.8 + 150 = $383.8m. Leverage falls from 5.5 times to 383.8 / 138.9 = 2.76 times, and interest coverage (EBITDA / interest) rises from 100 / 52.5 = 1.9 times in the first year's base to 138.9 / 41.8 = 3.3 times in year 5. Cumulative debt repaid is $166.2m, only about 30 percent of the original debt. The sponsor is not relying on repaying the whole debt; it relies on the debt being refinanceable or repaid from sale proceeds.

### 23.2.2 Reading the schedule critically

Three features of this schedule deserve emphasis. First, year 1 free cash flow of $20.1m on $52.5m of interest is thin: a 15 percent EBITDA shortfall in year 1 (to $91.8m) would remove about $16.2m of EBITDA, and after tax (about $12.1m) leave roughly $8m of free cash flow, well under half of the plan. The model's cushion is small in the early years when leverage is highest. Second, the schedule assumes the margin holds; a margin squeeze from input cost inflation, which is the classic risk in packaging, would hit cash flow more than proportionally. Third, floating-rate term debt exposes the sponsor to base rate movements. A 200 basis point increase on $400m of floating debt adds $8m of annual interest, which is 40 percent of year 1 free cash flow. Sponsors therefore hedge, with swaps or caps, at least half of floating exposure in most deals.

Lenders protect themselves with covenants (maximum leverage and minimum coverage tests, restrictions on dividends and additional debt), excess cash flow sweeps (typically 50 percent of excess cash flow, stepping down as leverage falls) and security over assets. Covenant-lite loans drop the maintenance tests but keep incurrence tests that apply only when the borrower does something, such as taking on more debt.

## 23.3 Returns Mathematics: MOIC, IRR and Attribution

### 23.3.1 The two headline metrics

Sponsors report two numbers. The multiple of invested capital (MOIC, also called money multiple) is total proceeds divided by total equity invested. The internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that sets the net present value of the equity cash flows to zero. For a single investment and a single exit,

```
MOIC = Exit equity value / Initial equity invested
IRR  = MOIC^(1/n) − 1        (single outflow, single inflow, n years)
```

MOIC ignores time; IRR rewards speed. A 2.0x in three years is a 26 percent IRR; a 2.0x in seven years is a 10.4 percent IRR. Sponsors target roughly 20 to 25 percent gross IRRs and 2.0x to 3.0x MOIC. Both metrics are gross of the fund's fees and carried interest, which we address in Section 23.4.

Exit equity value is exit enterprise value less net debt at exit, where net debt includes any residual term loan, notes, and accrued PIK interest, minus cash:

```
Exit EV     = Exit multiple × Exit-year EBITDA
Exit equity = Exit EV − (Term loan + Notes − Cash)
```

### 23.3.2 Where returns come from

Every dollar of equity gain can be attributed to three sources, which provides a disciplined check on whether a deal's thesis is realistic.

```
Equity gain = EBITDA growth + Multiple change + Net debt reduction − Fees and leakage
EBITDA growth  = (Exit EBITDA − Entry EBITDA) × Entry multiple
Multiple change = (Exit multiple − Entry multiple) × Exit EBITDA
Net debt reduction = Entry net debt − Exit net debt
```

The ordering of the first two terms (growth at the entry multiple, expansion on the exit EBITDA) is a convention that assigns the cross term to multiple change; other conventions exist, and an analyst should state the one used.

**Example 23.3 – Marlow returns, sensitivity and attribution.** Using Example 23.2, assume exit at the end of year 5 at 9.0 times EBITDA (no multiple change).

Step 1: Exit EBITDA = $138.9m; exit EV = 9.0 × 138.9 = $1,250.1m.

Step 2: Net debt at exit = term loan 233.8 + notes 150.0 − cash 10.0 = $373.8m.

Step 3: Exit equity = 1,250.1 − 373.8 = $876.3m.

Step 4: MOIC = 876.3 / 387 = 2.26x. IRR = 2.264^(1/5) − 1 = 17.8 percent.

Attribution of the $489.3m equity gain (876.3 − 387.0):

- EBITDA growth: (138.9 − 100.0) × 9.0 = $350.1m (72 percent of the gain).
- Multiple change: (9.0 − 9.0) × 138.9 = $0.
- Net debt reduction: entry net debt = 550 − 10 = $540m; exit net debt $373.8m; reduction = $166.2m (34 percent).
- Fees and leakage: −$27.0m (−6 percent).
- Check: 350.1 + 0 + 166.2 − 27.0 = $489.3m. ✓

The 17.8 percent IRR is below the sponsor's 20 to 25 percent target. Sensitivity to the exit multiple and to the holding period is shown below.

| Exit multiple (year 5) | Exit EV | Exit equity | MOIC | IRR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 8.0x | 1,111.2 | 737.4 | 1.91x | 13.8% |
| 9.0x | 1,250.1 | 876.3 | 2.26x | 17.8% |
| 10.0x | 1,389.0 | 1,015.2 | 2.62x | 21.3% |

| Exit year (at 9.0x) | Exit equity | MOIC | IRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 662.4 | 1.71x | 19.6% |
| 4 | 769.9 | 1.99x | 18.8% |
| 5 | 876.3 | 2.26x | 17.8% |

The first table shows how a one-turn multiple change swings the IRR by roughly 4 percentage points, comparable to the operational plan. The second shows the "IRR clock": because equity compounds slower than the early years' leveraged gains, extending the hold lowers the IRR even as MOIC rises. This explains why sponsors lean toward early exits, dividend recapitalizations and quick flips when markets are strong. For the sponsor to reach a 20 percent IRR on a five-year hold at a flat multiple, exit equity would have to be 387 × 1.2^5 = $963m, requiring either an exit multiple of about 9.6 times or year-5 EBITDA about $10m higher than planned.

### 23.3.3 Dividend recapitalizations

A dividend recapitalization (recap) raises new debt against a portfolio company and pays the proceeds to the sponsor as a dividend. It raises IRR by returning cash earlier, while leaving MOIC roughly unchanged or slightly lower because of the additional interest cost. It also raises risk, since leverage rises just as the sponsor has already recovered part of its capital.

**Example 23.4 – A year-3 recapitalization.** At the end of year 3, Marlow's EBITDA is $124.8m and total debt is 320.8 + 150 = $470.8m (3.8x). The company issues $150m of additional senior notes at 10 percent and pays the sponsor a $150m dividend. For simplicity, the added after-tax interest cost reduces each later year's debt paydown. Extra interest after tax is 150 × 10% × (1 − 25%) = $11.25m in year 4. In year 5 that foregone paydown also carries interest at the term loan rate: 11.25 × 9% × 75% = $0.76m extra, so year-5 foregone paydown = 11.25 + 0.76 = $12.0m.

Step 1: Additional net debt at exit = 150 + 11.25 + 12.01 = $173.3m (the new notes plus two years of foregone paydown).

Step 2: Exit equity = 1,250.1 − (373.8 + 173.3) = $703.0m.

Step 3: Sponsor cash flows: −387.0 (year 0), +150.0 (year 3), +703.0 (year 5).

Step 4: MOIC = (150 + 703.0) / 387 = 2.20x (versus 2.26x without the recap).

Step 5: IRR solves −387 + 150/(1+r)^3 + 703.0/(1+r)^5 = 0, giving r = 18.8 percent, compared with 17.8 percent without the recap.

The recap lifts IRR by about 1 point and reduces MOIC by 0.06x. Recaps transfer value from creditors and from the future only if the lenders mis-price the risk. After the recap, pro forma leverage is (470.8 + 150) / 124.8 = 5.0x, back near the original level, with less equity cushion. If EBITDA falls 20 percent, leverage becomes 6.2x. Sponsors who pay themselves a dividend funded by creditors leave management and employees to carry the downside, a reason recaps draw criticism when followed by distress.

## 23.4 Value Creation Levers and the Economics of a Private Equity Fund

### 23.4.1 Operational levers

The best sponsors insist that financial engineering is the smallest source of durable value. The main operational levers are:

- **Revenue growth:** pricing discipline, sales force reorganization, new products and geographic expansion, and bolt-on acquisitions at lower multiples than the platform (multiple arbitrage). In Marlow, a bolt-on bought at 6 times EBITDA and valued inside the platform at 9 times creates three turns of value per dollar of acquired EBITDA before synergies.
- **Margin improvement:** procurement consolidation, plant rationalization (Marlow's utilization at 71 percent signals excess capacity), overhead reduction and automation. A one-point margin gain on $695m of year 5 revenue is $6.95m of EBITDA, worth $62.6m of EV at 9 times.
- **Capital efficiency:** shrinking working capital through payables terms and inventory control, reducing capex through asset-light models and selling non-core assets. Each dollar of released working capital reduces net debt by one dollar immediately.
- **Governance and incentives:** a small, expert board, monthly reporting, management equity, and the removal of public-company costs. Management ownership of 5 to 15 percent of equity changes behavior.
- **Financial levers:** capital structure optimization, the tax shield, refinancing at tighter spreads when credit improves, and recaps.

### 23.4.2 What the evidence and theory suggest

Academic and industry studies broadly find that buyouts of mature companies are associated with improvements in operating margins and productivity, though results vary with the sample and period, and that PE-backed firms are more exposed to distress in downturns. Gross returns have historically exceeded public equity benchmarks on average, but the net-of-fees premium is smaller and varies widely between funds; top-quartile funds persist more than in mutual funds. Treat any single-number claim with caution. The conceptual point is that a sponsor earns an excess return only by one of four mechanisms: buying below intrinsic value, improving the business, using leverage whose cost is below the return on assets (which transfers risk to equity rather than creating value in a world without frictions), or exiting at a higher multiple than entry. The first and last depend on market timing; the second is skill; the third is compensated risk-bearing, not a free lunch. A managerial rule of thumb: ask what an unlevered return would be. If a deal needs leverage to clear the hurdle and has no operational plan, it is a bet on credit markets.

### 23.4.3 Fund structure and fees

A PE fund is a limited partnership. Limited partners (LPs: pension funds, endowments, sovereign funds, insurers) commit capital that the general partner (GP) draws down over a three- to five-year investment period. Funds typically live ten years, with extensions. The GP earns a management fee, commonly around 2 percent of committed capital during the investment period and lower thereafter, and carried interest, commonly 20 percent of profits above a preferred return (hurdle) of about 8 percent. A catch-up provision lets the GP receive a larger share of distributions after the hurdle is met until it has received 20 percent of total profits.

**Example 23.5 – A simple whole-fund waterfall.** Ignore management fees for clarity. LPs contribute $1,000m, and over five years the fund distributes a total of $1,900m. The preferred return is 8 percent compounded annually, there is a 100 percent GP catch-up, and carry is 20 percent.

Step 1: Return of capital: $1,000m to LPs.

Step 2: Preferred return = 1,000 × (1.08^5 − 1) = 1,000 × 0.469328 = $469.3m to LPs.

Step 3: Catch-up: GP receives 100 percent until it holds 20 percent of cumulative profit. Let x be the catch-up: x = 0.20 × (469.3 + x), so x = 0.20 × 469.3 / 0.80 = $117.3m to GP.

Step 4: Remaining distributions = 1,900 − 1,000 − 469.3 − 117.3 = $313.4m, split 80/20: LPs $250.7m, GP $62.7m.

Step 5: Totals: LPs = 1,000 + 469.3 + 250.7 = $1,720.0m; GP carry = 117.3 + 62.7 = $180.0m = 20 percent of the $900m profit.

The gross multiple is 1.90x; the LP net multiple (before management fees) is 1.72x. Including a 2 percent annual fee on $1,000m would reduce LP net proceeds by several hundred basis points of IRR over the life of the fund. The structure creates incentive effects: GPs hold a call option on fund profits, which can encourage risk-taking, since the downside to the GP is limited to lost fees, while the upside includes 20 percent of gains. LPs mitigate with hurdles, clawbacks (return of excess carry if later investments lose money), co-investment rights and key-person clauses.

### 23.4.4 Exit routes

Sponsors exit by (1) a trade sale to a strategic buyer, which can pay for synergies, (2) a secondary buyout to another sponsor, (3) an initial public offering, often with a staged sell-down because sponsors usually cannot sell their entire stake at the IPO, and (4) a recapitalization or continuation vehicle, in which a GP transfers an asset into a new vehicle for existing LPs to either cash out or roll over. Each route has a different valuation anchor and different execution risk; exit multiples are the most volatile input in the model.

## 23.5 Financial Distress and Bankruptcy

### 23.5.1 Distress, default and failure are not the same

Financial distress occurs when a firm cannot meet its obligations, or can meet them only by sacrificing the business. A firm can be economically viable, with its going-concern value exceeding liquidation value, yet financially distressed because its capital structure is too heavy. Conversely, an economically failed firm, with going-concern value below liquidation value, should be wound up even if its debt is small. Good restructuring practice separates the two questions: is the business worth more alive than dead (the operational question) and who bears the loss (the financial question)?

Distress produces direct costs (legal, advisory and administrative fees, which often total several percent of asset value for large cases, and more for complex ones) and indirect costs that are usually larger: customers who defect because they doubt warranty support, suppliers who demand cash in advance, key employees who leave, and managers who skip positive-NPV investments because the gains would go to creditors (debt overhang, per Myers). Equity holders in distress also have incentives to take excessive risk (asset substitution), since their payoff is an option on firm value, and to avoid raising new equity.

### 23.5.2 Early-warning indicators

Practitioners track:

```
Interest coverage    = EBITDA / Cash interest
Fixed charge cover   = (EBITDA − Capex) / (Interest + Scheduled principal)
Leverage             = Total debt / EBITDA
Liquidity runway     = (Cash + Undrawn committed facilities) / Monthly cash burn
```

Typical warning thresholds are coverage below 1.5 times, leverage above 6 times for cyclical businesses, and less than twelve months of runway. In Marlow's case, if capex is 4 percent of revenue (about $21.6m in year 1) the year-1 ratio of (EBITDA − capex) / interest is (108.0 − 21.6) / 52.5 = 1.65x, not far above the warning zone.

The Altman Z-score, a multivariate discriminant model for public manufacturers, combines five ratios:

```
Z = 1.2·(Working capital / Total assets) + 1.4·(Retained earnings / Total assets)
  + 3.3·(EBIT / Total assets) + 0.6·(Market value of equity / Total liabilities)
  + 1.0·(Sales / Total assets)
```

Scores below about 1.81 indicate high distress risk, scores above about 2.99 indicate safety, and the range between is the grey zone. Variants exist for private firms and non-manufacturers. The score is a screening aid, not a verdict, and its coefficients reflect the data and period on which it was estimated.

**Example 23.6 – Altman Z for a stressed manufacturer.** Harlan Metals (fictional) reports total assets $800m, working capital $40m, retained earnings $60m, EBIT $48m, market value of equity $150m, total liabilities $620m and sales $900m.

- X1 = 40 / 800 = 0.050; 1.2 × 0.050 = 0.060.
- X2 = 60 / 800 = 0.075; 1.4 × 0.075 = 0.105.
- X3 = 48 / 800 = 0.060; 3.3 × 0.060 = 0.198.
- X4 = 150 / 620 = 0.2419; 0.6 × 0.2419 = 0.145.
- X5 = 900 / 800 = 1.125; 1.0 × 1.125 = 1.125.
- Z = 0.060 + 0.105 + 0.198 + 0.145 + 1.125 = 1.633.

The score is below 1.81, so Harlan lies in the distress zone, although its relatively high asset turnover contributes most of the score. The warning is real but not decisive; the next step is a liquidity forecast and an assessment of whether the business or the balance sheet is broken.

### 23.5.3 Out-of-court workouts

Before filing, firms and creditors negotiate: maturity extensions, covenant waivers, interest deferrals, debt-for-equity swaps, exchange offers (new securities in exchange for old, often at a discount), and sale of assets. Workouts are faster and cheaper than court processes, but they face a holdout problem: each creditor prefers that others make concessions, and under bond indentures a unanimous consent is often required to change payment terms. Distressed exchanges therefore often include exit consents that strip covenants from holdouts' bonds, or are combined with a prepackaged court filing in which creditors vote before filing to bind dissenters.

## 23.6 Bankruptcy: Liquidation and Reorganization

### 23.6.1 The legal frameworks

Insolvency regimes differ by country, but the common structure involves two paths. Liquidation (Chapter 7 in the United States) sells assets, and a trustee distributes proceeds to claimants. Reorganization (Chapter 11 in the United States) keeps the business operating under court supervision while a plan is negotiated; an automatic stay prevents creditors from seizing assets, the debtor typically continues in possession of its business, and it may obtain debtor-in-possession (DIP) financing that is given priority over existing debt. Other jurisdictions use administration procedures, schemes of arrangement or safeguard proceedings, with different balances of creditor and debtor power. Cross-border cases often hinge on where the company's centre of main interests lies. This chapter uses the US vocabulary for illustration and does not provide legal advice.

### 23.6.2 The absolute priority rule

Claims are paid in order of legal priority: secured creditors from their collateral, then administrative expenses (including professional fees and DIP loans), priority claims (certain wages and taxes), general unsecured creditors, subordinated creditors, preferred equity and finally common equity. Under the absolute priority rule (APR), a junior class receives nothing until all senior classes are paid in full. In practice, negotiated plans often deviate: junior holders may receive small recoveries ("gifts" or warrants) in exchange for consent, and a plan can be confirmed over a dissenting class if it is "fair and equitable" (the cramdown standard) and at least one impaired class accepts.

**Example 23.7 – A liquidation recovery waterfall.** Harlan Metals files for liquidation. Net proceeds from asset sales are $540m, shared as follows: the secured term loan has a $400m claim on collateral worth enough to cover it; administrative and priority claims total $40m; general unsecured claims are $300m of notes and $100m of trade debt (pari passu); subordinated debentures are $150m; equity is last.

Step 1: Secured creditors: take $400m from the proceeds. Recovery = 100 percent. Remaining = 540 − 400 = $140m.

Step 2: Administrative and priority claims: $40m, paid in full. Remaining = $100m.

Step 3: General unsecured: total claims = 300 + 100 = $400m. Available $100m. Recovery rate = 100 / 400 = 25 percent. Notes receive 25% × 300 = $75m; trade creditors receive 25% × 100 = $25m.

Step 4: Subordinated debentures and equity: nothing remaining. Recovery 0 percent.

| Class | Claim ($m) | Recovery ($m) | Recovery rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Secured term loan | 400 | 400 | 100% |
| Administrative and priority | 40 | 40 | 100% |
| Senior unsecured notes | 300 | 75 | 25% |
| Trade creditors | 100 | 25 | 25% |
| Subordinated debentures | 150 | 0 | 0% |
| Equity | n/a | 0 | 0% |
| **Total distributed** | | **540** | |

In practice, administrative costs of the process reduce the distributable value further, and secured creditors often hold less than full collateral value, so recovery rates are typically lower and more uncertain than simple waterfalls suggest. Note that structural subordination also matters: creditors of an operating subsidiary are paid from that subsidiary's assets before a holding company's creditors share in the residual equity value.

### 23.6.3 The plan of reorganization and the fulcrum security

A reorganization plan must determine the going-concern value of the reorganized business, the new capital structure it can bear, and how claims are exchanged for new debt, equity or cash. The **fulcrum security** is the most senior class that is not paid in full; it is the class that, under the absolute priority rule, receives the equity of the reorganized company. Junior classes are out of the money, and senior classes are unimpaired or receive new debt equal to their claims. Valuation is therefore the battleground: a higher enterprise value moves the fulcrum down the capital structure and benefits junior creditors; a lower value moves it up and benefits the senior creditors who take the equity.

**Example 23.8 – Finding the fulcrum and allocating a plan.** Corvane Retail (fictional) emerges from Chapter 11 with a negotiated enterprise value of $540m. Its claims are first-lien term loan $450m, second-lien notes $200m, and general unsecured $300m.

Step 1: First lien: claim $450m versus EV $540m. It is covered; the residual after first lien = 540 − 450 = $90m.

Step 2: Second lien: claim $200m versus residual $90m. Recovery under APR = 90 / 200 = 45 percent. The second lien is the fulcrum security: it is the first class not paid in full.

Step 3: General unsecured and equity: residual after second lien is 0, so recovery is zero under strict APR.

Step 4: Plan design: first lien is reinstated (amended, with maturity extension) at $450m. New equity is valued at $90m. Second-lien holders receive 95 percent of new equity (value $85.5m, recovery 42.8 percent of their $200m claim), and unsecured creditors receive 5 percent of new equity as a settlement payment ($4.5m, recovery 1.5 percent of $300m) to secure their acceptance and avoid a costly valuation trial. That is a deviation from strict APR, which the second lien accepts as cheaper than litigation.

| Class | Claim | Treatment | Value received | Recovery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First-lien term loan | 450 | Reinstated | 450.0 | 100% |
| Second-lien notes | 200 | 95% of new equity | 85.5 | 42.8% |
| General unsecured | 300 | 5% of new equity | 4.5 | 1.5% |
| Old equity | n/a | Cancelled | 0 | 0% |

If a rival valuation puts enterprise value at $620m, the first lien still recovers 100 percent, the second lien equity pool becomes 170 and its recovery 85 percent. A $80m (15 percent) valuation difference moves the second lien's recovery from 45 to 85 percent, which shows why valuation experts and discount rates become the center of the dispute. For junior claimants, valuation is an option: the lower the value the less the junior class gets, but the higher the volatility the more their out-of-the-money claim is worth (warrants and contingent value rights are used to bridge differences in view).

### 23.6.4 Restructuring beyond the balance sheet

Financial restructuring alone rarely saves a business. Successful turnarounds combine four elements: stabilizing liquidity (13-week cash flow forecasting, DIP financing, working capital discipline), fixing the operating model (closing loss-making sites, renegotiating leases and contracts, repricing), resolving the capital structure (debt-for-equity swaps, maturity extension, haircut) and rebuilding stakeholder confidence (a credible business plan, new management or a chief restructuring officer). A "amend and extend" can buy time, but if the underlying business is impaired it merely delays a filing. Sponsors in distress face a decision: inject additional equity to protect their existing investment (an option on recovery), hand over the keys to lenders, or negotiate partial support. Rational sponsors compare the option value of additional equity to the probability-weighted gain; sunk costs are irrelevant.

## 23.7 Spin-Offs, Carve-Outs and Divestitures

### 23.7.1 Forms of separation

Companies can reduce their scope through several mechanisms, each with different cash, control and tax consequences.

| Form | What happens | Cash to parent | Control after | Typical purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Divestiture (sale) | Business sold to a strategic or financial buyer | Yes (sale price) | None | Raise cash, exit non-core |
| Spin-off | Subsidiary shares distributed pro rata to parent shareholders | No | Independent public company | Unlock focus, remove conglomerate discount |
| Split-off | Parent shareholders exchange parent shares for subsidiary shares | No (buyback effect) | Independent | Tax-efficient reduction of parent share count |
| Equity carve-out (IPO) | Minority stake of the subsidiary sold to public | Yes (IPO proceeds) | Parent keeps majority and consolidates | Raise capital, create currency and market price |
| Tracking stock | Parent issues shares linked to a division's performance | Possibly | Parent retains legal ownership | Separate valuation without legal separation |

Tax treatment is crucial. In many jurisdictions, a spin-off that satisfies tests (active business, continuity of ownership, valid business purpose, no device to distribute earnings) can be tax-free for the company and shareholders, whereas a taxable sale generates a capital gain. These rules are technical and vary by jurisdiction; the managerial point is that the tax cost of separation can offset the strategic benefit and must be modeled.

### 23.7.2 Why separation might create value

The case for separation rests on several arguments.

- **Focus and managerial incentives:** a stand-alone company can tie executive pay to its own stock price rather than to a diversified average.
- **Information and valuation:** analysts specialized in one industry can value a pure-play more accurately; opaque conglomerates trade at a "conglomerate discount" if investors cannot value divisions or fear cross-subsidization.
- **Capital allocation:** the corporate center may starve high-growth units to fund legacy ones. Separation lets each unit raise capital at a cost reflecting its own risk.
- **Investor clientele:** a high-growth division and a high-dividend utility attract different shareholders.
- **Takeover readiness:** a stand-alone unit is easier for a bidder to acquire.

Against these, separation destroys any real synergies (shared purchasing, brand, distribution, tax capacity and the internal capital market), creates dis-synergies (duplicated corporate costs, loss of diversification that reduces debt capacity), and incurs one-time costs (legal, systems separation, rebranding). The conglomerate discount is also a measurement problem: part of the observed discount reflects the fact that firms diversify when their original industries are weak, not that diversification itself destroys value. The honest test is a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) analysis net of costs.

### 23.7.3 Sum-of-the-parts valuation

SOTP values each segment at the multiple of comparable pure-play companies, capitalizes unallocated corporate costs, and subtracts consolidated net debt:

```
Equity value = Σ (Segment EBITDA × Peer multiple) − (Corporate costs × Multiple) − Net debt
```

**Example 23.9 – SOTP and the net value of a spin-off.** Stratus Holdings (fictional) has an Industrial division with EBITDA $200m (peer multiple 8.0x), a Consumer division with EBITDA $120m (peer multiple 11.0x), unallocated corporate costs of $30m capitalized at 8.0x, net debt of $900m and 100 million shares trading at $14.50.

Step 1: Industrial = 200 × 8 = $1,600m. Consumer = 120 × 11 = $1,320m. Corporate = −30 × 8 = −$240m.

Step 2: SOTP enterprise value = 1,600 + 1,320 − 240 = $2,680m. Equity value = 2,680 − 900 = $1,780m, or $17.80 per share.

Step 3: Current market EV = 14.50 × 100 + 900 = $2,350m, an implied multiple of 2,350 / 290 = 8.1x on combined EBITDA net of corporate costs (200 + 120 − 30 = 290). The conglomerate discount = (2,680 − 2,350) / 2,680 = 12.3 percent.

Step 4: Adjust for separation. A spin-off duplicates corporate functions, adding $15m of annual recurring costs (capitalized at 8.0x = $120m) and costs $60m one time. Net EV = 2,680 − 120 − 60 = $2,500m. Equity = 2,500 − 900 = $1,600m, or $16.00 per share.

Step 5: Value creation relative to market = 16.00 / 14.50 − 1 = 10.3 percent, or $150m in total, if investors re-rate each part to peer multiples. This depends on the key assumption that peer multiples apply. If the Consumer division would trade at 10x instead of 11x, its value falls by $120m and the net gain shrinks from $150m to $30m, which is within the uncertainty of the estimate. The decision should therefore hinge on credible evidence the discount is structural and removable.

### 23.7.4 Equity carve-outs

An equity carve-out sells a minority stake in a subsidiary through an IPO. It generates cash (primary proceeds to the subsidiary or secondary proceeds to the parent), establishes a market price that can be used to incentivize managers, and often serves as a stepping stone to a full spin-off or sale. Carve-outs often involve underpricing of 10 to 15 percent, which transfers value to the new investors, and create agency issues because the parent controls a company with minority public holders (related-party transactions, transfer pricing). Control and consolidation remain with the parent when it keeps more than 50 percent.

**Example 23.10 – Pricing a carve-out.** Stratus carves out 20 percent of Consumer. The division's EV is $1,320m (11x) and $300m of Stratus's net debt is pushed down to it. The IPO is priced at a 10 percent discount to fair value.

Step 1: Consumer fair equity value = 1,320 − 300 = $1,020m.

Step 2: 20 percent stake fair value = 0.20 × 1,020 = $204.0m.

Step 3: IPO proceeds at a 10 percent discount = 204.0 × 0.90 = $183.6m. The underpricing cost to Stratus shareholders is 204.0 − 183.6 = $20.4m. If the proceeds are used to repay debt, Stratus net debt falls to 900 − 183.6 = $716.4m (consolidated). The parent still consolidates 100 percent of Consumer's EBITDA but 20 percent of its equity value is now held by outside investors.

A carve-out thus raises funding at a cost of 10 percent of the amount sold, which compares with the cost of an equivalent equity issue at the parent. It makes sense when the subsidiary's valuation is expected to exceed that implied within the parent, or when it is a precursor to complete separation.

## Case Study: Halden Foods and the Carved-Up Conglomerate

*(Fictional company and figures, for teaching purposes only.)*

Halden Foods is a diversified food group with three divisions: Ambient Grocery (shelf-stable products), Frozen & Chilled, and Foodservice Distribution. Combined revenue is $3.2 billion and EBITDA is $400 million (12.5 percent margin). Net debt is $1.6 billion, or 4.0 times EBITDA, a legacy of a leveraged recapitalization five years ago in which Halden's controlling shareholder, Brightwater Partners, a private equity firm, extracted a $500 million dividend. Brightwater holds 62 percent of the shares and the rest is publicly traded at 7.5 times EBITDA, below the 9.5 times average multiple of branded grocery peers and the 8.0 times of distributors.

Divisional EBITDA is: Ambient $210 million, Frozen & Chilled $120 million, Foodservice $110 million, less unallocated corporate costs of $40 million. Foodservice has been losing contracts to a larger rival, and a 12 percent margin decline in the last year pushed Halden's total interest coverage to 2.4 times. Its senior secured term loan carries a maximum leverage covenant of 4.5 times, so EBITDA can fall only to about $356 million, a cushion of roughly $44 million, before the covenant is breached. The $700 million of senior unsecured notes mature in 26 months, and refinancing them at current market yields would add roughly $25 million to annual interest.

The board is considering three options. Option A is to sell Foodservice to a strategic buyer, which has indicated a price of 7.0 times divisional EBITDA, and use the proceeds to repay the term loan. Option B is to spin off Ambient Grocery, the highest-quality business, as a separately listed company with a lower debt level of 2.5 times, leaving Frozen & Chilled and Foodservice together with the remainder of the debt. Option C is a standstill: operate as is, cut costs by $30 million, and refinance the notes at current rates.

Brightwater's fund is in year seven of a ten-year life and wants liquidity within three years. The CFO worries that option B leaves the remaining company too levered, and that the notes' covenants may require note holders' consent to the spin-off because it removes the largest asset pool. The treasury team estimates one-time separation costs of $90 million for the spin-off and $35 million in transaction costs for the sale, and annual dis-synergies of $20 million from duplicated corporate functions in option B.

Employees, suppliers and the controlling shareholder have competing interests. Ambient's managers want a listing so equity-based pay tracks their own performance. Foodservice's customers fear a buyer will rationalize product lines. The lenders prefer option A, which delevers directly. Minority shareholders suspect that Brightwater will favor the structure that gets its fund the earliest liquidity.

**Discussion questions**

1. Compute a sum-of-the-parts valuation for Halden using the multiples given (Ambient at 9.5x, Frozen & Chilled at 9.0x as an assumed peer multiple, Foodservice at 7.0x, corporate costs capitalized at 8.0x) and compare it with the market-implied enterprise value. How large is the apparent conglomerate discount, and how much of it could be explained by the leverage and the Foodservice problem rather than by opacity?
2. For option A, calculate pro forma leverage after using the sale proceeds to repay debt, and for option B, estimate the leverage of the remaining company after the Ambient spin-off with 2.5 times leverage on its EBITDA. Which structure leaves the more stable credit profile? Consider the covenant, the note maturity and the ability to refinance.
3. Brightwater's incentives differ from those of minority holders and lenders. Identify the conflicts of interest and recommend governance safeguards (independent committee, fairness opinion, minority approval) for whichever option the board chooses.
4. What early-warning indicators should Halden's treasury monitor, and what would trigger a pre-emptive restructuring discussion with lenders? Should the board consider a distressed exchange of the notes?

## Key Takeaways

- An LBO finances a purchase mainly with debt serviced by the target's own cash flow; it works best for stable, cash-generative, asset-rich businesses with identifiable improvements and clear exits.
- The sources-and-uses table anchors every deal: sponsor equity is the plug after debt and fees, and fees are real value leakage.
- Debt paydown transfers enterprise value from creditors to equity holders, but early-year free cash flow is thin, so downside stress tests and interest hedging matter more than the base case.
- Sponsor returns decompose into EBITDA growth, multiple change and net debt reduction, less fees; a return that depends on multiple expansion is a market bet, not a business plan.
- IRR falls as the holding period lengthens even as MOIC rises, which explains the appeal of early exits and dividend recapitalizations; a recap raises IRR but increases leverage and risk.
- Fund economics (management fee, hurdle, catch-up, carry) create a call-option payoff for the GP; net LP returns are meaningfully below gross deal returns.
- Distress is a financial problem, economic failure is an operating problem; restructuring succeeds only when the going-concern value exceeds liquidation value and both the balance sheet and the business are fixed.
- Under the absolute priority rule, the fulcrum security is the first class not paid in full; valuation disputes decide how far down the capital structure value reaches.
- Spin-offs and carve-outs create value only when the discount to a sum-of-the-parts is structural and exceeds the dis-synergies, tax and separation costs.
- Delay is costly in distress and in separation: early, credible negotiation with creditors and a clear strategic rationale preserve value.
