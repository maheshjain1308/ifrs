## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario. Helix Devices is a fictional company.*

Helix Devices designs connected medical monitors and sells them to hospital systems. For three straight years it reported rising profits. Revenue grew 22% in the latest year, net income grew 31%, and the chief executive told analysts that Helix had "turned the corner." The company's lender, a regional bank, was about to renew a $60 million revolving credit facility, and the relationship manager asked a new credit analyst, Priya, to prepare a one-page recommendation.

Priya did not start with the income statement. She began with the cash flow statement and found that operating cash flow had been negative for two years even though net income was strongly positive. She then moved to the balance sheet. Accounts receivable had grown 64% while revenue grew 22%. Days sales outstanding had risen from 58 to 91. Inventory had risen faster than sales, and the company had begun to extend supplier payments from 45 to 70 days. Capital expenditure had been financed by new borrowing. The footnotes revealed that Helix had begun offering hospitals extended payment terms, and that a growing share of revenue was recognised at shipment under distributor arrangements that allowed returns.

None of this proved wrongdoing. It might simply mean a growth company stretching to win accounts. But the profit figure that had impressed investors told only part of the story. The firm was consuming cash faster than it earned it, its liquidity depended on suppliers' patience, and its reported earnings depended on judgements about collectibility and returns that were open to challenge.

Priya recommended renewing the facility with a smaller limit, tighter covenants on receivables and a requirement for quarterly cash flow reporting. The line was one she would repeat to her colleagues: "Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact."

Financial statements are the common language of management, lenders, investors and regulators. This chapter teaches you to read them as Priya did: to connect the statements, to extract cash flows, to compute ratios that summarise performance and risk, and to ask whether the numbers deserve to be trusted.

## 2.1 The Financial Statements and How They Fit Together

### 2.1.1 Purpose and framework

Financial statements summarise the economic activity of a firm in a standard format so that outsiders can compare it with other firms and with itself over time. Listed firms in most of the world prepare them under International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), and in the United States under US generally accepted accounting principles (US GAAP). The two frameworks differ in detail but share the same architecture: accrual accounting, going-concern assumption, a historical-cost orientation with selective use of fair value, and a set of qualitative characteristics (relevance, faithful representation, comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability).

Accrual accounting is the central idea. Revenue is recognised when it is earned, and expenses when the resources are consumed to earn it, irrespective of when cash changes hands. This gives a better measure of performance than cash receipts and payments over short periods, but it introduces estimates and judgement, and therefore the possibility of bias.

A complete set of annual statements has four primary components and accompanying notes:

1. The **balance sheet** (statement of financial position): a snapshot of assets, liabilities and equity at a date.
2. The **income statement** (statement of profit or loss): revenues, expenses and profit over a period.
3. The **cash flow statement**: cash inflows and outflows over a period, classified as operating, investing and financing.
4. The **statement of changes in equity**: the reconciliation of opening to closing equity through profit, dividends, share issues and other items.

The **notes** disclose accounting policies, details of major items, commitments and contingencies, segment data and related-party transactions. Many of the most useful analytical facts are found in the notes, not on the face of the statements.

### 2.1.2 The balance sheet

The balance sheet rests on the accounting identity:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' Equity
```

**Assets** are resources controlled by the firm from which future economic benefits are expected. They are typically listed in order of liquidity or presented as current (expected to be converted to cash or used within a year or the operating cycle) and non-current. Current assets include cash, receivables, inventory and prepaid expenses. Non-current assets include property, plant and equipment (PP&E) net of accumulated depreciation, intangible assets and goodwill, long-term investments and deferred tax assets.

**Liabilities** are present obligations to transfer economic resources. Current liabilities include trade payables, accrued expenses, short-term debt and the current portion of long-term debt. Non-current liabilities include long-term debt, lease liabilities, pension obligations and deferred taxes.

**Equity** is the residual: assets minus liabilities. It comprises contributed capital (share capital and premium), retained earnings and other reserves. Book equity is a historical accounting number and can differ markedly from market capitalisation, because the balance sheet omits many valuable assets, such as brands, internally generated technology and human capital, and reports others at depreciated historical cost.

Several measurement points matter for analysis. Inventory may be valued at cost using FIFO or weighted average (LIFO is permitted under US GAAP but not IFRS), which affects margins during inflation. PP&E is depreciated over estimated lives; longer lives defer expense. Goodwill, created in acquisitions, is tested for impairment, and impairments tend to be recognised late. Under current lease standards, most leases appear on the balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, which makes lease-heavy firms look more leveraged than they once did and requires care when comparing firms across periods or between standards.

### 2.1.3 The income statement

The income statement reports financial performance over a period. A typical layout, together with the intermediate measures analysts use, is:

```
Revenue
− Cost of goods sold (COGS)
= Gross profit
− Selling, general and administrative expenses (SG&A)
− Research and development
− Depreciation and amortisation (if shown separately)
= Operating profit (EBIT)
− Interest expense
= Profit before tax (EBT)
− Income tax expense
= Net income
```

**EBITDA** (earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation) equals EBIT plus depreciation and amortisation. It is widely used as a rough proxy for operating cash generation and as the denominator in leverage ratios, but it ignores capital expenditure, working-capital investment and taxes. A business that must continually replace expensive equipment can have a high EBITDA and little free cash.

**Net income** is the profit attributable to the owners after all expenses including interest and tax. **Earnings per share (EPS)** is net income less preferred dividends, divided by the weighted-average shares outstanding; diluted EPS also counts options, convertibles and other potentially dilutive securities.

Some income statements classify expenses by function (as above), others by nature (materials, personnel, depreciation). Items outside normal operations, such as restructuring charges, impairments and gains on disposal, require judgement: are they truly non-recurring? A firm that reports "one-off" charges every year should be analysed as if they were recurring.

### 2.1.4 The cash flow statement

The cash flow statement reconciles the opening and closing cash balance by activity.

- **Operating activities:** cash generated by the firm's principal revenue-producing activities. It starts with net income and adjusts for non-cash items and working-capital changes (the indirect method) or lists cash receipts and payments directly (the direct method).
- **Investing activities:** purchases and sales of long-term assets, acquisitions and divestitures, and investments in securities.
- **Financing activities:** issuance and repayment of debt, issuance and repurchase of shares, and dividends. (Under IFRS, interest paid and dividends paid may be classified in operating or financing, subject to consistent policy; US GAAP places interest paid in operating activities.)

The cash flow statement is harder to manipulate than the income statement, although classification choices exist. Analysts use it to answer three questions. Does the business generate cash from its operations? Is it funding growth internally or by raising capital? Is it returning cash to owners from genuine surplus or from borrowing?

### 2.1.5 The statement of changes in equity and the articulation of the statements

Equity movements link the income statement to the balance sheet:

```
Closing retained earnings = Opening retained earnings + Net income − Dividends
```

(with similar lines for share issues, repurchases and other comprehensive income). The statements **articulate**: net income flows into retained earnings; the cash flow statement explains the change in the cash line of the balance sheet; the changes in other balance sheet accounts explain the adjustments in the operating, investing and financing sections. Learning to trace these connections is the best protection against error and manipulation, because an inconsistency in one statement shows up in another.

## 2.2 Cash Flows: From Profit to Free Cash Flow

### 2.2.1 The indirect method

Operating cash flow (CFO) begins with net income and applies two sets of adjustments. First, add back non-cash expenses such as depreciation and amortisation (they reduced profit but not cash). Second, adjust for changes in operating working capital: an increase in an operating asset (receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses) uses cash, whereas an increase in an operating liability (payables, accruals) provides cash.

```
CFO = Net income + D&A + other non-cash charges
      − Increase in receivables − Increase in inventory − Increase in prepaids
      + Increase in payables + Increase in accrued liabilities
```

The logic is intuitive. If Calder sells goods on credit, revenue and profit rise, but the cash arrives only when the customer pays; the increase in receivables is therefore a deduction. If Calder delays paying a supplier, expense is recorded but cash is retained; the increase in payables is an addition.

**Example 2.1 – Operating cash flow for Calder Components.** Using the Year 2 and Year 1 balance sheets:

*Step 1 – Start with net income:* 198.

*Step 2 – Add back depreciation and amortisation:* +96. Subtotal 294.

*Step 3 – Working-capital changes:*
- Receivables: 360 − 305 = +55 increase → −55
- Inventory: 330 − 270 = +60 increase → −60
- Prepaid expenses: 30 − 25 = +5 increase → −5
- Accounts payable: 240 − 210 = +30 increase → +30
- Accrued liabilities: 120 − 100 = +20 increase → +20

Net working-capital effect = −55 − 60 − 5 + 30 + 20 = **−70**.

*Step 4 – CFO* = 294 − 70 = **224**.

*Step 5 – Investing.* PP&E rose from 1,000 to 1,100. Net PP&E = opening + capital expenditure − depreciation, so capex = 1,100 − 1,000 + 96 = **196**. Goodwill and intangibles were unchanged. CFI = **−196**.

*Step 6 – Financing.* Short-term debt rose 10 and long-term debt rose 40, giving net borrowing of +50; dividends were −48. CFF = 50 − 48 = **+2**.

*Step 7 – Net change in cash.* 224 − 196 + 2 = **+30**, which equals the change in the balance sheet cash line (180 − 150 = 30). The statements articulate.

| Calder Components – Cash flow statement, Year 2 | $m |
|---|---|
| Net income | 198 |
| Depreciation and amortisation | 96 |
| Increase in receivables | (55) |
| Increase in inventory | (60) |
| Increase in prepaid expenses | (5) |
| Increase in payables | 30 |
| Increase in accrued liabilities | 20 |
| **Cash flow from operations** | **224** |
| Capital expenditure | (196) |
| **Cash flow from investing** | **(196)** |
| Net borrowing | 50 |
| Dividends | (48) |
| **Cash flow from financing** | **2** |
| **Net increase in cash** | **30** |

*Interpretation.* Calder converted 224 / 198 = 113% of net income into operating cash flow, a healthy sign, but it also spent 196 on capex, nearly twice its depreciation (196 / 96 = 2.04), so internally generated cash after investment was only 28. The dividends of 48 and part of the growth were financed with debt. This is a growth firm investing ahead of sales, a pattern revisited in Section 2.7.

### 2.2.2 Why free cash flow matters

Net income and even CFO are not the amounts available to investors, since the firm must reinvest in fixed assets and working capital to sustain its operations. **Free cash flow (FCF)** is the cash generated after those reinvestments. It is the amount that can be paid to the providers of capital without impairing the business, and it is the quantity that discounted-cash-flow valuation (developed in later chapters) discounts.

Two versions are used.

**Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF)**, also called unlevered free cash flow, is the cash flow available to all capital providers, debt and equity, before any financing flows:

```
FCFF = EBIT × (1 − t) + D&A − Capital expenditure − Increase in operating working capital
     = NOPAT + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC
```

Equivalent expressions use CFO: `FCFF = CFO + Interest × (1 − t) − Capex`. FCFF is independent of capital structure, because it is computed before interest and uses the tax charge on operating income (the tax shield on interest is accounted for in the discount rate, via the after-tax cost of debt).

**Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)** is the cash flow available to the shareholders after meeting all debt obligations:

```
FCFE = FCFF − Interest × (1 − t) + Net borrowing
     = CFO − Capex + Net borrowing
```

Operating working capital here includes only operating items (receivables, inventory, prepaids, less payables and accruals); cash and interest-bearing debt are excluded, because they are financing items.

**Example 2.2 – FCFF and FCFE for Calder Components.**

*Step 1 – NOPAT.* EBIT × (1 − t) = 312 × 0.75 = **234.0**.

*Step 2 – Change in operating working capital.* Increases in receivables (55), inventory (60) and prepaids (5) less increases in payables (30) and accruals (20): 55 + 60 + 5 − 30 − 20 = **70**.

*Step 3 – FCFF.* 234 + 96 − 196 − 70 = **64**.

*Step 4 – Cross-check using CFO.* Interest after tax = 48 × 0.75 = 36. FCFF = 224 + 36 − 196 = **64**. ✓

*Step 5 – FCFE.* FCFF − after-tax interest + net borrowing = 64 − 36 + 50 = **78**. Cross-check: CFO − capex + net borrowing = 224 − 196 + 50 = **78**. ✓

*Step 6 – Use of FCFE.* Dividends were 48, so 78 − 48 = 30 was added to cash, matching the change in the cash balance.

*Interpretation.* Despite NOPAT of 234, the firm's FCFF is only 64, because it invested 196 in fixed assets and 70 in working capital. Whether this is good or bad depends on what the investment buys. If the new capacity earns returns above the cost of capital, then low current FCFF is the price of future value. If not, it is a warning. Note also that FCFE of 78 includes 50 of borrowing; without new debt, Calder's cash after investment would have been 28, short of its dividend of 48.

A useful way of thinking about free cash flow is as the reconciliation of the *earnings* a firm reports with the *cash* it can distribute. Firms with high EBITDA and high capital intensity (telecommunications, utilities, airlines) often have low FCF; firms with low capital needs (software, consulting) often convert most of their profit to cash.

### 2.2.3 Common mistakes in cash-flow analysis

- **Using EBITDA as "cash flow."** EBITDA omits capex, working capital and taxes.
- **Ignoring capex versus depreciation.** Capex persistently below depreciation means that the asset base is being consumed; persistently above depreciation signals growth (or inflation of asset costs).
- **Treating borrowing as operating performance.** A firm reporting positive total cash flow may be funding operations with debt.
- **Mismatching FCFF with equity measures.** FCFF is discounted at the WACC to give enterprise value; FCFE is discounted at the cost of equity to give equity value. Mixing them leads to double counting of debt.
- **Forgetting that one year can mislead.** Working-capital swings and lumpy capex distort single-year FCF; analysts typically examine three to five years and normalise.

## 2.3 Common-Size and Trend Analysis

Ratios compress information; common-size and trend statements preserve the shape of the statements while making firms and years comparable.

### 2.3.1 Common-size statements

A **common-size income statement** expresses every line as a percentage of revenue; a **common-size balance sheet** expresses every line as a percentage of total assets. This removes the effect of scale, so that a small firm can be compared with a large one in the same industry, and it highlights structural change over time.

**Example 2.3 – Common-size analysis of Calder.** Dividing each line of the income statement by revenue:

| Line (% of revenue) | Year 2 | Year 1 | Change (points) |
|---|---|---|---|
| COGS | 63.0% | 65.0% | −2.0 |
| Gross profit | 37.0% | 35.0% | +2.0 |
| SG&A | 17.5% | 18.0% | −0.5 |
| R&D | 2.5% | 2.4% | +0.1 |
| D&A | 4.0% | 4.0% | 0.0 |
| EBIT | 13.0% | 10.6% | +2.4 |
| Interest | 2.0% | 1.9% | +0.1 |
| Net income | 8.25% | 6.5% | +1.7 |

*Step 1 – Calculations.* Year 2: COGS 1,512 / 2,400 = 63.0%; SG&A 420 / 2,400 = 17.5%; EBIT 312 / 2,400 = 13.0%; net income 198 / 2,400 = 8.25%. Year 1: COGS 1,430 / 2,200 = 65.0%; SG&A 396 / 2,200 = 18.0%; EBIT 234 / 2,200 = 10.6%; net income 144 / 2,200 = 6.5%.

*Step 2 – Growth rates.* Revenue grew 2,400 / 2,200 − 1 = 9.1%. EBIT grew 312 / 234 − 1 = 33.3%, and net income grew 198 / 144 − 1 = 37.5%.

*Step 3 – Where did the improvement come from?* Of the 2.4-point rise in EBIT margin, 2.0 points came from gross margin (cheaper inputs, better pricing or more efficient production) and 0.5 points from SG&A leverage, partly offset by a 0.1-point increase in R&D. Profit grew about four times faster than sales, which is **operating leverage** at work: when a portion of costs is fixed, a modest increase in revenue lifts profit disproportionately. The degree of operating leverage here is 33.3% / 9.1% = **3.7**. The firm must investigate whether the gross-margin gain is sustainable (a one-time commodity price fall) or structural (a plant efficiency programme).

The common-size balance sheet reveals shifts in the structure of assets and financing:

| Line (% of total assets) | Year 2 | Year 1 |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | 7.8% | 7.3% |
| Receivables | 15.7% | 14.9% |
| Inventory | 14.3% | 13.2% |
| Total current assets | 39.1% | 36.6% |
| Net PP&E | 47.8% | 48.8% |
| Goodwill and intangibles | 13.0% | 14.6% |
| Current liabilities | 20.0% | 19.5% |
| Long-term debt | 26.1% | 27.3% |
| Equity | 53.9% | 53.2% |

*Interpretation.* Receivables grew 18% and inventory 22%, both faster than revenue (9.1%). The share of current assets in total assets rose by 2.5 points. This signals either a build to support growth or slower collections and slower-moving stock, a question the efficiency ratios in Section 2.4 will address. Financing is stable: equity still funds about 54% of assets, and long-term debt a little over a quarter.

### 2.3.2 Trend (index) analysis

Trend analysis expresses each line over several years as a percentage of a base year (base = 100). It shows which items grow faster or slower than others. If receivables index at 160 while revenue indexes at 130, receivables are rising faster than sales. A standard test of reasonableness is that receivables, inventory and payables should move broadly in line with sales unless there is a known reason (a change in credit policy, a new product with a longer cycle, a supplier negotiation). Differences between growth rates, rather than the rates themselves, are the signal.

### 2.3.3 Benchmarks

A ratio has meaning only relative to a benchmark. Three are customary: the firm's own history, the industry or peer group, and a theoretical or contractual standard (such as a covenant). Peer comparison is not straightforward: firms differ in accounting policies, business mix, geographic exposure and fiscal year-ends. Where possible, analysts adjust for differences (for example, converting leases consistently or reclassifying one-off items) before comparing.

## 2.4 Liquidity and Efficiency Ratios

### 2.4.1 Liquidity: can the firm pay its bills?

Liquidity ratios measure the firm's ability to meet short-term obligations from short-term resources.

```
Current ratio = Current assets / Current liabilities
Quick (acid-test) ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities + Receivables) / Current liabilities
Cash ratio = (Cash + Marketable securities) / Current liabilities
```

The current ratio counts inventory and prepaids, which may take a long time to convert to cash, or may be sold only at a discount. The quick ratio is more conservative. The cash ratio is the strictest. What counts as an adequate level depends on the industry: a supermarket that collects cash at the till and pays suppliers on 30-day terms may operate with a current ratio below 1, while a capital-goods manufacturer with long production cycles may need 2 or more. Very high liquidity is not unambiguously good; it may indicate idle cash or excessive inventory.

### 2.4.2 Efficiency (activity) ratios

Efficiency ratios measure how intensively the firm uses its assets, in particular the working capital that sits between buying inputs and collecting cash.

```
Receivables turnover = Revenue / Receivables             Days sales outstanding (DSO) = 365 / Receivables turnover
Inventory turnover = COGS / Inventory                      Days inventory outstanding (DIO) = 365 / Inventory turnover
Payables turnover = COGS / Payables                        Days payables outstanding (DPO) = 365 / Payables turnover
Cash conversion cycle (CCC) = DSO + DIO − DPO
```

The **cash conversion cycle** is the number of days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. A shorter cycle means less capital is tied up in working capital. A negative cycle (as in some retail and subscription businesses) means that customers and suppliers effectively finance the firm.

Where balances are seasonal or growing quickly, analysts use *average* balances rather than year-end, though year-end figures are acceptable if applied consistently. Credit sales should be used instead of total revenue where disclosed. Payables turnover is sometimes computed on purchases rather than COGS; if purchases are not disclosed, COGS is a reasonable approximation.

Broader asset-efficiency measures include **total asset turnover** (revenue / total assets) and **fixed asset turnover** (revenue / net PP&E).

**Example 2.4 – Liquidity and the cash conversion cycle for Calder.**

*Step 1 – Liquidity, Year 2.* Current ratio = 900 / 460 = **1.96**. Quick ratio = (180 + 360) / 460 = 540 / 460 = **1.17**. Cash ratio = 180 / 460 = **0.39**.

*Step 2 – Liquidity, Year 1.* Current ratio = 750 / 400 = **1.875**. Quick ratio = (150 + 305) / 400 = **1.14**. Cash ratio = 150 / 400 = **0.375**.

Liquidity is comfortable and slightly improved.

*Step 3 – Efficiency, Year 2 (year-end balances).*
- DSO = 360 / 2,400 × 365 = **54.8 days**
- DIO = 330 / 1,512 × 365 = **79.7 days**
- DPO = 240 / 1,512 × 365 = **57.9 days**
- CCC = 54.8 + 79.7 − 57.9 = **76.5 days**

*Step 4 – Efficiency, Year 1.*
- DSO = 305 / 2,200 × 365 = **50.6 days**
- DIO = 270 / 1,430 × 365 = **68.9 days**
- DPO = 210 / 1,430 × 365 = **53.6 days**
- CCC = 50.6 + 68.9 − 53.6 = **65.9 days**

*Step 5 – Interpretation.* The cycle lengthened by about 10.6 days. Customers took 4 more days to pay, inventory stayed on the shelf 11 more days, and the firm stretched suppliers by only 4 more days, which offset only a portion of the rest. The impact in cash terms: one day of sales is 2,400 / 365 = $6.58 million and one day of cost of goods is 1,512 / 365 = $4.14 million. A four-day slip in collections ties up roughly 4 × 6.58 = $26 million, and the eleven-day inventory build roughly 11 × 4.14 = $46 million.

The current ratio looks fine, yet the underlying drivers of the cycle are moving the wrong way; this is why one should examine the components of working capital, not only the aggregate liquidity ratio. Management should find out whether the longer cycle reflects deliberate strategy (higher safety stocks ahead of a product launch, more generous terms to win a big customer) or deteriorating discipline (slow-moving stock, rising disputes over invoices).

**Example 2.5 – Window dressing a liquidity ratio.** Calder's treasurer proposes using $100 million of cash on the last day of the year to repay payables ahead of schedule. The effect on the ratios, using the year-end figures from Example 2.4:

*Step 1 – Current ratio after the payment.* Current assets fall to 900 − 100 = 800; current liabilities fall to 460 − 100 = 360. New ratio = 800 / 360 = **2.22** (up from 1.96).

*Step 2 – Quick ratio after the payment.* Quick assets fall to 540 − 100 = 440; new ratio = 440 / 360 = **1.22** (up from 1.17).

*Step 3 – Reality.* No new resources have been created, and the firm has lost 100 of cash. It has less ability to meet unexpected needs, even though both ratios improved. This works because the starting ratios exceeded 1: removing equal amounts from numerator and denominator increases a ratio that is above one. The reverse is true for a ratio below one, where the same payment would worsen the ratio. Lenders know the trick, which is why covenants are often tested on average or quarterly figures, and why analysts examine cash flow, not merely ratios.

## 2.5 Leverage and Solvency Ratios

### 2.5.1 Why leverage matters

Leverage measures how much of the firm's financing is fixed-cost debt. Debt amplifies returns to shareholders when the firm earns more than its cost of debt, and magnifies losses when it does not. It also creates fixed obligations that, if unmet, trigger distress. Leverage ratios fall into two groups: **capital-structure ratios**, which compare debt with the capital base, and **coverage ratios**, which compare earnings or cash flow with the burden of servicing the debt.

### 2.5.2 Capital-structure ratios

```
Debt-to-equity = Total debt / Shareholders' equity
Debt-to-capital = Total debt / (Total debt + Equity)
Debt ratio = Total liabilities / Total assets
Equity multiplier = Total assets / Equity
Net debt = Total debt − Cash and marketable securities
Net debt / EBITDA
```

"Total debt" means interest-bearing obligations, including lease liabilities under IFRS 16 and similar US standards. Some analysts also add underfunded pensions and guarantees. Book values of equity may understate the market value of equity for profitable firms, so some analysts use market-value ratios as well.

### 2.5.3 Coverage ratios

```
Interest coverage (times interest earned) = EBIT / Interest expense
EBITDA coverage = EBITDA / Interest expense
Fixed-charge coverage = (EBIT + Lease payments) / (Interest + Lease payments)
Debt service coverage = Cash flow available for debt service / (Interest + Scheduled principal)
```

Coverage should be assessed under stress. A coverage ratio of 6 means that EBIT could fall by 83% before interest is uncovered; but for a cyclical business whose profit may fall by half in a downturn, the cushion matters more than the average. Bank covenants frequently specify a maximum Net debt / EBITDA and a minimum coverage ratio, and breaches give lenders the right to demand repayment or renegotiate terms.

**Example 2.6 – Leverage analysis of Calder.** Total debt in Year 2 is 100 + 600 = 700, and in Year 1 is 90 + 560 = 650. EBITDA in Year 2 is 312 + 96 = 408; in Year 1, 234 + 88 = 322.

*Step 1 – Capital structure, Year 2.*
- Debt-to-equity = 700 / 1,240 = **0.56**
- Debt-to-capital = 700 / (700 + 1,240) = 700 / 1,940 = **36.1%**
- Debt ratio = 1,060 / 2,300 = **46.1%**
- Equity multiplier = 2,300 / 1,240 = **1.85**
- Net debt = 700 − 180 = **520**; Net debt / EBITDA = 520 / 408 = **1.27×**

*Step 2 – Capital structure, Year 1.* Debt-to-equity = 650 / 1,090 = **0.60**. Net debt = 650 − 150 = 500; Net debt / EBITDA = 500 / 322 = **1.55×**.

*Step 3 – Coverage.* Year 2: EBIT coverage = 312 / 48 = **6.5×**; EBITDA coverage = 408 / 48 = **8.5×**. Year 1: EBIT coverage = 234 / 42 = **5.6×**.

*Step 4 – Average interest rate.* 48 / [(700 + 650) / 2] = 48 / 675 = **7.1%**.

*Step 5 – Stress test.* Suppose EBIT fell 50% in a downturn, to 156. Coverage would then be 156 / 48 = 3.25×, still above 3, a typical comfort threshold, though the figure will vary with industry and rating. Debt capacity is not exhausted. If Calder had to cover the entire 100 of short-term debt from operating cash flow in addition to interest, a drop to 156 of EBIT would leave after-tax earnings (156 − 48) × 0.75 = 81 plus depreciation 96 = 177 of cash flow before capex and working capital, enough to repay the short-term debt but not to sustain 196 of capex.

*Interpretation.* Calder's leverage is moderate and falling in relative terms (profit grew faster than debt). It could carry more debt, but whether it should depends on the stability of its cash flows, its investment needs and its financial flexibility; those issues are developed in the capital-structure chapters.

## 2.6 Profitability and the DuPont Framework

### 2.6.1 Profitability ratios

Profitability ratios measure the return earned on sales and on capital.

```
Gross margin = Gross profit / Revenue
Operating (EBIT) margin = EBIT / Revenue
EBITDA margin = EBITDA / Revenue
Net profit margin = Net income / Revenue
Return on assets (ROA) = Net income / Total assets
Return on equity (ROE) = Net income / Shareholders' equity
Return on invested capital (ROIC) = NOPAT / Invested capital
```

ROA and ROE depend on the denominator measure: year-end, beginning or average balances. Average balances better match a flow earned over a period with a stock that changed during it; for simplicity many textbook calculations, including those in this chapter, use year-end balances unless stated, and the choice should be applied consistently.

**ROIC** is the preferred measure of operating performance because it is independent of capital structure. Invested capital is the capital tied up in operations: operating working capital plus fixed assets plus other operating assets (equivalently, debt plus equity less excess cash). For Calder in Year 2: operating working capital = 360 + 330 + 30 − 240 − 120 = 360; plus net PP&E 1,100 and intangibles 300 gives **1,760**. As a check: debt 700 + equity 1,240 − cash 180 = 1,760. ✓ ROIC = NOPAT / invested capital = 234 / 1,760 = **13.3%**. In Year 1, invested capital was 650 + 1,090 − 150 = 1,590 and NOPAT was 234 × 0.75 = 175.5, so ROIC = 175.5 / 1,590 = **11.0%**. Calder's ROIC improved by about 2.3 points, and, if the cost of capital is, say, 9%, the firm earns a spread of about 4.3 points in Year 2.

### 2.6.2 The three-step DuPont identity

The DuPont framework, developed at the DuPont corporation in the early twentieth century, breaks ROE into the product of three drivers:

```
ROE = Net income / Equity
    = (Net income / Revenue) × (Revenue / Total assets) × (Total assets / Equity)
    =     Net profit margin    ×    Asset turnover     ×    Equity multiplier
```

- **Net profit margin** captures operating efficiency and pricing power: how much profit each dollar of sales yields.
- **Asset turnover** captures asset efficiency: how many dollars of sales each dollar of assets supports.
- **Equity multiplier** captures financial leverage: how many dollars of assets each dollar of equity controls.

The decomposition shows that a high ROE can arise from different strategies. A discount retailer typically has thin margins but fast turnover; a luxury brand has high margins and slow turnover; a bank has low margins on assets and high leverage. Comparing ROE alone conceals these differences, and, importantly, it shows that ROE can be raised by leverage without any improvement in the underlying business, at the price of more risk.

### 2.6.3 The five-step extension

The five-step version separates the effects of taxes and interest:

```
ROE = Tax burden × Interest burden × EBIT margin × Asset turnover × Equity multiplier
    = (NI / EBT) × (EBT / EBIT) × (EBIT / Revenue) × (Revenue / Assets) × (Assets / Equity)
```

The tax burden (NI / EBT = 1 − tax rate) reflects the fraction of pre-tax profit retained after tax. The interest burden (EBT / EBIT) reflects the fraction of operating profit retained after paying lenders. The EBIT margin is a measure of operating profitability before financing and tax.

**Example 2.7 – DuPont analysis of Calder.**

*Step 1 – Three-step, Year 2 (year-end balances).*
- Net profit margin = 198 / 2,400 = **8.25%**
- Asset turnover = 2,400 / 2,300 = **1.043×**
- Equity multiplier = 2,300 / 1,240 = **1.855×**
- ROE = 0.0825 × 1.0435 × 1.8548 = **15.97%**. Check: 198 / 1,240 = 15.97%. ✓

*Step 2 – Three-step, Year 1.*
- Net profit margin = 144 / 2,200 = **6.55%**
- Asset turnover = 2,200 / 2,050 = **1.073×**
- Equity multiplier = 2,050 / 1,090 = **1.881×**
- ROE = 0.06545 × 1.0732 × 1.8807 = **13.21%**. Check: 144 / 1,090 = 13.21%. ✓

*Step 3 – What changed?* ROE rose 2.76 points. Margin rose from 6.55% to 8.25% (+1.7 points, a 26% relative improvement), asset turnover fell from 1.073 to 1.043, and the equity multiplier fell slightly from 1.881 to 1.855. All of the improvement therefore came from profitability; the asset base grew faster than sales (12.2% against 9.1%) and leverage dropped a little. The improvement is attributable to operations rather than financial engineering, which is the better kind.

*Step 4 – Five-step, Year 2.*
- Tax burden = 198 / 264 = **0.750**
- Interest burden = 264 / 312 = **0.846**
- EBIT margin = 312 / 2,400 = **13.0%**
- Asset turnover = **1.0435**; equity multiplier = **1.8548**
- ROE = 0.750 × 0.8462 × 0.130 × 1.0435 × 1.8548 = **15.97%** ✓

*Step 5 – Five-step, Year 1.*
- Tax burden = 144 / 192 = **0.750**; interest burden = 192 / 234 = **0.821**; EBIT margin = 234 / 2,200 = **10.64%**; turnover **1.0732**; multiplier **1.8807**
- ROE = 0.75 × 0.8205 × 0.10636 × 1.0732 × 1.8807 = **13.21%** ✓

*Step 6 – Interpretation.* The tax burden was constant. The interest burden improved from 0.821 to 0.846, because EBIT grew faster than interest. The dominant driver is the EBIT margin, up 2.4 points. Notably, the improvement in the interest burden is a by-product of operating success rather than lower debt. A rival reporting the same ROE might get there with a 10% margin, 0.9× turnover and a 2.3× multiplier; the DuPont decomposition would reveal that its ROE depends on gearing, and would raise the question of how it would fare in a downturn.

*Using average balances:* Average equity is (1,240 + 1,090) / 2 = 1,165, so ROE on average equity = 198 / 1,165 = 17.0%. The level differs, but the diagnosis is the same.

### 2.6.4 ROE, growth and the sustainable growth rate

ROE links profitability to growth. If a firm retains a fraction **b** of earnings (the retention or plowback ratio) and earns a return ROE on its equity, then equity grows at b × ROE when ROE is calculated on *beginning* equity. This is the **sustainable growth rate**: the rate at which the firm can grow without issuing new equity or changing its leverage and profitability. When ROE is measured on year-end equity, the same result is written:

```
Sustainable growth rate g = (ROE × b) / (1 − ROE × b)      [ROE on year-end equity]
                          = ROE(beginning equity) × b
```

**Example 2.8 – Sustainable growth for Calder.**

*Step 1 – Retention.* Dividends were 48 on net income of 198, a payout ratio of 48 / 198 = 24.2%, so b = 1 − 0.2424 = **0.7576**.

*Step 2 – Return on beginning equity.* 198 / 1,090 = **18.17%**.

*Step 3 – Growth.* g = 0.1817 × 0.7576 = **13.76%**. Check: equity grew from 1,090 to 1,240, an increase of 13.76%. ✓

*Step 4 – Using year-end ROE.* ROE × b = 0.1597 × 0.7576 = 0.1210; g = 0.1210 / (1 − 0.1210) = **13.76%**. ✓ (The simple product 12.1% understates growth when year-end equity is used.)

*Step 5 – Compare with actual sales growth.* Revenue grew 9.1%, below the 13.8% sustainable rate. Calder could grow faster without raising equity, provided its margin, turnover and leverage hold. In fact, if turnover and the capital structure stay the same, assets must grow at the same rate as equity.

*Interpretation.* The sustainable growth rate gives a quick test of financial feasibility. A firm planning growth above its sustainable rate must raise more equity, increase leverage, improve margins or turnover, or reduce dividends. Rapidly growing businesses frequently fail not because they are unprofitable but because their growth outpaces their financing, a condition known as overtrading.

## 2.7 Quality of Earnings and the Limits of Ratio Analysis

### 2.7.1 What is meant by quality of earnings

High-quality earnings are those that are sustainable, repeatable, backed by cash and derived from conservative, transparent accounting. Low-quality earnings are inflated by one-off gains, aggressive revenue recognition, understated expenses or non-cash items that will reverse. Because management has discretion over accrual estimates and timing, no analysis of profitability is complete without an assessment of quality.

Red flags commonly include:

- **Revenue recognised early or aggressively:** receivables growing faster than sales, rising DSO, bill-and-hold arrangements, large returns or credit notes after quarter-end, channel stuffing (shipping more than distributors need).
- **Expenses deferred:** capitalisation of costs that peers expense (software development, customer-acquisition costs), lengthening depreciation lives, reduced maintenance or R&D, inadequate provisions for bad debts or warranties.
- **Reliance on non-operating items:** gains on asset sales, pension income assumptions, reversal of provisions, tax benefits.
- **Frequent "non-recurring" charges** and heavy use of adjusted or "pro forma" earnings that exclude recurring costs, such as stock-based compensation.
- **Divergence between earnings and cash flow:** profit persistently exceeding CFO.
- **Complex structures:** off-balance-sheet entities, aggressive related-party transactions, frequent changes in auditors or accounting policies, unusual fourth-quarter profits.

### 2.7.2 Cash-based and accrual-based diagnostics

Two simple diagnostics compare profit with cash.

```
Cash conversion (CFO / Net income)
Accruals ratio = (Net income − CFO) / Average total assets
```

A ratio of CFO to net income persistently below one, or a persistently positive accruals ratio, means that earnings rely on accruals rather than cash. Research on the accrual anomaly, notably the work of Richard Sloan, found that firms with high accruals tend to experience lower subsequent returns, consistent with investors overpricing the persistence of accrual-based earnings. A refined version scales accruals by net operating assets (NOA).

**Example 2.9 – Earnings quality of Calder and of a contrast firm.**

*Step 1 – Calder: cash conversion.* CFO / net income = 224 / 198 = **1.13**.

*Step 2 – Calder: accruals ratio.* (198 − 224) / [(2,300 + 2,050) / 2] = −26 / 2,175 = **−1.2%**. Negative accruals mean cash earnings exceed accounting profit, usually a good sign. Part of this is the large depreciation add-back; the working-capital investment of 70 is a drag on CFO, which is partly why the ratio is not even higher.

*Step 3 – Calder: free cash flow conversion.* FCFE / net income = 78 / 198 = **0.39**. Cash conversion before investment is excellent, but after heavy capex only 39% of profit is free cash.

*Step 4 – Contrast firm "Vireo Systems".* Vireo reports net income of 120 and CFO of 45, with average total assets of 800 and sales of 3,000. Cash conversion = 45 / 120 = **0.375**. Accruals ratio = (120 − 45) / 800 = **9.4%**, a high level that would trigger scrutiny.

*Step 5 – Diagnosis.* Vireo's DSO rose from 48 to 62 days. At sales of 3,000 per year (8.22 per day), receivables at 48 days were 3,000 × 48 / 365 = 394.5; at 62 days they are 3,000 × 62 / 365 = 509.6, an increase of **115.1**. That increase alone exceeds the entire gap between profit and cash (75), so the profit is, in effect, sitting in customers' accounts. Possible explanations include deliberate loosening of credit, early revenue recognition or customers in difficulty. Each implies a different risk, but none supports valuing Vireo at a multiple of reported earnings without further investigation.

### 2.7.3 Normalisation and adjusted earnings

Analysts often adjust reported earnings to estimate sustainable (core or recurring) earnings: remove gains and losses on disposals, restructuring and impairment charges that are genuinely unusual, and adjust for unusual tax rates. The risk lies in the adjustments themselves. A rule of thumb: if a cost recurs, it is an operating cost. Another: treat stock-based compensation as an economic expense, since the firm pays employees in claims on shareholders' wealth rather than in cash.

### 2.7.4 Limits of ratio analysis

Ratios are indispensable but imperfect.

- **They are backward-looking** and based on historical cost; valuation is forward-looking.
- **Accounting policies differ** across firms and countries (inventory methods, depreciation lives, capitalisation of development costs, lease treatment), reducing comparability.
- **Year-end snapshots can mislead** for seasonal businesses, or if management times transactions (see Example 2.5).
- **Aggregation hides detail.** A consolidated ratio for a diversified firm blends different businesses with different norms.
- **A ratio has no meaning in isolation.** It should be interpreted against history, peers and the firm's strategy and risk, and tied to the drivers of cash flow.
- **Inflation and growth distort** comparisons over time.
- **Non-financial drivers** (customer satisfaction, product pipeline, management quality, regulatory exposure) are invisible in the statements but often decisive for future cash flows.

The right approach is therefore to treat ratios as questions rather than answers. A deteriorating cash conversion cycle prompts a question to operations; a rising accruals ratio prompts questions to the controller and auditor; a falling interest burden prompts a question about the debt maturity profile. Used this way, ratio analysis is a disciplined method of directing management's attention.

### 2.7.5 Putting it together: a systematic approach

A practical sequence for analysing a firm is the following.

1. **Understand the business and the accounting.** Read the strategy, segment data and accounting policy notes; identify the key judgements.
2. **Read the cash flow statement first.** Is CFO healthy and consistent with profit? How is growth funded?
3. **Common-size and trend the statements.** Identify the lines that moved.
4. **Compute ratios by category.** Liquidity, efficiency, leverage and profitability, with DuPont to link them.
5. **Benchmark** against history and peers, adjusting for accounting differences.
6. **Test earnings quality** with the diagnostics above.
7. **Form a view**: what are the drivers of value, the key risks and the questions for management?

| Category | Key ratios | Question answered | Typical warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Current, quick, cash ratio | Can the firm pay its bills in the next year? | Falling ratios with rising short-term debt |
| Efficiency | DSO, DIO, DPO, CCC, asset turnover | How well are assets and working capital used? | CCC lengthening; receivables outpacing sales |
| Leverage | Debt/equity, net debt/EBITDA, coverage | How much fixed-claim financing, and can it be serviced? | Coverage below covenants; rising net debt/EBITDA |
| Profitability | Margins, ROA, ROE, ROIC | How much return is earned on sales and capital? | ROIC below WACC; margin erosion |
| Cash quality | CFO/NI, accruals ratio, FCF | Is profit backed by cash? | Persistent CFO below net income |

## Key Takeaways

- The balance sheet, income statement, cash flow statement and statement of changes in equity articulate; checking that they reconcile is the first line of defence against error and manipulation.
- Accrual accounting gives a better picture of performance than cash receipts, but it depends on estimates and judgements; profit is an opinion, while cash is a fact.
- Operating cash flow is derived from net income by adding back non-cash charges and adjusting for working-capital changes: increases in operating assets use cash and increases in operating liabilities provide it.
- Free cash flow to the firm (NOPAT + D&A − capex − ΔNWC) is the cash available to all capital providers and is the basis of enterprise valuation; free cash flow to equity subtracts after-tax interest and adds net borrowing.
- Common-size and trend analysis reveal the structure and direction of change in a firm and show whether margins, working capital and financing are moving together with growth.
- Liquidity ratios test short-term solvency, but the efficiency ratios and the cash conversion cycle explain why liquidity changes; year-end ratios can be window-dressed.
- Leverage must be judged on both capital structure (debt to equity or capital, net debt to EBITDA) and coverage (EBIT or EBITDA to interest), and stress-tested against downturns and covenants.
- The DuPont framework decomposes ROE into margin, asset turnover and leverage (and in five steps, tax burden, interest burden and operating margin), showing whether returns arise from operations or financial engineering.
- ROIC, being independent of capital structure, is the best summary of operating performance and should be compared with the cost of capital; the sustainable growth rate links ROE and retention to feasible growth.
- Quality of earnings is assessed by comparing profit with cash (CFO/NI, accruals ratio), by scrutinising receivables, capitalisation and non-recurring items, and ratios should be treated as questions that direct investigation rather than as final answers.
