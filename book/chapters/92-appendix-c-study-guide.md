# Appendix C: Study Guide and Course Designs

This appendix helps students and instructors turn the book into a working course. It offers semester plans for a 14-week and an 8-week format, exam-preparation checklists, a team project template with a grading rubric, and suggested reading categories for going further. The plans are starting points; adapt them to class size, prior preparation, and the mix of full-time, part-time, and executive students.

## C.1 Principles Behind the Course Designs

Four principles shaped the plans below. First, **valuation is the spine**. Chapters 3 to 6 build the machinery of discounting, and every later topic, from cost of capital to M&A, is an application of it. Students who are shaky here will struggle later, so the early weeks deserve more time than their page count suggests. Second, **risk is priced, not avoided**. Chapters 9 to 11 explain why discount rates differ across projects, and this idea must be re-used continuously. Third, **decisions come in three families**: investment (what to buy), financing (how to pay for it), and payout or risk management (what to return or protect). Each class session should be placed in one of these families so students see the whole. Fourth, **judgement matters as much as calculation**. Models are only as good as their assumptions, so every numerical session should end with the question: what would change my mind?

Suggested workload is roughly two to three hours of preparation per hour of class. Students from non-financial backgrounds benefit from a pre-course refresher on algebra, logarithms, and basic statistics (mean, variance, correlation, regression), plus a spreadsheet tutorial covering NPV, IRR, and goal seek functions.

## C.2 Fourteen-Week Semester Plan

The 14-week plan assumes one three-hour session per week or two 90-minute sessions. It covers all 24 chapters, with some chapters paired. Weeks 7 and 14 hold assessments or review.

### Week 1: The Firm and Its Financial Statements (Chapters 1 and 2)

Introduce the corporation, the goal of value maximisation, agency problems, and the roles of the CFO and treasurer. Then read financial statements as a manager: link the balance sheet, income statement, and cash flow statement, and use DuPont analysis to diagnose performance. In-class activity: students receive statements for a fictional firm and write a half-page diagnosis of strengths and weaknesses. Deliverable: ratio workbook template completed.

### Week 2: Time Value of Money and Bonds (Chapters 3 and 4)

Teach present value, future value, annuities, perpetuities, effective rates, and loan amortisation, then apply them to bond pricing, yields, duration, and credit spreads. In-class activity: price a bond from a coupon schedule and shock its yield to compare duration-based and exact price changes. Deliverable: problem set 1 on discounting and bonds.

### Week 3: Equity Valuation and NPV (Chapters 5 and 6)

Value stocks with dividend models, earnings multiples, and residual income; then establish NPV as the master rule and compare it with IRR, payback, profitability index, and capital rationing. In-class activity: a debate on a project where IRR and NPV conflict. Deliverable: problem set 2.

### Week 4: Capital Budgeting and Uncertainty (Chapters 7 and 8)

Build project cash flows with taxes, depreciation, working capital, and terminal values; run sensitivity, scenario, and break-even analysis; introduce decision trees and real options. In-class activity: a full spreadsheet capital budget for a fictional plant, with an abandonment option added. Deliverable: capital budgeting mini-case.

### Week 5: Risk, Return, Portfolios and CAPM (Chapters 9 and 10)

Define risk and return, correlation, diversification, the efficient frontier, the capital allocation line, beta, the security market line, and multifactor models. In-class activity: a two-asset portfolio exercise showing how correlation changes risk. Deliverable: problem set 3.

### Week 6: Cost of Capital and Market Efficiency (Chapters 11 and 12)

Estimate WACC, unlever and relever betas, and select project-specific rates; then discuss what efficiency means, the evidence on anomalies, and the lessons of behavioural finance for managers and investors. In-class activity: estimate a divisional hurdle rate from comparable firms. Deliverable: cost of capital worksheet; project proposal due (Section C.6).

### Week 7: Midterm Examination and Review

A 120-minute closed-book examination covering Chapters 1 to 12, with a formula sheet permitted (Appendix A without worked content). The balance should be about 40 percent calculations, 30 percent short interpretation, and 30 percent a mini-case.

### Week 8: Raising Capital (Chapters 13 and 14)

Cover venture financing and valuation rounds, IPOs, seasoned offerings and rights issues, then bank loans, bonds, covenants, leases, convertibles, and project finance. In-class activity: structure the financing of a growth company and justify the mix. Deliverable: problem set 4.

### Week 9: Capital Structure and Payout Policy (Chapters 15 and 16)

Teach Modigliani-Miller, taxes, distress costs, trade-off and pecking-order views, and the practical determinants of leverage; then dividends, repurchases, signalling, and clientele. In-class activity: choose a target capital structure for a fictional firm and recommend a payout policy. Deliverable: capital structure memo (one page).

### Week 10: Working Capital and Options (Chapters 17 and 18)

Study the cash conversion cycle, credit policy, inventory, and short-term financing, then option payoffs, put-call parity, the binomial model, Black-Scholes, and corporate securities as options. In-class activity: compute the cost of forgoing a trade discount and price an option on a tree. Deliverable: problem set 5.

### Week 11: Derivatives, Hedging and Foreign Exchange (Chapters 19 and 20)

Cover forwards, futures, swaps, caps and floors, hedge ratios, exchange-rate parity, exposure types, and hedging policy. In-class activity: design a hedging programme for a fictional exporter, comparing a forward with an option. Deliverable: hedging recommendation.

### Week 12: Enterprise Valuation and M&A (Chapters 21 and 22)

Perform a full discounted cash flow valuation with terminal value and cross-checks against multiples; then evaluate synergies, payment methods, accretion and dilution, and takeover defences. In-class activity: a bid simulation in which teams decide how much of the synergy to concede. Deliverable: valuation exercise.

### Week 13: LBOs, Restructuring, and Sustainable Finance (Chapters 23 and 24)

Study leveraged buyout models, private equity economics, distress and restructuring, then sustainable finance, governance, and fintech business models. In-class activity: a simple LBO model with returns attribution, and a debate on whether green financing lowers the cost of capital. Deliverable: team project drafts exchanged for peer review.

### Week 14: Integration and Final Presentations

Teams present their projects (Section C.6); the instructor leads a closing discussion tying together investment, financing, and payout decisions. The final examination is cumulative, with weight on Chapters 13 to 24 but including integrative valuation questions.

### Suggested Assessment Weights (14 Weeks)

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Problem sets (five, best four count) | 15% |
| Midterm examination | 25% |
| Team project (written plus presentation) | 25% |
| Final examination | 30% |
| Class contribution | 5% |

## C.3 Eight-Week Intensive Plan

The 8-week format suits an accelerated or executive course, with one four-hour session per week or two two-hour sessions. It cannot cover all 24 chapters in equal depth, so it designates **core chapters** (taught fully) and **reading chapters** (assigned for self-study with a brief class discussion). Core chapters are 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, and 22. Reading chapters are 1, 2, 5, 8, 12, 13, 14, 17, 20, 23, and 24.

### Week 1: Foundations (Chapters 1, 2, 3)

Frame value maximisation and agency, read the financial statements quickly (a pre-class self-study with a short quiz), and teach discounting thoroughly. Students leave able to compute present values, annuities, effective rates, and loan schedules.

### Week 2: Bonds and Investment Decisions (Chapters 4, 6, 7)

Price bonds and measure interest rate risk; adopt NPV as the decision rule and compare it with IRR; build a project cash flow forecast with taxes, depreciation, and working capital. Chapter 5 is assigned as reading; students apply the dividend discount model in a short exercise.

### Week 3: Risk and Return (Chapters 9, 10, with 8 as reading)

Teach diversification, the efficient frontier, and CAPM. Use Chapter 8 reading to introduce sensitivity analysis and real options in a brief class discussion rather than full technical development. Quiz covering Weeks 1 to 3 at the start of Week 4.

### Week 4: Cost of Capital and Capital Structure (Chapters 11, 15)

Estimate WACC and project rates, then examine how leverage affects value and risk. Include a short discussion of market efficiency (Chapter 12 reading) as it bears on issuing decisions and the cost of equity. Midterm exam (take-home or in-class, 90 minutes) covering Weeks 1 to 4.

### Week 5: Financing and Payout (Chapters 16, with 13 and 14 as reading)

Cover dividends and repurchases in class. Students read the financing chapters, then complete a short instrument-choice exercise: given a firm's profile, justify equity, bank debt, bonds, or convertibles.

### Week 6: Options and Derivatives (Chapters 18, 19, with 20 as reading)

Teach payoffs, put-call parity, binomial and Black-Scholes pricing, and the logic of forwards, futures, swaps, and hedge ratios. Exchange-rate exposure and the parity conditions in Chapter 20 are assigned as reading and examined through a short case.

### Week 7: Valuation and M&A (Chapters 21, 22)

Conduct a full DCF valuation with terminal value and multiples cross-check, followed by an M&A session on synergy, premium, payment method, and accretion/dilution. Teams begin integrating their project analysis.

### Week 8: LBOs, Sustainability, and Presentations (Chapters 23, 24 as reading)

Teams present projects. A closing session uses a short LBO illustration and a discussion of sustainable finance and governance to show how valuation concepts extend to new settings. The final examination is a 120-minute cumulative test.

### Suggested Assessment Weights (8 Weeks)

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Weekly problem sets and quizzes | 20% |
| Midterm examination | 25% |
| Team project (shortened version) | 25% |
| Final examination | 30% |

### Notes for Executive Audiences

Executive students usually come with strong judgement and limited tolerance for algebra. Replace some derivations with decision-focused cases drawn from their own firms. Emphasise interpreting outputs: what a WACC implies, why a hedge may create accounting volatility, what the market is saying through a multiple. Keep calculation practice in spreadsheets, and give a one-page "what would change my mind" prompt with each case.

## C.4 Exam-Preparation Checklists

Use these checklists in the final week before an exam. A topic is "ready" when you can solve a problem on it without notes, explain the intuition in two sentences, and name the most common mistake.

### Checklist 1: Foundations and Valuation (Chapters 1 to 8)

- Distinguish value maximisation from profit maximisation and explain at least three agency problems with a remedy for each.
- Compute and interpret liquidity, turnover, margin, leverage, and return ratios; carry out a three-factor DuPont decomposition.
- Build a free cash flow from financial statement data.
- Compute present values and future values for single sums, annuities, growing annuities, and perpetuities, including cases with non-annual compounding.
- Convert between nominal, effective, and real rates.
- Construct a loan amortisation schedule and identify the interest and principal in a given payment.
- Price a bond, find its yield to maturity, and estimate the price effect of a yield change using duration and convexity.
- Explain the relationship between bond prices, coupons, and yields, including why prices of long-maturity, low-coupon bonds are most sensitive.
- Value a stock with constant-growth and two-stage dividend models; decompose price into assets-in-place and growth opportunities.
- Compute NPV, IRR, payback, discounted payback, and profitability index; explain when IRR misleads (multiple IRRs, mutually exclusive projects, scale and timing differences).
- Choose between projects of unequal lives using equivalent annual annuity.
- Develop incremental after-tax cash flows, including depreciation tax shields, working capital, salvage, and opportunity costs; ignore sunk costs and financing flows.
- Run sensitivity, scenario, and break-even analysis and interpret operating leverage.
- Value a simple option to delay, expand, or abandon using a decision tree or a binomial approach.

### Checklist 2: Risk, Return and Cost of Capital (Chapters 9 to 12)

- Calculate expected return, variance, standard deviation, covariance, and correlation from a probability table or a return history.
- Compute two-asset portfolio risk and return, and show how correlation alters the benefit of diversification.
- Distinguish systematic from idiosyncratic risk and explain why only the former earns a premium.
- Locate the minimum-variance portfolio and the tangency portfolio; use the capital allocation line.
- Estimate beta by regression or from covariance, and compute required return with CAPM.
- Compute Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio, and Jensen's alpha and say what each concludes.
- Explain what multifactor models add and what the Fama-French factors represent.
- Compute WACC with market-value weights and after-tax debt costs; justify using a project-specific rate.
- Unlever and relever betas using the Hamada relation; state the assumptions behind it.
- Estimate the cost of equity by three methods and explain why they differ.
- Define weak, semi-strong, and strong market efficiency and describe the type of evidence for each.
- Perform and interpret a simple event study.
- Explain at least four behavioural biases and the limits to arbitrage that allow mispricing to persist.

### Checklist 3: Financing and Policy (Chapters 13 to 17)

- Compute pre- and post-money valuations, ownership shares, and dilution through a venture round.
- Calculate IPO proceeds, underwriting costs, and underpricing; explain why underpricing occurs.
- Compute the ex-rights price and the value of a right.
- Compare bank loans, public bonds, private placements, leases, and convertibles on cost, flexibility, covenants, and risk.
- Value a convertible bond using conversion value and straight-bond value.
- Evaluate a refunding decision or a lease-versus-buy decision with after-tax cash flows.
- State Modigliani-Miller propositions with and without taxes and compute the value of the interest tax shield.
- Describe trade-off and pecking-order theories and the evidence for each.
- Analyse the EPS effects of alternative financing plans and find the break-even EBIT.
- Explain dividend irrelevance, signalling, clientele, and taxes; compare dividends and buybacks in a numerical example.
- Compute the cash conversion cycle and the effect of a policy change on investment in working capital.
- Calculate the cost of forgoing a trade discount and compare it with bank borrowing.
- Evaluate a credit policy change using incremental contribution, receivables investment, and bad debts.

### Checklist 4: Options, Hedging and Valuation (Chapters 18 to 24)

- Draw payoffs and profits for calls, puts, and common combinations.
- Apply put-call parity to find an arbitrage or a missing price.
- Price an option with a one- or two-step binomial tree and by the Black-Scholes formula.
- Interpret delta, gamma, vega, and theta.
- Explain equity as a call option on the firm's assets and warrants as dilutive options.
- Price a forward using cost of carry; compute the variance-minimising hedge ratio and number of futures contracts.
- Value a plain-vanilla interest rate swap and explain how swaps convert exposures.
- Use covered interest parity and purchasing power parity; identify triangular arbitrage.
- Compare forward, money-market, and option hedges of a foreign-currency receivable.
- Value an enterprise with free cash flow to the firm, a terminal value, and a bridge to equity value per share.
- Cross-check a DCF with comparable multiples and explain the sources of divergence.
- Estimate synergies, the premium paid, and who captures value in cash and stock deals; compute accretion or dilution and explain why it misleads.
- Build a simple LBO sources-and-uses table and compute MOIC and IRR; attribute returns to EBITDA growth, multiple change, and debt paydown.
- Explain recovery in bankruptcy under absolute priority and when restructuring beats liquidation.
- Discuss how carbon pricing, green financing, governance, and fintech unit economics affect valuation.

### General Examination Strategy

1. **Read the whole exam first** and mark the questions you can answer quickly. Begin with them to secure marks and confidence.
2. **State assumptions explicitly.** When a problem is ambiguous (timing of cash flows, whether to use book or market weights), write your assumption and proceed; partial credit often depends on it.
3. **Show formulas before numbers.** A correct setup with an arithmetic slip earns more than a bare wrong answer.
4. **Check units and periods.** Match the rate period to the cash-flow period and keep nominal with nominal, real with real.
5. **Sanity-check results.** A bond priced above par with a yield above its coupon, or a beta of 15, signals an error.
6. **Interpret.** Close each calculation with a sentence on what it means for the decision. Many exams reward the recommendation as much as the arithmetic.
7. **Manage time** in proportion to marks, and leave a few minutes for review.

### Common Errors to Avoid

| Error | Correction |
|---|---|
| Discounting flows at a rate that does not match their frequency | Convert the rate to the period of the cash flows |
| Including sunk costs or interest payments in project cash flows | Use incremental, after-tax, unlevered cash flows; capture financing in the discount rate |
| Using book-value weights in WACC | Use target market-value weights |
| Applying the firm's WACC to a project of different risk | Use a rate that reflects the project's own risk |
| Using IRR to choose between mutually exclusive projects | Use NPV, or incremental IRR with care |
| Treating EPS accretion as proof of value creation | Compare price paid to stand-alone value plus achievable synergy |
| Forgetting that debt raises equity risk even when it lowers WACC mechanically | Remember the equity cost rises with leverage |
| Mixing continuously compounded and discrete rates | Convert explicitly |
| Ignoring working capital recovery at project end | Add the release of working capital to the terminal year |
| Describing hedging as profit-seeking | Describe it as variance reduction, with a clear policy |

## C.5 Using the Book in Class

### Active-Learning Formats

**Cold call and warm call.** Assign a worked example in advance and ask a student to walk the class through it, announcing the order beforehand for anxious students.

**Two-minute drills.** At the beginning of a session, pose a single quantitative question that students answer individually, then compare with a neighbour. This reveals misconceptions quickly.

**Decision memos.** Replace some problem sets with a one-page memo addressed to a named fictional executive, requiring a recommendation, a computation, and a risk. This builds the communication skill that distinguishes leaders from analysts.

**Debates.** Useful topics include whether a firm should pay dividends or repurchase shares, whether to hedge, whether market prices can be trusted, and whether green financing creates value. Assign sides at random to force students to argue positions they may not hold.

**Spreadsheet labs.** Dedicate part of a session to building a model live: an amortisation schedule, a capital budget, a WACC, a DCF, or an LBO. Provide a clean template and require students to audit one another's formulas.

### Case Method

Each chapter's case study can anchor a class discussion. A workable sequence is: five minutes to frame the decision, fifteen to establish the facts and calculations, twenty to debate alternatives, and ten to extract lessons. Encourage students to separate **what the numbers say** from **what the numbers leave out**, such as strategic fit, execution risk, and stakeholder reactions.

### Differentiating for Mixed Backgrounds

Pair students with financial and non-financial backgrounds for problem sets, rotating partners each week. Provide optional "bridge" exercises on accounting and statistics for those who need them and "stretch" tasks, such as adding a real option to a capital budget, for those who want more. Hold weekly office hours devoted to spreadsheet troubleshooting.

## C.6 Team Project Template

The team project integrates the course into a single piece of analysis. Teams of four or five students act as an advisory group to the chief financial officer of a company, real (using public information only) or fictional (using a data pack supplied by the instructor). The deliverables are a written report of 3,000 to 4,000 words with a spreadsheet model appended, and a 15-minute presentation followed by questions.

### Project Options

1. **Investment and financing recommendation.** Evaluate a major expansion, then recommend how to finance it.
2. **Valuation and strategic options.** Value the firm, test the sensitivity of value to key assumptions, and recommend whether to acquire, divest, or remain independent.
3. **Capital structure and payout review.** Diagnose the existing structure and payout policy, benchmark against peers, and recommend changes.
4. **Risk management programme.** Identify the firm's exposures, quantify them, and design a hedging policy.
5. **Transaction analysis.** Assess a merger or leveraged buyout, including synergy, price, financing, and returns.

### Required Structure of the Report

1. **Executive summary (one page).** The decision, the recommendation, the key numbers, and the main risks. A reader should be able to act on this page alone.
2. **Company and context.** Business model, strategy, industry position, and the decision at hand. Include a short ratio analysis using Chapter 2 tools.
3. **Analysis.** The core quantitative work, with every assumption stated. Depending on the option, this includes cash flow forecasts, cost of capital, valuation, capital structure analysis, hedging calculations, or transaction modelling.
4. **Uncertainty and alternatives.** Sensitivity and scenario analysis, a discussion of real options, and at least one realistic alternative to the recommendation, evaluated on the same basis.
5. **Implementation and risks.** How the recommendation would be executed, timing, governance requirements, and what monitoring signals would trigger a change of course.
6. **Appendices.** Model documentation, data sources, and detailed tables.

### Model Requirements

The spreadsheet should separate inputs, calculations, and outputs; include a sheet listing every assumption with its source or rationale; use no hard-coded numbers inside formulas; and include an error check, such as a balance check or sources-equal-uses test. Teams should be able to change one assumption and see all results update.

### Milestones

| Milestone | Timing (14-week course) | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Proposal | Week 6 | Project option, company, data plan, team roles (one page) |
| Draft analysis | Week 10 | Key models and preliminary results |
| Draft report for peer review | Week 13 | Complete draft exchanged with another team |
| Final report and presentation | Week 14 | Polished submission and presentation |

In the 8-week course, shorten the report to 2,000 words, reduce the model scope, and set the proposal in Week 3 and the presentation in Week 8.

### Team Project Rubric

Each criterion is scored from 1 (unsatisfactory) to 5 (excellent) and multiplied by its weight.

| Criterion | Weight | Excellent (5) | Satisfactory (3) | Unsatisfactory (1) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem framing | 10% | Decision, objective, and constraints crisply defined; value criterion explicit | Decision identified but objective or constraints vague | Decision unclear or confused with description |
| Technical accuracy | 25% | Correct methods, consistent assumptions, accurate calculations | Mostly correct with minor errors or inconsistencies | Fundamental errors such as wrong discount rate logic |
| Use of data and assumptions | 15% | Assumptions sourced, reasonable, and tested | Assumptions stated but weakly justified | Assumptions missing or unrealistic |
| Treatment of risk and uncertainty | 15% | Sensitivity, scenarios, and options integrated into the recommendation | Sensitivity done but not linked to the decision | Risk ignored or only qualitative |
| Quality of recommendation | 15% | Clear, actionable, and consistent with the analysis; alternatives compared | Reasonable but under-argued | Missing or contradicts analysis |
| Communication, written | 10% | Concise, well-organised, professional tables | Understandable but uneven | Disorganised or hard to follow |
| Presentation and questions | 10% | Confident, within time, handles questions with evidence | Adequate delivery, partial answers | Poor structure, unable to defend work |

A total score is computed as the weighted sum divided by five, giving a percentage. Instructors may adjust individual marks using a peer-evaluation form in which each member rates others' contribution; a common approach is to limit adjustments to plus or minus ten percentage points.

### Guidance on Using Real Companies

Use only public information, such as annual reports and market data, and state the date of the data. Avoid inventing facts about a real firm; where information is unavailable, say so and state an assumption. Teams must treat their work as an academic exercise, not investment advice, and must not claim to represent the views of the company studied.

## C.7 Suggested Reading Categories

The following categories point to the types of sources that deepen each part of the course. No specific titles are listed here; instructors and students should consult library catalogues and current syllabi for up-to-date editions and recommendations.

**Foundational corporate finance textbooks.** Standard MBA and graduate texts offer alternative explanations, additional problems, and broader coverage. Reading a second treatment of a difficult topic often unlocks it.

**Valuation handbooks.** Practitioner-oriented books on enterprise valuation, multiples, and cash flow forecasting complement Chapter 21 and provide industry-specific guidance.

**Asset pricing and portfolio theory.** Graduate-level treatments of mean-variance theory, CAPM, factor models, and market efficiency help students who wish to understand the foundations and the empirical debates.

**Original academic papers.** Foundational contributions on capital structure and dividend irrelevance, portfolio selection, the capital asset pricing model, option pricing, agency theory, signalling, and prospect theory are accessible to motivated MBA students, and reading excerpts of the originals shows how the ideas were first argued.

**Behavioural finance and decision-making.** Popular and academic accounts of judgement under uncertainty, heuristics and biases, and the limits of arbitrage provide context for Chapter 12 and for corporate decision-making generally.

**Fixed income and derivatives references.** Specialist texts on bond markets, interest rate modelling, options, futures, and swaps support Chapters 4, 18, and 19, and show how markets work in practice.

**Financial statement analysis and accounting.** Guides to interpreting statements, quality of earnings, and accounting standards help students apply Chapter 2 and avoid misreading reported numbers.

**Mergers, acquisitions, and private equity.** Works that combine deal mechanics, valuation, negotiation, and empirical evidence on acquisition outcomes and buyout returns enrich Chapters 22 and 23.

**Corporate governance and sustainable finance.** Regulatory codes, stewardship principles, standards for sustainability disclosure and reporting, and academic evidence on ESG and cost of capital are relevant to Chapter 24. Because standards and regulations change quickly, consult the current versions published by the relevant bodies.

**Case collections.** Case books from business schools provide realistic, ambiguous decisions for discussion and can be combined with the chapter cases here.

**Data sources and tools.** Company annual reports and regulatory filings, central bank and statistical agency publications, and market data providers supply the inputs for projects. Spreadsheet and statistical software manuals help with modelling.

**Financial press and professional journals.** Regular reading of business newspapers, magazines, and practitioner journals links theory to events. Encourage students to bring one current news item per week and classify it as an investment, financing, or payout or risk decision.

### How to Read Academic and Professional Sources Critically

When reading any source, ask five questions. What is the claim? What evidence or reasoning supports it? What are the assumptions, and do they apply to the problem at hand? Who benefits from the conclusion? What would a sceptic say? Treat statistics found in secondary sources with caution, check the date and the definition, and verify against primary data when the figure matters to a decision.

## C.8 Self-Study Roadmap

Independent learners can use the following sequence. First, read Chapters 1 to 3 and make sure you can discount cash flows fluently; use a spreadsheet to reproduce every worked example. Second, read Chapters 4 to 8, then try capital budgeting on a personal or small-business decision. Third, complete Chapters 9 to 12, and estimate the cost of capital of a listed company, noting every judgement you make. Fourth, study Chapters 13 to 17, and read the financing section of a company's annual report to identify its capital structure and payout policy. Fifth, study Chapters 18 to 20 and write one paragraph explaining why a firm in your industry would or would not hedge. Sixth, finish Chapters 21 to 24 by valuing a company and writing a one-page recommendation on a hypothetical acquisition.

After each chapter, spend ten minutes on three tasks: write the key formula from memory, restate the main idea in two sentences, and list one situation where the idea would not apply. Return to Appendix A before exams, and to Appendix B whenever a term is unfamiliar.

## C.9 Instructor Notes on Pacing and Common Difficulties

Several topics reliably cause difficulty and merit extra time. **Compounding conventions** confuse students who have not met continuous rates; spend a short session converting among nominal, effective, and continuous rates. **Cash flow forecasting** is a skill, not a formula; model one forecast live and let teams repeat it. **Beta and WACC** involve many moving parts; give a complete numerical walk-through from regression output to final rate. **Capital structure theory** is abstract; anchor it in a numerical example with and without taxes before introducing distress costs. **Option pricing** benefits from the binomial tree before Black-Scholes, since the tree reveals the logic of replication and risk-neutral valuation. **M&A** invites enthusiasm for accretion; consistently return to the question of who pays for, and who receives, the synergies.

Throughout the course, keep returning to the book's central message: finance is a toolkit for deciding where to invest, how to fund investment, and how to manage the risks and returns that follow. The tools are quantitative, but the decisions are about people, incentives, and judgement under uncertainty.
