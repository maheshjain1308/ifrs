# Chapter 1: The Corporation and the Finance Function

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

- Describe the three core decisions of corporate finance (investment, financing and payout) and explain how the finance function is organised to support them.
- Explain why maximising the long-run value of the firm's equity is the operating objective of corporate finance, and distinguish it from profit maximisation, earnings-per-share targets and stakeholder-neutral slogans.
- Identify the principal agency conflicts (shareholders and managers, shareholders and creditors, controlling and minority shareholders) and quantify their cost in simple settings.
- Evaluate governance mechanisms such as boards, incentive pay, creditor covenants, the market for corporate control and regulation, and judge which fit a given situation.
- Connect financial analysis to strategy using return on invested capital, economic profit and the value-driver formula.
- Describe the structure and function of financial markets and intermediaries, and interpret an announcement-day abnormal return as evidence about how markets value corporate decisions.

## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario. Northgate Industrial Group is a fictional company.*

On a Tuesday in March, the board of Northgate Industrial Group met to approve its capital budget. Northgate makes industrial pumps and valves, employs about 6,000 people and has a market capitalisation of roughly $1.2 billion and 100 million shares outstanding. The chief executive, Marguerite Ellison, presented a plan that included a $380 million acquisition of Delmar Flow Systems, a smaller rival. Delmar's standalone value, by the finance team's best estimate, was $300 million. The synergy case, built mainly on purchasing savings and cross-selling, was worth perhaps $40 million in present value. The price therefore exceeded everything the deal could plausibly deliver.

The debate that followed was revealing. The head of corporate development argued that Delmar would add 14% to revenue and make Northgate the second-largest firm in its niche. The chief operating officer pointed out that the deal would lift earnings per share in year one because Northgate could borrow cheaply. An independent director who had spent a career in private equity asked a different question: "What does a Northgate shareholder own after this transaction that she did not own before, and what did she pay for it?" The chief financial officer, Tomas Reyes, had prepared for exactly that question. He showed that Northgate would pay $80 million more than Delmar's standalone value, $40 million of it beyond any synergy, and that on a fair reading of the numbers the share price should fall by about $0.40.

Ellison's annual bonus was tied to revenue growth and to the size of the company relative to its peers. Reyes's was tied to earnings per share. Neither measure punished the board for overpaying. The independent director proposed that executive compensation and acquisition approval both be reframed around returns above the cost of the capital employed.

Nothing in this story is unusual. It contains almost every theme of this chapter: the objective of the firm, the gap between managers' interests and owners' interests, the machinery of governance that tries to close the gap, the link between finance and strategy, and the role of markets that will grade the decision within hours of its announcement. This chapter builds the framework within which the rest of the book operates.

## 1.1 What Corporate Finance Is, and Who Does It

### 1.1.1 The three decisions

Corporate finance is the study of how firms decide which real assets to acquire, how to pay for them, and how to return money to the people who supplied it. Every topic in this book, from capital budgeting to option pricing to mergers, is a refinement of one of three decisions.

1. **The investment decision (capital budgeting).** Which projects, products, acquisitions and assets should the firm commit resources to? This decision determines the left-hand side of the balance sheet: the operating assets the firm uses to generate cash flows. A manager who builds a plant, launches a brand, buys a competitor or invests in software is making an investment decision, whatever department she belongs to.
2. **The financing decision (capital structure).** How should the firm raise the money for those investments? The choices include retained earnings, new equity, bank loans, bonds, leases and hybrid securities. This decision determines the right-hand side of the balance sheet and the risk borne by each class of investor.
3. **The payout decision (dividend and repurchase policy).** When the firm generates more cash than it can profitably reinvest, how much should it return to shareholders, and in what form? Payout determines how much of the firm's cash flow stays inside the firm, under management's control, rather than going back to owners.

A fourth, operational theme underlies these: **working-capital and liquidity management**, the short-term decisions that keep the firm able to pay its bills while pursuing long-term goals. We treat it in detail later; Chapter 2 introduces the ratios that diagnose it.

The decisions interact. A firm cannot judge whether a project is worth doing without a view of the return investors require, and that return depends on how the project is financed and how risky its cash flows are. A firm that over-borrows may have to cancel good projects. A firm that hoards cash may invite bad ones. Teaching the three decisions separately is a pedagogical convenience; practising managers face them at once.

### 1.1.2 The organisation of the finance function

In a large corporation the finance function is typically led by a **chief financial officer (CFO)**, who reports to the chief executive and often sits on the board or attends its meetings. Beneath the CFO, responsibilities are usually divided between two broad roles.

- The **controller** (or chief accounting officer) is responsible for recording transactions, producing the financial statements, maintaining internal controls, managing tax compliance and supporting external audit. The controller looks backward and sideways: what happened, and is the record accurate?
- The **treasurer** is responsible for raising capital, managing cash and liquidity, handling banking relationships, managing foreign-exchange and interest-rate exposure and, often, investor relations. The treasurer looks forward and outward: how will we fund the plan, and what can go wrong with prices and markets?

Many firms add **financial planning and analysis (FP&A)**, a group that builds budgets, forecasts and variance analysis and supports business units; **corporate development**, which originates and evaluates mergers, acquisitions and divestitures; and **internal audit** and **risk management**, which should report with some independence from the CFO.

The modern CFO's role extends well beyond stewardship. Surveys of practice over recent decades describe CFOs as strategic partners to the chief executive: they shape portfolio decisions, communicate with investors and analysts, and challenge business-unit proposals. This is also where tension arises. A CFO who is too close to management may bless every proposal; a CFO who is too distant may be ignored. Part of the skill that this book aims to develop is the ability to ask the right question at the right moment, as the independent director did in the vignette.

### 1.1.3 Why non-financial leaders need finance

You do not need to become a CFO to need corporate finance. A marketing leader proposing a brand-building campaign is asking for capital that will pay back over several years; the proposal is an investment decision. An operations leader choosing between a flexible factory and a cheaper dedicated one is making a trade-off about fixed versus variable cost and operating leverage. A product leader negotiating a transfer price is influencing which division appears to create value. A start-up founder deciding how much equity to sell is making a financing decision with consequences for control. Fluency in the language of cash flows, discount rates and risk is the price of being taken seriously in resource-allocation debates.

## 1.2 Forms of Organisation and the Goal of the Firm

### 1.2.1 Why the corporation dominates large-scale business

Businesses are organised as sole proprietorships, partnerships, limited liability companies and corporations. Each trades off simplicity, taxation, liability and the ability to raise capital. The corporation's defining features explain its dominance among large firms.

- **Separate legal personality.** The corporation owns assets, signs contracts and sues or is sued in its own name. It outlives any individual owner.
- **Limited liability.** A shareholder can lose what she invested but, in general, no more. This caps downside risk, makes it rational to hold a diversified portfolio of many firms, and makes it possible for strangers to pool capital.
- **Transferable ownership.** Shares can be sold without the consent of other owners and without dissolving the firm. Liquidity in the shares lowers the return investors demand.
- **Centralised management.** Owners elect a board, which appoints managers. Specialised professionals can run the firm, and owners can hold many other things besides.

These features create the firm's strength and also its central problem. Pooling capital from thousands of dispersed owners makes large projects possible, but it separates **ownership** from **control**. The people who bear the financial consequences of decisions are not the people who make them. Adolf Berle and Gardiner Means described this separation in a classic study of the American corporation, and Michael Jensen and William Meckling formalised its consequences in 1976 in the theory of agency costs we develop in Section 1.3.

| Form | Liability of owners | Ease of raising large capital | Typical tax treatment | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited | Very low | Income taxed to the owner | Ends with the owner |
| General partnership | Unlimited, joint | Low | Pass-through to partners | Depends on partnership agreement |
| Limited liability company | Limited | Moderate | Flexible; often pass-through | Governed by operating agreement |
| Corporation | Limited | High | Corporate tax, then tax on distributions (jurisdiction-dependent) | Perpetual |

The tax row varies across countries and over time, so treat it as a pattern rather than a rule.

### 1.2.2 The objective: maximise the value of the equity

If the firm has multiple stakeholders and multiple conceivable goals, how should a manager choose? Corporate finance answers with a clear objective: **take the actions that maximise the current market value of the shareholders' equity**, subject to honouring contracts and obeying the law.

The logic rests on several arguments.

1. **Shareholders are the residual claimants.** Employees are paid wages, lenders interest, suppliers invoices, governments taxes; shareholders receive whatever is left. Because the residual is uncertain, shareholders bear the risk of every decision, and they benefit most when the decision is good. Giving them the decision rule aligns the person who gains from a good decision with the person who chose it.
2. **Value maximisation is a single, measurable criterion.** Competing goals, such as growth, market share, employee satisfaction and profit, cannot be traded off without a common currency. Market value is that currency. It incorporates the size, timing and risk of all future cash flows, a combination that no accounting number captures.
3. **Value is the surplus available for everyone.** A firm that creates more total value can, in principle, pay its employees and suppliers more or invest more in the community. A firm that destroys value will eventually have fewer resources for every constituency.
4. **Markets discipline the alternatives.** A firm that pursues other goals at the expense of value is vulnerable to takeover, activist investors and higher capital costs.

This is not the same as saying that managers should ignore other stakeholders. Treating customers, employees and suppliers well is usually a route to value, because their loyalty, effort and cooperation are productive assets. Environmental and social liabilities have real cash-flow consequences. A modern view of value maximisation is therefore long-horizon and attentive to stakeholder relationships. Where stakeholder commitments cost money without producing any compensating benefit, the manager is spending the owners' money and should be transparent that she is doing so, ideally with owner consent.

### 1.2.3 What value maximisation is not

Several common objectives sound similar to maximising value but lead to different decisions.

**Profit maximisation.** Accounting profit ignores the timing of cash flows, ignores risk, ignores the capital used to earn it and can be altered by accounting choices. A firm can raise this year's profit by cutting research and maintenance, stretching suppliers, or taking on projects whose returns fall below the cost of capital but above zero.

**Earnings-per-share (EPS) maximisation.** EPS rises when a firm acquires a target with a lower price-to-earnings ratio using its own more highly valued stock, or when it borrows to buy back shares, even if neither action creates any real value. EPS is also silent on risk: a leveraged firm's EPS is higher, but so is its volatility.

**Market-share or revenue growth.** Growth is valuable only if the return on the new investment exceeds the cost of capital. Section 1.5 shows that growth at a return below the cost of capital destroys value.

**Short-term share price maximisation.** The goal is the current price, which reflects expectations of the entire future. Taking actions that inflate the price today at the expense of the long run is not value maximisation, and informed markets tend to see through it. The key phrase is *current* value, which already discounts long-run effects.

**Example 1.1 – Value maximisation versus accounting profit.** Hartwell Components, a fictional firm, has 20 million shares outstanding. It considers a project requiring a $40 million outlay today. The project will generate cash flows of $14 million at the end of each of the next five years. For simplicity assume no taxes, and depreciation of $8 million per year on a straight-line basis. The firm's cost of capital for projects of this risk is 10%.

*Step 1 – Accounting view.* Annual accounting profit is cash flow minus depreciation: 14 − 8 = $6 million. Average book investment is (40 + 0) / 2 = $20 million, so the accounting return on average investment is 6 / 20 = 30%. By this measure the project looks outstanding.

*Step 2 – Present value of the cash flows.* The five-year annuity factor at 10% is [1 − 1.10^−5] / 0.10 = 3.7908. Present value = 14 × 3.7908 = $53.07 million.

*Step 3 – Net present value.* NPV = 53.07 − 40 = **$13.07 million**.

*Step 4 – Effect on shareholders.* If the market recognises the project's value, the share price rises by 13.07 / 20 = **$0.65 per share**.

*Step 5 – Internal rate of return.* The discount rate that makes NPV zero is found by trial: at 22.1%, 14 × annuity factor = 40, so IRR ≈ **22.1%**.

*Interpretation.* The accounting return of 30% overstates the true rate of return because it divides by *average* book value and ignores the time value of money. The project is nevertheless worth doing, and the proper measure of how worth doing is the NPV of $13.07 million, the expected increase in shareholder wealth. Accounting returns can also point the wrong way: a project whose cash arrives late can show a high accounting return yet have a negative NPV, while a project with modest early profits but a large terminal value can be strongly positive. The decision rule that aligns with shareholder wealth is: accept projects with NPV greater than zero.

### 1.2.4 The stakeholder debate

Critics argue that maximising shareholder value is too narrow and encourages short-termism, inequality and environmental harm. Defenders reply that stakeholder maximisation without a priority gives managers no usable decision rule and no accountability: a manager who is responsible to everyone is accountable to no one. Both sides have a point, and a useful synthesis exists.

- **Enlightened value maximisation** (sometimes called enlightened stakeholder theory) holds that a firm cannot maximise long-run value if it ignores the interests of important constituencies, but also insists that managers keep value as the tiebreaker when interests conflict.
- Where the law or contracts protect stakeholders, as with workplace safety, environmental rules and creditor rights, the manager treats those protections as constraints within which she maximises value.
- Where the firm voluntarily goes beyond the law, the manager should be able to articulate how the choice affects long-run cash flows (reputation, recruitment, regulatory risk, customer loyalty) or that owners have accepted the cost for non-financial reasons.
- Some corporate forms, including public-benefit corporations, write a broader purpose into the charter explicitly. This resolves the accountability problem by changing the contract rather than ignoring it.

For the purposes of this book, the operating objective is long-run equity value, interpreted with attention to stakeholder relationships and externalities. It is the criterion against which investment, financing and payout decisions are tested in later chapters.

## 1.3 Agency Problems

### 1.3.1 The principal-agent relationship

An **agency relationship** exists whenever one party (the principal) delegates decision-making authority to another (the agent) whose interests differ. Shareholders are principals and managers are agents. The difficulty arises because (a) interests diverge, (b) the principal cannot observe everything the agent does or knows, and (c) the agent's actions affect the principal's wealth. Jensen and Meckling defined **agency costs** as the sum of three elements:

1. **Monitoring costs** borne by the principal to limit the agent's deviant behaviour (audits, boards, reporting requirements).
2. **Bonding costs** borne by the agent to assure the principal that she will behave (contractual commitments, certified accounts, restrictive covenants).
3. **Residual loss**, the decline in the principal's welfare that remains even after monitoring and bonding, because perfect alignment costs more than it is worth.

Agency theory does not require anyone to be dishonest. Rational, honest managers can still prefer larger firms, quieter lives, and safer paths than diversified owners would choose, and they can persuade themselves that these preferences are the owners' interest.

### 1.3.2 Shareholders and managers

The classic manager-shareholder conflicts include the following.

- **Perquisite consumption and shirking.** Executives may spend firm resources on luxuries or exert less effort than owners would wish, because they bear only a fraction of the cost.
- **Empire building.** Managers' pay, status and security often rise with firm size, which gives them an incentive to grow beyond the point where growth adds value. Acquisitions are the prime vehicle.
- **Risk aversion.** A manager's wealth is concentrated in her job and her reputation, while shareholders can diversify. She may therefore reject positive-NPV but risky projects, preferring the quiet life.
- **Horizon problems.** A manager nearing retirement has less incentive to invest in projects that pay off after she leaves, such as research and development, while one with a short-term bonus may boost near-term earnings at the expense of the long term.
- **Entrenchment.** Managers may adopt strategies that make them hard to replace, such as investing in assets that depend on their special skills, resisting takeovers, or packing the board with allies.
- **Free cash flow.** Jensen's free-cash-flow hypothesis argues that when a firm generates cash beyond its profitable investment opportunities, managers tend to retain and misspend it rather than return it. High leverage and payouts discipline this behaviour by committing cash to outsiders.

**Example 1.2 – The price of empire building.** Return to the Northgate acquisition from the opening vignette. Northgate has 100 million shares and a market capitalisation of $1,200 million before the deal (so $12.00 per share). Delmar's standalone value is $300 million, the present value of synergies is $40 million, and Northgate would pay $380 million in cash.

*Step 1 – Value created by the combination.* Standalone value of the target plus synergies: 300 + 40 = $340 million.

*Step 2 – NPV to the acquirer.* NPV = value acquired − price paid = 340 − 380 = **−$40 million**.

*Step 3 – Premium.* The premium over standalone value is 380 / 300 − 1 = 26.7%. Because synergies are only 40 / 300 = 13.3% of standalone value, the acquirer is paying away all the synergies and a further $40 million of its own shareholders' wealth.

*Step 4 – Effect on the share price.* Equity value after the deal, if the market is efficient and believes the estimates: 1,200 + 340 − 380 = $1,160 million. Share price = 1,160 / 100 = **$11.60**, a fall of $0.40 (3.3%).

*Step 5 – Who gains?* Delmar's shareholders receive $380 million for something worth $300 million standalone: a gain of $80 million. Of that, $40 million is their share of synergies they did not create alone and $40 million is a transfer from Northgate's owners.

*Interpretation.* The deal enlarges Northgate and may raise EPS, but it transfers $40 million of wealth from Northgate's shareholders to Delmar's. A manager rewarded on revenue and EPS has no reason to refuse; a manager rewarded on value creation would. The empirical finding that acquirers' shareholders earn around zero or slightly negative returns on average, while targets gain substantially, is consistent with this pattern, although the average hides many good deals.

### 1.3.3 The cost of outside equity: a Jensen-Meckling illustration

Jensen and Meckling's insight can be seen in a simple model. Suppose an owner-manager runs a firm and consumes perquisites. When she owns 100% of the firm, each dollar of perks costs her a dollar of firm value, so she consumes only until the marginal benefit of perks equals their cost. When she sells part of the firm to outsiders, she bears only her ownership fraction of each perquisite dollar, and she will consume more.

**Example 1.3 – Selling equity to outsiders.** An entrepreneur owns 100% of a firm worth $1,000 million if run with no excess perquisites. She sells 40% to outside investors and keeps 60%. After the sale she will consume extra perquisites costing the firm $80 million of value, but each dollar of perquisites is worth only $0.50 to her. Investors understand this and price the shares accordingly.

*Step 1 – Value of the firm after perquisites.* 1,000 − 80 = $920 million.

*Step 2 – Price rational outsiders pay for 40%.* 0.40 × 920 = **$368 million**. They do not pay 0.40 × 1,000 = $400 million because they anticipate the perquisites.

*Step 3 – The entrepreneur's wealth.* She holds cash of $368 million, a 60% stake worth 0.60 × 920 = $552 million, and perquisites worth to her 0.50 × 80 = $40 million. Total = 368 + 552 + 40 = **$960 million**.

*Step 4 – Her wealth if she could commit to no perquisites.* She would sell the 40% for $400 million and keep a 60% stake worth $600 million, total **$1,000 million**.

*Step 5 – The agency cost.* The difference, 1,000 − 960 = **$40 million**, is the residual loss. It is borne by the entrepreneur herself, because outsiders paid a fair price. The firm's value fell by $80 million, but she recovered $40 million through the perks she values; the net deadweight loss is $40 million.

*Interpretation.* The agent bears the cost of her own lack of commitment, so she has an incentive to invest in bonding and monitoring: independent audits, a board with outside members, a perquisite policy in the bylaws. Each such device is worth adopting as long as it costs less than the loss it removes. This is why firms voluntarily subject themselves to external audit and why public-company governance rules are generally acceptable to issuers, though the optimal intensity varies by firm.

### 1.3.4 Shareholders and creditors

Debt introduces a second conflict. Creditors have a fixed claim and receive no extra reward from success, but absorb losses if the firm fails. Shareholders, who hold the residual, benefit disproportionately when risk rises. Equity can be viewed as a call option on the firm's assets, with the face value of debt as the exercise price, and an option's value increases with the volatility of the underlying asset. This leads to three classical problems.

- **Asset substitution (risk shifting).** After raising debt, the firm switches to riskier projects, shifting value from creditors to shareholders.
- **Underinvestment (debt overhang).** A highly indebted firm may reject positive-NPV projects because the benefits would accrue largely to creditors.
- **Claim dilution and payout.** The firm may issue more debt of equal or higher priority, or pay large dividends or repurchases, leaving less for existing creditors.

Creditors anticipate these behaviours and protect themselves through higher interest rates, covenants, collateral, shorter maturities and seniority. The costs of the conflict thus return to shareholders as a higher cost of debt.

**Example 1.4 – Asset substitution.** A firm has debt with a face value of $100 million due in one year, and it must choose between two projects that each require the same investment. The asset values at the end of the year are:

- Project A (safe): worth $120 million with certainty.
- Project B (risky): worth $190 million with probability 50%, and $30 million with probability 50%.

*Step 1 – Expected value.* A: 120. B: 0.5 × 190 + 0.5 × 30 = **$110 million**. Project A is worth $10 million more to the firm as a whole.

*Step 2 – Equity under A.* Debt is repaid in full, so equity = 120 − 100 = **$20 million**.

*Step 3 – Equity under B.* In the good state, equity = 190 − 100 = 90. In the bad state, the firm is worth 30, below the debt, so equity gets 0 (limited liability). Expected equity = 0.5 × 90 + 0.5 × 0 = **$45 million**.

*Step 4 – Creditors under B.* Good state: 100. Bad state: 30. Expected = 0.5 × 100 + 0.5 × 30 = **$65 million**, versus $100 million under A.

*Step 5 – Check.* 45 + 65 = 110, the total expected value of B.

*Interpretation.* Shareholders prefer the inferior project B because their expected payoff rises from $20 million to $45 million, even though total value falls by $10 million. Creditors lose $35 million in expected value. Shareholders gain $25 million, creditors lose $35 million, and the net loss of $10 million is the cost of the conflict. If lenders foresee this, they will demand covenants or a higher interest rate before lending. This is a central reason why highly leveraged firms in volatile industries face tighter contracts.

### 1.3.5 Controlling and minority shareholders

In many countries, including much of Europe, Asia and Latin America, large firms are not widely held. A founding family, a state, a bank or a business group holds a controlling stake. The main conflict then is not between dispersed owners and managers but between **controlling** and **minority** shareholders. Controlling holders may extract private benefits through related-party transactions, transfer pricing within a business group, dual-class share structures that separate votes from cash-flow rights, or dilutive issuance. The legal protection of minority shareholders, the quality of disclosure and the independence of directors are therefore crucial in these settings. A manager valuing a controlled company, or investing alongside a controlling shareholder, should adjust for the risk that value will not reach minority investors.

### 1.3.6 Information asymmetry

A related friction is **information asymmetry**: managers know more about the firm's prospects than outside investors do. Akerlof's analysis of markets with hidden quality, and later Myers and Majluf, showed that this affects financing choices. If managers know the stock is overvalued, they have an incentive to sell it, and so investors treat a new equity issue as bad news; the share price tends to fall on announcement. Firms respond by preferring internal funds, then debt, then equity (the pecking-order theory), and by using costly signals, such as dividends or insider ownership, to convey confidence. We return to these ideas in the chapters on capital structure and payout policy.

## 1.4 Corporate Governance

**Corporate governance** is the system of rules, practices and institutions by which a firm is directed and controlled, and in particular the mechanisms that reduce agency costs. No single mechanism works everywhere; they substitute for and complement one another.

### 1.4.1 The board of directors

The board is the owners' first line of defence. It appoints, evaluates and removes the chief executive, approves strategy, major investments and financing, oversees risk and the integrity of financial reporting, and sets executive compensation. Its effectiveness depends on several characteristics.

- **Independence.** Directors with no material ties to management are more likely to challenge proposals. Independence is not a guarantee, as friendly outsiders can be as compliant as insiders, but it is a precondition for challenge.
- **Expertise and time.** Directors need financial and industry knowledge, and enough time to read the materials. Overcommitted directors are weak monitors.
- **Information.** Management controls the information flow. Strong boards demand direct access to the CFO, internal audit and external auditors, and meet without management present.
- **Structure.** Separation of the roles of chair and chief executive is common in many jurisdictions and hotly debated in others. Audit, compensation and nomination committees staffed by independent directors are standard in listed companies.
- **Incentives.** Directors who hold meaningful equity have a stake in outcomes; those paid mainly by fees do not.

### 1.4.2 Executive compensation

Pay is the most direct tool for aligning interests, and also a source of problems when badly designed. Typical components are base salary, an annual bonus, long-term incentives such as restricted stock and stock options, and benefits including pensions and severance.

**Principles of good design.**

- *Pay for performance the executive controls and that matters to owners.* Measures should track value creation, such as economic profit or total shareholder return relative to peers, rather than size or accounting measures that can be manipulated.
- *Match the horizon.* Vesting periods and clawbacks should extend beyond the point at which the consequences of decisions become visible.
- *Avoid rewarding luck.* Raw share-price-based pay rises with the market tide. Relative performance measures and indexed options filter out market-wide movements.
- *Avoid distorting risk.* Options have convex payoffs: they reward upside and cost nothing on the downside, so an option-heavy package can encourage excess risk, much like the shareholder's position in Example 1.4. Restricted stock, in contrast, exposes the manager to losses too.
- *Manage manipulation.* Any metric that triggers a bonus creates a reason to manage the metric.

**Example 1.5 – What a stock option actually pays.** An executive receives 200,000 at-the-money options with a strike price of $40 per share. Her annual bonus also equals 0.5% of net income, and net income is $120 million.

*Step 1 – Bonus.* 0.005 × 120,000,000 = **$600,000**.

*Step 2 – Option payoff if the share price at exercise is $55.* Intrinsic value per option = 55 − 40 = $15. Total = 200,000 × 15 = **$3,000,000**.

*Step 3 – Option payoff if the share price is $35.* Intrinsic value = max(35 − 40, 0) = 0. Total = **$0**.

*Step 4 – Asymmetry.* A share price rise of $15 earns her $3.0 million; a fall of $5 costs her nothing out of pocket. A shareholder holding the same 200,000 shares would have gained $3.0 million in the first case and lost $1.0 million (200,000 × 5) in the second.

*Interpretation.* The option holder faces only upside, so she may favour riskier strategies than a shareholder would. Boards often counter this by granting restricted stock, requiring share-ownership guidelines, and including downside-sensitive measures such as returns on capital in the performance mix. The bonus formula tied to net income, meanwhile, rewards profit regardless of the capital used to earn it, the same weakness that makes profit an imperfect objective.

### 1.4.3 Creditors, lenders and debt discipline

Creditors monitor firms through covenants, reporting requirements and the discipline of refinancing. Bank loans, in particular, involve a lender with expertise in monitoring borrowers and the power to renegotiate. Debt service also commits the firm's cash flow, which restrains free-cash-flow waste. The cost is that excessive debt creates distress risk and the conflicts in Section 1.3.4. Capital structure is therefore a governance decision as well as a financing one.

### 1.4.4 The market for corporate control

When internal governance fails, the firm's equity is likely to trade below its potential value, which creates an opportunity for an outsider. Hostile takeovers, proxy contests and activist campaigns are the mechanisms. The threat can discipline managers even when no bid occurs. Defences, such as poison pills, staggered boards and super-voting shares, protect incumbents. They can serve shareholders by giving the board bargaining power against a low-ball offer, or serve managers by entrenching them; the evidence is mixed, and which effect dominates depends on the quality of the board that wields the defence.

Activist investors have become a prominent form of governance. They accumulate a stake, propose changes to strategy, capital allocation or leadership, and seek support from other shareholders. Institutional investors, such as pension funds and index funds, are increasingly important voters and engage with management through voting, dialogue and stewardship codes.

### 1.4.5 Law, regulation and disclosure

Securities law, stock-exchange listing rules, accounting standards, external audit and enforcement shape the environment in which all other mechanisms operate. Disclosure requirements reduce information asymmetry. Fiduciary duties, which in common-law systems include duties of care and loyalty owed by directors, and anti-fraud provisions give shareholders legal recourse. Following major accounting scandals in the early 2000s and the financial crisis of 2007-2009, many jurisdictions strengthened requirements on internal control, auditor independence, audit committee composition and executive accountability, and introduced say-on-pay votes. The effectiveness of any rule depends on enforcement, and the quality of legal protection differs substantially between countries.

| Mechanism | What it does | Main strength | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent board | Monitors and removes management | Close, continuous oversight | Information dependence on management; social ties |
| Incentive pay | Aligns managers' wealth with owners' | Directly rewards value creation | Can be gamed; encourages risk; rewards luck |
| Debt and covenants | Commits cash flow; creditors monitor | Hard constraint on waste | Distress costs; creditor-shareholder conflicts |
| Takeovers and activism | External challenge to poor performance | Works when internal controls fail | Costly, uncertain; defences blunt it |
| Law and disclosure | Sets minimum standards; enables recourse | Universal, baseline protection | Depends on enforcement; compliance cost |
| Reputation and labour market | Managers' careers depend on past results | Cheap and automatic | Weak near retirement or in a boom |

## 1.5 Finance and Strategy

### 1.5.1 Strategy creates value only if returns exceed the cost of capital

Strategy is about choosing where to compete and how to win. Finance supplies the test of whether the choice pays. A strategy creates value when the firm earns, on the capital it commits, a return greater than the return investors could earn elsewhere on investments of similar risk. This leads to the key measures.

**Return on invested capital (ROIC)** measures operating profit after tax relative to the capital invested in operations:

```
ROIC = NOPAT / Invested Capital
NOPAT = EBIT × (1 − tax rate)
Invested Capital = Operating working capital + Net fixed assets + Other operating assets
              (equivalently, Debt + Equity − Excess cash)
```

**Economic profit** (also known as economic value added, or EVA, a trademark of Stern Stewart, or residual income) measures the dollar surplus after charging for all capital, including equity:

```
Economic Profit = NOPAT − WACC × Invested Capital
                = (ROIC − WACC) × Invested Capital
```

where WACC is the weighted average cost of capital, the blended return required by debt and equity holders. We derive WACC in a later chapter; for now treat it as the opportunity cost of capital.

A business unit with a positive spread (ROIC − WACC) creates value; one with a negative spread destroys value even if it reports an accounting profit. Accounting net income deducts interest but not the cost of equity, which is why a firm can appear profitable while failing to earn what its owners could have earned elsewhere.

**Example 1.6 – Economic profit by division.** Orion Diversified, a fictional conglomerate, reports the following for three divisions. WACC differs by division because the risk of their businesses differs.

| Division | NOPAT ($m) | Invested capital ($m) | WACC | ROIC | Capital charge ($m) | Economic profit ($m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aerospace | 96 | 800 | 9% | 12.0% | 72 | +24 |
| Marine | 130 | 1,000 | 10% | 13.0% | 100 | +30 |
| Retail | 54 | 600 | 9% | 9.0% | 54 | 0 |

*Step 1 – ROIC.* Aerospace: 96 / 800 = 12.0%. Marine: 130 / 1,000 = 13.0%. Retail: 54 / 600 = 9.0%.

*Step 2 – Capital charge = WACC × invested capital.* Aerospace: 0.09 × 800 = 72. Marine: 0.10 × 1,000 = 100. Retail: 0.09 × 600 = 54.

*Step 3 – Economic profit = NOPAT − capital charge.* Aerospace: 96 − 72 = **24**. Marine: 130 − 100 = **30**. Retail: 54 − 54 = **0**.

*Step 4 – Interpretation.* Marine has the highest ROIC and the highest economic profit. Aerospace has a lower ROIC than Marine, but both earn the same 3-point spread over their own costs of capital (12% − 9% and 13% − 10%); Marine's larger capital base makes its dollar economic profit larger. Retail earns exactly its cost of capital: it is profitable in accounting terms (NOPAT of $54 million) but creates no value for owners. The group's total economic profit is 24 + 30 + 0 = **$54 million**.

*Management implication.* Capital should flow to businesses where the spread is positive and the growth opportunity is real. The Retail division deserves a hard look: either find a way to raise its returns, or release its $600 million of capital to better uses. Note that comparing ROICs without regard to WACC would have misled: Marine's 13% looks superior, but it is the riskier business with a higher cost of capital, and its spread over that cost is the same 3 points as Aerospace's.

### 1.5.2 The value-driver formula: growth is not always good

A useful result links value to three drivers: the level of profit, the growth rate and the return on new investment. For a firm with next year's NOPAT, perpetual growth rate g and return on new invested capital RONIC, the value of operations is:

```
Value of operations = NOPAT₁ × (1 − g / RONIC) / (WACC − g)
```

The term (1 − g / RONIC) is the fraction of NOPAT that need not be reinvested: to grow at rate g with a return RONIC on new capital, the firm must reinvest g / RONIC of its profits, and the remainder is free cash flow. The formula is the constant-growth perpetuity applied to free cash flow, and it holds for g < WACC.

Three cases follow directly.

- If RONIC = WACC, the formula collapses to NOPAT₁ / WACC regardless of g. Growth at the cost of capital creates no value.
- If RONIC > WACC, higher growth increases value.
- If RONIC < WACC, higher growth reduces value.

**Example 1.7 – When does growth help?** A business has next-year NOPAT of $96 million and WACC of 9%. We examine its value under different growth and return assumptions.

*Step 1 – Value with no growth (g = 0).* FCF = NOPAT because no reinvestment is required, so value = 96 / 0.09 = **$1,066.7 million**, regardless of RONIC.

*Step 2 – g = 4%, RONIC = 12%.* Reinvestment rate = 0.04 / 0.12 = 33.3%. Free cash flow = 96 × (1 − 0.3333) = 64. Value = 64 / (0.09 − 0.04) = 64 / 0.05 = **$1,280.0 million**.

*Step 3 – g = 6%, RONIC = 12%.* Reinvestment rate = 0.06 / 0.12 = 50%. FCF = 48. Value = 48 / 0.03 = **$1,600.0 million**.

*Step 4 – g = 4%, RONIC = 9% (equal to WACC).* Reinvestment rate = 0.04 / 0.09 = 44.4%. FCF = 96 × 0.5556 = 53.33. Value = 53.33 / 0.05 = **$1,066.7 million**, the same as with no growth.

*Step 5 – g = 4%, RONIC = 6% (below WACC).* Reinvestment rate = 0.04 / 0.06 = 66.7%. FCF = 96 × 0.3333 = 32. Value = 32 / 0.05 = **$640.0 million**.

| Scenario | g | RONIC | Reinvestment rate | FCF ($m) | Value ($m) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| No growth | 0% | any | 0% | 96.0 | 1,066.7 |
| Value-creating growth | 4% | 12% | 33.3% | 64.0 | 1,280.0 |
| Faster value-creating growth | 6% | 12% | 50.0% | 48.0 | 1,600.0 |
| Growth at cost of capital | 4% | 9% | 44.4% | 53.3 | 1,066.7 |
| Value-destroying growth | 4% | 6% | 66.7% | 32.0 | 640.0 |

*Interpretation.* The same 4% growth is worth +$213 million in one case, nothing in another, and −$427 million in a third. The question is never "how fast can we grow?" but "what do we earn on the capital that growth requires?" This is the mathematical form of the warning in Section 1.2.3 against growth and market-share objectives, and it explains why strategists who care about value spend as much effort on the sources of returns as on the sources of growth.

### 1.5.3 Sources of returns above the cost of capital

In a competitive economy, returns above the cost of capital attract entry and erode. Persistent positive spreads require some form of **competitive advantage**: cost leadership based on scale or process, differentiation through brand or technology, switching costs, network effects, regulatory protection or privileged access to inputs. A finance leader tests a strategy by asking three questions. What is the source of the advantage? How long will it last, given competitors' likely response? How much capital must be tied up to exploit it? The answers become the explicit forecasting assumptions about margin, growth and capital intensity in a valuation.

### 1.5.4 The CFO as strategic partner

Finance contributes to strategy in four main ways. First, **portfolio management**: deciding which businesses to invest in, harvest or divest, using ROIC, growth and risk. Second, **capital allocation**: setting hurdle rates, deciding the pace of investment and the balance between reinvesting, paying down debt and returning cash. Third, **financial flexibility**: maintaining the debt capacity and liquidity to seize opportunities and survive shocks. Fourth, **communication**: explaining to investors why the strategy will create value, so that the share price reflects the firm's real prospects. A strategy no investor can understand is priced at a discount.

## 1.6 Financial Markets and Institutions

### 1.6.1 What financial markets do

Financial markets channel savings from households and institutions with surplus funds to firms and governments with productive uses for them. In doing so they perform four functions.

1. **Resource allocation.** Prices and expected returns guide capital to its most productive uses.
2. **Risk sharing.** Securities with different risk and return profiles allow investors to hold the risk they prefer and diversify the rest.
3. **Liquidity.** Secondary markets let investors convert holdings to cash, which lowers the return they require from issuers.
4. **Information and price discovery.** Prices aggregate information from many participants and provide a continuous signal about the value of corporate decisions.

### 1.6.2 Classifying markets

| Dimension | Categories | Examples and notes |
|---|---|---|
| Maturity | Money market (up to one year) versus capital market (longer) | Treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit versus bonds and equities |
| Issue stage | Primary (new securities sold by the issuer) versus secondary (trading among investors) | Initial public offerings and bond issues versus exchange trading |
| Claim type | Debt versus equity versus derivatives | Loans and bonds; common and preferred stock; options, futures, swaps |
| Trading structure | Exchange-traded (centralised, standardised) versus over-the-counter (negotiated, dealer-based) | Listed shares versus many bonds and swaps |
| Participation | Public (open offering, regulated disclosure) versus private (limited investors, fewer disclosures) | Listed shares versus private placements and private equity |

The **primary market** is where the firm receives cash; it matters directly to corporate finance. An initial public offering (IPO) converts a private company into a public one; a seasoned equity offering issues more shares of an already listed company; a bond issue sells debt securities. Investment banks typically underwrite and distribute these issues, advising on pricing and timing, and often committing their own capital to buy any unsold portion. The **secondary market** does not provide new money to the issuer, but it is essential because it gives primary-market investors liquidity and creates the price that the board sees every day.

### 1.6.3 Financial intermediaries and institutions

Many savers do not lend directly to firms. **Intermediaries** such as banks, insurers, pension funds, mutual funds and exchange-traded funds stand between them, adding value through diversification, maturity transformation, screening and monitoring, and economies of scale in information and transactions. Banks take deposits and make loans, bearing credit and liquidity risk. Insurers pool risk. Asset managers aggregate savings into diversified portfolios; their voting power makes them important governance actors. **Credit-rating agencies** assess the creditworthiness of issuers, and their ratings shape borrowing costs and covenants. **Regulators and central banks** supervise the system, set the policy interest rate and act as lenders of last resort in crises. The "shadow banking" sector, comprising non-bank lenders and market-based credit, performs bank-like functions with different regulation.

The structure of financing differs across countries. Economies such as the United States rely heavily on capital markets for corporate funding; others, such as Germany and Japan, have traditionally relied more on bank lending, though markets have grown in importance. For a firm, the practical implication is that the menu of financing options and the relationship with providers vary by location and size, and a leader needs to know which markets are open to her business.

### 1.6.4 Market efficiency

An **efficient market** is one in which prices fully reflect available information. Eugene Fama classified efficiency into three forms according to the information set.

- **Weak form:** prices reflect all past trading information, so technical analysis of price patterns cannot generate abnormal returns.
- **Semi-strong form:** prices reflect all public information, so fundamental analysis of published data cannot generate abnormal returns, and prices adjust quickly to new announcements.
- **Strong form:** prices reflect all information, including private information, so even insiders cannot earn abnormal returns (a form widely rejected, and one that insider-trading laws presuppose to be false).

Evidence broadly supports reasonably quick price adjustment to public news in developed markets, while documenting anomalies and episodes, such as speculative bubbles, that challenge the strictest interpretations. The behavioural finance literature, associated with Kahneman, Tversky, Shiller, Thaler and others, argues that cognitive biases and limits to arbitrage can sustain mispricing. A balanced position for managers is this: the market price is the best available single estimate of value in most circumstances; it can be wrong, sometimes badly, but a manager cannot predict when, and should not base long-term strategy on the belief that she can outguess it. The implication for corporate finance is that, in the main, a decision that increases true value will, once understood, be reflected in the price.

### 1.6.5 Reading the market's verdict: event studies

Efficiency suggests a way to measure how the market evaluates a corporate decision: look at the share price reaction around the announcement. Because the stock also moves with the market, we measure the **abnormal return**, the actual return minus the return expected given the market's movement. The simplest tool is the market model:

```
Expected return on day t = α + β × (market return on day t)
Abnormal return (AR) = Actual return − Expected return
Cumulative abnormal return (CAR) = Σ AR over the event window
```

**Example 1.8 – An event study of an acquisition announcement.** Meridian Foods, a fictional firm with a market capitalisation of $2,500 million, announces the acquisition of a regional competitor. Using a market model with α = 0 and β = 1.2 estimated from prior data, the following daily returns were observed (in percent):

| Day | Meridian actual return | Market return | Expected return (1.2 × market) | Abnormal return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Announcement day (0) | 4.5 | 1.0 | 1.2 | 3.3 |
| Day +1 | 1.0 | −0.5 | −0.6 | 1.6 |
| Day +2 | −0.2 | 0.3 | 0.36 | −0.56 |

*Step 1 – Expected returns.* Day 0: 1.2 × 1.0 = 1.20%. Day 1: 1.2 × (−0.5) = −0.60%. Day 2: 1.2 × 0.3 = 0.36%.

*Step 2 – Abnormal returns.* Day 0: 4.5 − 1.20 = **3.30%**. Day 1: 1.0 − (−0.60) = **1.60%**. Day 2: −0.2 − 0.36 = **−0.56%**.

*Step 3 – Cumulative abnormal return over the three days.* 3.30 + 1.60 − 0.56 = **4.34%**.

*Step 4 – Dollar value.* 0.0434 × 2,500 = **$108.5 million** of value attributed to the announcement.

*Interpretation.* The market views the acquisition as creating about $108 million for Meridian's shareholders, more than the price paid for the target would suggest as a pure transfer. Such evidence is powerful for managers because it reveals the market's assessment of both the deal and the credibility of the team announcing it. It has limits. The abnormal return includes anything else announced at the same time; the market model relies on estimated parameters; and the price reaction reflects expectations about how the deal will be executed, not realised outcomes. But the method is widely used in research and in litigation, and it illustrates why a board should ask not only "what is the NPV?" but also "how will the market read this?".

## 1.7 Ethics, Responsibility and the Road Ahead

### 1.7.1 Ethics as a financial issue

Agency problems and ethical failures are close relatives. Accounting manipulations, misleading disclosures, insider trading, mis-selling of products and bribery all use the information advantages of insiders at the expense of outsiders, and each has contributed to spectacular corporate collapses. Ethics is also a matter of self-interest. A firm's reputation for honesty is an asset that lowers the cost of capital, because investors will pay more for a firm whose numbers they trust. A single scandal can destroy years of accumulated trust; the share-price drop on revelation of fraud typically exceeds the direct legal penalty, because the market is repricing the credibility of everything the firm has ever said.

Finance leaders face particular pressures: targets that incentivise earnings management, the temptation to treat the quarter as the horizon, and requests from colleagues to adjust assumptions to justify a preferred outcome. A practical guard is to establish in advance, as the independent director in the Northgate vignette did, the criteria by which an investment will be judged, so that proposals are tested against standards that were set before the temptation arose. Another is to separate the people who propose projects from the people who evaluate them, and to review a sample of past decisions against actual outcomes (post-audits).

### 1.7.2 The architecture of the book

The remaining chapters build the toolkit this chapter has motivated. We begin by learning to read the firm's financial statements and to compute the cash flows that valuation depends on (Chapter 2). We then develop the time value of money, the valuation of bonds and equities, the measurement of risk and return, the cost of capital, capital-budgeting techniques, capital-structure and payout theory, and the application of these tools in mergers, international finance and risk management. At each stage the question is the one the independent director asked at the Northgate board table: what does the shareholder own after this decision that she did not own before, and what did she pay for it?

## Case Study: The Westbrook Packaging Board Room

*Illustrative case. Westbrook Packaging is a fictional company.*

Westbrook Packaging is a listed manufacturer of corrugated and flexible packaging, with annual revenue of about $3.1 billion, 11,000 employees and plants in nine countries. Founded by the Westbrook family, it remains 18% family-owned, with the remainder held by institutions and the public. Chief executive Dana Okafor has held the role for eleven years. Her tenure has been marked by steady expansion: revenue has doubled through eight acquisitions. Return on invested capital, however, has drifted from 11.5% to 8.1% over the same period, against an estimated weighted average cost of capital of 8.8%.

The compensation committee, which is chaired by a long-serving director who was Okafor's mentor, sets the annual bonus using three equally weighted measures: revenue growth, adjusted EBITDA growth and adjusted EPS. The long-term plan awards stock options with a three-year vesting period, struck at the grant-date price. Adjusted EBITDA excludes restructuring costs, which have been recorded in each of the last six years. The board has nine members. Five are classified as independent, but three of those have served for more than twelve years and two have business relationships with the company through consulting contracts. Okafor serves as both chair and chief executive.

Westbrook now has a proposal before it. A private flexible-packaging company, Tarrant Films, is for sale at an enterprise value of $900 million, which is 10.5 times its EBITDA. Management's analysis projects annual synergies with a present value of $70 million. Independent valuation work by the treasury team, using conservative growth and margin assumptions and Tarrant's cost of capital, places Tarrant's standalone enterprise value at $780 million. Financing would be a mix of new debt and new shares. The combined company would carry net debt of about 3.6 times EBITDA, up from 2.4 times, which would put it near the limit of its bank covenants. The treasurer has also warned that the rating agencies could place the company on negative watch.

A large institutional investor holding 6% of the shares has written to the board. It notes that ROIC has been below the cost of capital for four years, that bonus measures do not penalise capital misallocation, and that the shares trade at a discount to peers. It requests an independent chair, a bonus measure based on returns on capital, and a pause on acquisitions until a portfolio review has been completed. It has hinted at a campaign at the next annual meeting.

Okafor argues that the Tarrant deal will make Westbrook the leading flexible-packaging producer in two regions and that a pause would hand the asset to a competitor. The CFO, Aisha Rahman, privately shares the investor's concerns but also knows her own bonus is linked to EPS, which the deal would raise in year one because of the accretion from leveraged financing. The family representative on the board supports the deal and is wary of an activist campaign. Two independent directors have asked for a second opinion from an outside adviser.

## Key Takeaways

- Corporate finance addresses three linked decisions, investment, financing and payout, and the quality of each is judged by its effect on the value of the firm's equity.
- The operating objective is the maximisation of long-run equity value. This differs from maximising accounting profit, EPS, revenue or market share, none of which accounts for the time value of money, risk and the capital used.
- Net present value is the measure of the change in shareholder wealth from an investment; accounting returns and payback rules can mislead.
- Separation of ownership and control creates agency problems: perquisites, empire building, excess risk aversion, short horizons and entrenchment between managers and shareholders; asset substitution and underinvestment between shareholders and creditors; and private-benefit extraction by controlling over minority holders.
- Agency costs are the sum of monitoring costs, bonding costs and residual loss. Rational parties adopt monitoring and bonding to the point where the marginal cost equals the marginal reduction in loss.
- Governance works through a combination of an independent and informed board, well-designed incentive pay, disciplined use of debt, the market for corporate control, and law and disclosure. No single device suffices, and each has typical failure modes.
- Strategy creates value only when the return on invested capital exceeds the cost of capital. Growth at a return equal to the cost of capital adds nothing, and growth at a lower return destroys value.
- Economic profit, NOPAT minus a charge for all capital, aligns divisional performance measurement and executive pay with value creation more closely than net income or EPS.
- Financial markets allocate capital, share risk, provide liquidity and generate prices that reveal how investors judge corporate decisions; event studies convert that judgement into a measurable abnormal return.
- Ethical conduct and transparent reporting reduce the cost of capital, while manipulation and fraud transfer wealth from outsiders to insiders and destroy the trust on which the cost of capital depends.
