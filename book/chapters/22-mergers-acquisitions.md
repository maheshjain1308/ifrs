# Chapter 22: Mergers and Acquisitions

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

- Distinguish value-creating motives for mergers from motives that benefit managers or advisers at the expense of shareholders.
- Value operating and financial synergies, net of integration costs, and compute the maximum premium a buyer can pay without destroying value.
- Decompose the division of merger gains between acquirer and target shareholders under cash and stock consideration, and explain how stock consideration shares valuation risk.
- Calculate EPS accretion and dilution, break-even synergies, exchange ratios and collar mechanics, and explain why EPS accretion is not a test of value creation.
- Describe tender offers, proxy contests, and takeover defences, and analyse the effect of a poison pill on a bidder.
- Explain the principal reasons integration fails, and summarise how antitrust review measures concentration and what remedies are available.

## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario. Aventra Foods and Lumio Beverages are fictional.*

Aventra Foods, a packaged-foods group with a market capitalisation of 16 billion, has just announced the acquisition of Lumio Beverages, a faster-growing premium drinks maker, for 3.12 billion. The price is 39 per share against an undisturbed price of 30, a premium of 30 percent. Aventra will pay entirely in its own shares, and its chief executive tells analysts that the deal will deliver 60 million of annual cost savings and open Lumio's brands to Aventra's distribution network.

On announcement day, Aventra's stock falls 3 percent, wiping out about 480 million of market value, while Lumio's stock jumps 27 percent. A fund manager asks the question that every acquirer should be able to answer: "You are paying 720 million more than Lumio was worth yesterday. Where is the 720 million of value coming from, and who is going to deliver it?"

The CFO of Aventra, Tobias Reyes, has done the work. He has valued the synergies at roughly 800 million after integration costs, so on paper the deal creates value even after the premium. But he also knows three things. First, about 275 million of that figure depends on revenue synergies, which he trusts the least. Second, because Aventra is paying in shares, Lumio's shareholders will own 16 percent of the combined company and so will share both in the gains and in any shortfall. Third, EPS accretion, which the board has been fixated on, is slightly negative in the stock deal and slightly positive in a hypothetical cash deal, which says nothing about which is better for shareholders.

This chapter builds the toolkit to answer the fund manager: why firms buy other firms, how to put a value on synergies and a limit on the price, how consideration and deal structure allocate risk, how to calculate accretion and dilution without being misled by it, how hostile bids and defences work, how integration destroys or preserves value, and how regulators decide whether a combination may proceed.

## 22.1 Why Companies Merge

### 22.1.1 Terminology

A **merger** combines two companies into one legal entity; in an **acquisition** one company buys the shares or assets of another and may keep it as a subsidiary. A **tender offer** is a public offer to buy shares directly from shareholders, often bypassing the target's board. A **hostile** bid is one the target's board does not endorse; a **friendly** deal is negotiated. In practice, "M&A" covers all of these, plus divestitures, spin-offs, and leveraged buy-outs.

### 22.1.2 Motives that can create value

*Economies of scale and scope.* Combining plants, distribution, procurement, or overheads can cut unit costs. These are the most reliable synergies because they are within management's control.

*Market access and revenue enhancement.* Selling one firm's products through the other's channels, cross-selling, or entering new geographies. These are real but less certain, and take longer to realise.

*Vertical integration.* Acquiring a supplier or customer can reduce transaction costs and hold-up risk, especially where assets are specific and contracts are incomplete (Coase, Williamson).

*Acquiring capabilities.* Buying technology, talent, or brands is often faster than building them (the "buy versus build" decision), though retention of key people is a major risk.

*Tax benefits.* Use of tax losses or interest tax shields. These are limited by anti-abuse rules and rarely a primary rationale, and a deal that makes sense only because of tax should be examined sceptically.

*Replacing poor management.* The market for corporate control (Manne) lets outsiders buy underperforming firms and change strategy. The gain is the difference between value under better management and current value.

*Market power.* Combining competitors may let the firm raise prices. This is a private gain but a social cost, and it is exactly what antitrust law examines.

### 22.1.3 Motives that rarely create value

*Diversification.* Shareholders can diversify more cheaply than the firm can. Conglomerate acquisitions that diversify cash flows without improving them often lead to a "conglomerate discount."

*EPS bootstrapping.* A high-P/E company buying a low-P/E target with shares increases EPS mechanically, because it issues fewer shares than the target's earnings are "worth" at the acquirer's multiple. No economic value is created, and the market, if rational, adjusts the P/E downward on the combined earnings.

*Empire building and hubris.* Managers benefit from size (pay, prestige), and Jensen's free cash flow argument suggests that firms with excess cash tend to spend it on acquisitions. Roll's hubris hypothesis holds that bidders overestimate their ability to find synergies and so overpay. Both predict that acquirers' shareholders earn little or lose on average.

*Misvaluation.* Overvalued firms may use their shares as currency to acquire real assets. This is a rational choice for managers if it is correct, and a value-neutral transfer between old and new shareholders; it is not an operational synergy.

*Merger waves.* Bids cluster in time, with high valuations and abundant credit, and are often driven by industry shocks such as deregulation or technology change, or by the fear of being left behind.

### 22.1.4 What the evidence says

Empirical studies, across periods and countries, consistently report that target shareholders earn large positive abnormal returns around announcement, while acquirer shareholders earn returns close to zero or slightly negative on average, with wide dispersion across deals. Combined returns are typically positive, so deals create value in aggregate, but the premium transfers most of it to the target. Evidence also suggests that deals financed with stock tend to do less well for acquirers than cash deals, consistent with the idea that stock issuance signals overvaluation and shares risk. These are tendencies, not laws; carefully prepared acquisitions of focused scope, such as bolt-on deals in a core business, tend to do better than large transformational ones.

The managerial conclusion is simple: the buyer must be able to identify specific, controllable sources of value, quantify them, and compare them with the premium. If it cannot, the default is that the acquisition will not create value for the buyer's shareholders.

## 22.2 Valuing Synergies

### 22.2.1 The basic equation

The standard logic starts from the value of the combined firm:

```
V(A+B) = V(A) + V(B) + Synergies − Integration costs
Gain to acquirer's shareholders = Synergies − Integration costs − Premium
Premium = Price paid − Target's stand-alone market value
```

Equivalently, the acquisition has a positive net present value (NPV) for the buyer only if the **net synergies** exceed the **premium**. Because the target's stand-alone value is already reflected in its market price, only the incremental value counts.

### 22.2.2 Types of synergies

| Type | Source | Typical risk | Discount-rate view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost synergies | Overhead, procurement, footprint consolidation | Lowest | Rate for the combined business |
| Revenue synergies | Cross-selling, distribution, pricing | High; competitors respond | Higher rate, or probability-weight |
| Financial synergies | Lower cost of debt, debt capacity, tax shields | Moderate | Discount per financing risk |
| Dis-synergies | Customer losses, key-person exits, culture clashes | Often ignored | Include explicitly |

Each synergy should be specified with a base, a driver, a timing profile, an owner, and a realisation cost. A statement such as "cost savings of 5 percent of combined SG&A" is a target; "elimination of two distribution centres and 180 positions, saving 60 per year after the second year, at a one-off cost of 150" is a plan.

### 22.2.3 Valuation steps

1. **Forecast synergies** by category, pre-tax, with the timing of ramp-up.
2. **Tax-effect** them at the marginal rate.
3. **Subtract integration costs** (severance, systems, rebranding, retention bonuses, advisory fees), which are real cash and typically come first.
4. **Discount** at a rate matching the risk: the combined firm's WACC for cost synergies, higher rates for revenue synergies, or equivalently probability-weight the cash flows.
5. **Capitalise** the sustainable run-rate at a growth rate no higher than inflation. Competition tends to pass on persistent synergies to customers, so perpetual growth assumptions on synergies should be conservative.

**Example 22.1 – Valuing the Aventra-Lumio synergies.**
Assumptions: tax rate 25%; discount rate 9% for cost synergies and 12% for revenue synergies; terminal growth 2% for both.

*Cost synergies.* Run-rate pre-tax savings of 60, phased 50% in year 1 and 100% in year 2 (growing at 2% thereafter). After tax: full run-rate = 60 × 0.75 = 45.

- Year 1: 0.5 × 45 = 22.5. Year 2: 45 × 1.02 = 45.9.
- Terminal value at end of year 2: 45.9 × 1.02 / (0.09 − 0.02) = 46.82 / 0.07 = 668.8.
- PV = 22.5 / 1.09 + 45.9 / 1.09² + 668.8 / 1.09² = 20.6 + 38.6 + 562.9 = **622.2**.

*Revenue synergies.* Incremental revenue of 40, 80, 120, 140, 150 in years 1 to 5, at a contribution margin of 30%, taxed at 25%. After-tax cash flows: 9.0, 18.0, 27.0, 31.5, 33.75. Discount at 12%:

- PV of years 1–5 = 9.0/1.12 + 18.0/1.12² + 27.0/1.12³ + 31.5/1.12⁴ + 33.75/1.12⁵ = 8.0 + 14.3 + 19.2 + 20.0 + 19.2 = 80.8.
- Terminal value at end of year 5 = 33.75 × 1.02 / (0.12 − 0.02) = 344.3; PV = 344.3 / 1.7623 = 195.3.
- Total = 80.8 + 195.3 = **276.1**.

*Integration costs.* Pre-tax 90 in year 1 and 60 in year 2; after tax 67.5 and 45.0. PV at 9% = 67.5 / 1.09 + 45.0 / 1.09² = 61.9 + 37.9 = **99.8**.

*Net synergies.* 622.2 + 276.1 − 99.8 = **798.5**.

| Component | PV (millions) | Share of gross |
|---|---|---|
| Cost synergies | 622.2 | 69.3% |
| Revenue synergies | 276.1 | 30.7% |
| Gross synergies | 898.3 | 100% |
| Less integration costs | (99.8) | |
| **Net synergies** | **798.5** | |

*Interpretation.* Cost synergies form more than two-thirds of the value, and each 1 percentage point change in the terminal growth rate would change the cost-synergy value by more than 100 (raising g to 3% lifts it from 622 to about 729). If the revenue synergies were zero, net synergies would be 522.4, so a revenue-synergy shortfall alone does not eliminate value but strongly affects the maximum price.

## 22.3 Premiums and Who Captures the Gain

### 22.3.1 The maximum price

The most the buyer should pay is the target's stand-alone value plus the net synergies it can capture with certainty. At any higher price the buyer's shareholders transfer wealth to the seller. The target's board, conversely, should hold out for most of the synergies, which it will argue the buyer can only realise because of its asset. The negotiation is essentially about how to divide the **synergy surplus**.

```
Maximum premium (%) = Net synergies / Target stand-alone market value
```

**Example 22.2 – How much can Aventra pay?**
Lumio has 80 million shares trading at 30 (market capitalisation 2,400). Net synergies are 798.5 (Example 22.1).

*Step 1 – Maximum premium.* 798.5 / 2,400 = **33.3%**, corresponding to a maximum price of 30 × 1.3327 = 39.98, about 40 per share.

*Step 2 – NPV to Aventra at various prices.*

| Offer price | Premium % | Premium (millions) | NPV to Aventra | Target's share of synergies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 36.00 | 20.0% | 480.0 | +318.5 | 60.1% |
| 39.00 | 30.0% | 720.0 | +78.5 | 90.2% |
| 39.98 | 33.3% | 798.5 | 0.0 | 100.0% |
| 42.00 | 40.0% | 960.0 | −161.5 | 120.2% |

*Interpretation.* At the offer of 39, Aventra pays away 90.2% of the synergies and keeps 78.5 (9.8%). A buyer in this position has almost no margin for error: a shortfall of 10% in the net synergies (about 80) would eliminate the entire gain. This is why acquirers in competitive auctions tend to experience the "winner's curse": the winning bidder is typically the one with the most optimistic estimate of synergies, and therefore the one most likely to overpay.

### 22.3.2 Cash versus stock: sharing risk and value

When the buyer pays in cash, the target's shareholders receive a fixed amount; the buyer's shareholders hold all the uncertainty about synergies. When the buyer pays in stock, the target's shareholders become owners of the combined firm and share in the outcome. Stock also conserves cash and debt capacity, and may be tax-deferred for target shareholders in some jurisdictions. It also signals management's view: a buyer offering shares may be implying its stock is fully valued.

The split of value follows directly. Let the acquirer's market value be V_A, the target's V_T, synergies S, and the price in cash be P.

```
Cash deal:   Acquirer shareholders' value = V_A + V_T + S − P
Stock deal:  Target shareholders own a fraction x = New shares / (Old shares + New shares)
             Acquirer shareholders' value = (1 − x) × (V_A + V_T + S)
```

**Example 22.3 – Cash versus stock when synergies disappoint.**
Aventra has 400 million shares at 40 (V_A = 16,000). Lumio: V_T = 2,400. Offer: 39 per Lumio share, total 3,120. In a stock deal Aventra issues 3,120 / 40 = 78 million new shares, giving Lumio holders 78 / 478 = 16.3% of the combined firm. Evaluate for synergies of 0, 798.5, and 1,597 (assume the stock price reflects the true combined value).

*Combined value* = 16,000 + 2,400 + S = 18,400 + S.

| Synergies S | Cash: acquirer holders' value | Gain vs. 16,000 | Stock: acquirer holders' value | Gain vs. 16,000 | Stock: target holders' value | Target gain vs. 2,400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 15,280.0 | −720.0 | 15,397.5 | −602.5 | 3,002.5 | +602.5 |
| 798.5 | 16,078.5 | +78.5 | 16,065.7 | +65.7 | 3,132.8 | +732.8 |
| 1,597 | 16,877.0 | +877.0 | 16,733.9 | +733.9 | 3,263.1 | +863.1 |

*Calculation checks.* Cash, S = 0: 18,400 − 3,120 = 15,280. Stock, S = 0: 18,400 × (1 − 0.16318) = 18,400 × 0.83682 = 15,397.5. Stock, S = 798.5: 19,198.5 × 0.83682 = 16,065.7. Target holders in the stock deal with S = 798.5: 19,198.5 × 0.16318 = 3,132.8.

*Interpretation.* If synergies are nil, the stock deal costs Aventra's shareholders 602.5 instead of 720, because Lumio's holders bear 16.3% of the shortfall. If synergies are double, Aventra's holders in the cash deal gain 877 against 733.9 in the stock deal: stock gives away part of the upside. Stock is thus a hedge against disappointment, for which the buyer pays by sharing the upside. Where the buyer is highly uncertain about synergies (large revenue-synergy content, cross-border, different business models), stock is the better risk-sharing instrument; where the buyer is confident, cash keeps all the upside.

An additional consideration is **signalling and information asymmetry** (Myers-Majluf; Hansen): the target's holders know that a buyer who is confident about large synergies would prefer cash, so a stock offer may be read as bad news about the acquirer or about synergies. The announcement-day decline of 3 percent in Aventra's shares in the vignette is consistent with that.

## 22.4 Deal Structure and Consideration

### 22.4.1 Asset deal versus share deal

In a **share purchase**, the buyer acquires the target company with all its assets and liabilities, known and unknown, and its contracts, licences and tax attributes (subject to change-of-control clauses). In an **asset purchase**, the buyer chooses the assets and liabilities it takes. Asset deals allow ring-fencing of liabilities and, in some tax systems, a step-up in the tax basis of assets, but they require re-papering contracts and employee transfers, and may be taxed twice for the seller. The choice reflects tax, liability exposure, and the need for third-party consents. Two further structures are the **merger** by operation of law (forward, reverse, or triangular, where the target merges into a subsidiary of the buyer to isolate liabilities) and the **tender offer** (section 22.6).

### 22.4.2 Accounting for the acquisition

Under IFRS 3 (and its US counterpart, ASC 805), acquisitions are accounted for under the **acquisition method**. The buyer measures the identifiable assets acquired and the liabilities assumed at fair value on the acquisition date. **Goodwill** is the excess of consideration transferred (plus the fair value of any non-controlling interest and any previously held interest) over the net identifiable assets at fair value. Goodwill is not amortised under IFRS but is tested for impairment at least annually; an impairment charge a few years later is the accounting trace of an overpayment. Transaction costs are expensed as incurred, so advisory fees reduce reported earnings, and contingent consideration is recognised at fair value, with changes in value flowing through profit or loss. Because goodwill sits on the balance sheet, a high-premium deal also affects leverage ratios (equity is unchanged but total assets grow) and later impairment risk.

### 22.4.3 Consideration choices and protections

*Fixed exchange ratio.* The target receives a fixed number of acquirer shares per target share. The headline value floats with the buyer's price; the target bears the price risk between signing and closing.

*Fixed value (floating ratio).* The target receives a fixed value in shares, with the number of shares adjusting to the acquirer's price. The acquirer bears the price risk through the ownership dilution it concedes.

*Collar.* A band combining the two: within the band the ratio is fixed, and outside it the ratio floats to keep the value at a floor or a cap. Collars limit both parties' exposure to share price swings during the (often months-long) period between signing and closing.

*Earn-outs and contingent value rights.* Part of the price is deferred and paid if the target meets targets (revenue, EBITDA, regulatory approval). They bridge differences in valuation and align interests of sellers who stay on, but invite disputes about how the metrics are measured and manipulated.

*Deal protections.* Break-up fees (commonly a few percent of deal value), no-shop clauses with "fiduciary out" exceptions, matching rights, and lock-ups deter competing bidders; courts examine them for reasonableness. *Material adverse change* (MAC) clauses allow the buyer to walk away in extreme circumstances, but the bar is high.

**Example 22.4 – Fixed ratio versus fixed value and a collar.**
Aventra's price at signing is 40, the exchange ratio is 39 / 40 = 0.975 Aventra shares per Lumio share, and Lumio has 80 million shares. Compare outcomes at closing for different Aventra prices.

| Aventra price at close | Fixed ratio: value per Lumio share | Premium to 30 | Fixed value (39): ratio | Shares issued (m) | Lumio holders' ownership |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 44 | 42.90 | 43.0% | 0.886 | 70.9 | 15.1% |
| 40 | 39.00 | 30.0% | 0.975 | 78.0 | 16.3% |
| 36 | 35.10 | 17.0% | 1.083 | 86.7 | 17.8% |
| 32 | 31.20 | 4.0% | 1.219 | 97.5 | 19.6% |

*Calculations.* Fixed ratio at 36: 0.975 × 36 = 35.10; premium 35.10 / 30 − 1 = 17.0%. Fixed value at 36: ratio = 39 / 36 = 1.0833; shares = 80 × 1.0833 = 86.7; ownership = 86.7 / (400 + 86.7) = 17.8%.

*A collar.* Suppose the parties agree to a fixed ratio of 0.975 for Aventra prices between 36 and 44, a floor of value 35.10 below 36, and a cap of 42.90 above 44. At 32: the ratio floats to 35.10 / 32 = 1.0969, so Aventra issues 80 × 1.0969 = 87.75 million shares and Lumio holders own 87.75 / 487.75 = 18.0%. At 48: ratio = 42.90 / 48 = 0.894, Aventra issues 71.5 million shares.

*Interpretation.* Under a fixed ratio, Lumio's holders bear a swing from a 43% to a 4% premium. Under a fixed value, Aventra's holders concede ownership from 15.1% to 19.6%, a larger share of the synergy surplus when its stock has fallen. The collar splits the risk and provides a defined worst case for each party. Note that the share price may fall precisely because the market doubts the deal, so a fixed-value structure can create a "death spiral" in which a falling price leads to more issuance.

## 22.5 Accretion and Dilution Analysis

### 22.5.1 What it measures

**Accretion/dilution analysis** compares the acquirer's pro forma EPS with its stand-alone EPS. It is simple, quick, and the metric boards and analysts ask for first. Its use is natural for short-run reporting and for signalling to equity markets, but it is a poor guide to value creation, for two reasons: it ignores risk (a deal can be accretive and still destroy value if the target's earnings are riskier than the acquirer's), and it ignores the time value of money and the cost of equity (cheap debt makes almost any deal accretive).

### 22.5.2 The mechanics

```
Pro forma NI  = NI(acquirer) + NI(target) + after-tax synergies
                − after-tax interest on new debt − after-tax foregone interest on cash used
                − incremental D&A on asset write-ups (and amortisation of intangibles)
Pro forma shares = Acquirer shares + New shares issued
Pro forma EPS = Pro forma NI / Pro forma shares
Accretion (%) = Pro forma EPS / Stand-alone EPS − 1
```

### 22.5.3 A shortcut: earnings yield comparison

For a deal with no synergies and no write-ups, accretion depends on comparing the target's earnings yield at the offer price with the after-tax cost of the funding:

- **Cash funded by debt:** accretive if the target's earnings yield at the offer (NI_target / Purchase price) exceeds the after-tax cost of debt.
- **Stock-funded:** accretive if the target's offer-price P/E is lower than the acquirer's P/E (equivalently, the target's earnings yield at the offer exceeds the acquirer's earnings yield).
- **Cash from balance sheet:** accretive if the target's earnings yield exceeds the after-tax interest forgone on the cash.

**Example 22.5 – Accretion and dilution for the Aventra-Lumio deal.**
Aventra: net income 800, 400 million shares, EPS 2.00, price 40 (P/E 20.0×; earnings yield 5.0%). Lumio: net income 150, 80 million shares, EPS 1.875, price 30 (P/E 16.0×). Offer: 39 per share, total 3,120; offer P/E = 39 / 1.875 = 20.8×. New debt costs 6.0% pre-tax; tax rate 25%.

*Alternative 1 – All debt.* After-tax interest = 3,120 × 0.06 × 0.75 = 140.4. Pro forma NI = 800 + 150 − 140.4 = 809.6. Shares = 400. EPS = 2.024. Accretion = 2.024 / 2.00 − 1 = **+1.2%**.

*Alternative 2 – All stock.* New shares = 3,120 / 40 = 78.0. Pro forma NI = 950. Shares = 478. EPS = 950 / 478 = 1.9874. Accretion = **−0.6%**.

*Alternative 3 – 50% cash (debt), 50% stock.* Debt = 1,560; after-tax interest = 1,560 × 0.045 = 70.2; NI = 879.8. New shares = 1,560 / 40 = 39.0; shares = 439. EPS = 2.0041. Accretion = **+0.2%**.

*Earnings-yield check.* Target's earnings yield at the offer = 150 / 3,120 = 4.81%. After-tax cost of debt = 4.50%, so debt financing is accretive (4.81% > 4.50%). The acquirer's earnings yield is 5.00%, higher than the target's 4.81%, so stock financing is dilutive (the target is bought at a higher P/E, 20.8×, than the acquirer's 20.0×).

*Alternative 4 – With synergies.* Run-rate cost synergies of 60 pre-tax = 45 after tax. All debt: EPS = (809.6 + 45) / 400 = 2.1365 (+6.8%). All stock: EPS = (950 + 45) / 478 = 2.0816 (+4.1%).

*Break-even synergies for the stock deal.* Required NI = 2.00 × 478 = 956; shortfall = 956 − 950 = 6.0 after tax, or 6.0 / 0.75 = **8.0 pre-tax**.

| Financing | Pro forma NI | Shares (m) | EPS | Accretion/(dilution) | With 60 pre-tax synergies |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All debt | 809.6 | 400.0 | 2.024 | +1.2% | +6.8% |
| 50% debt / 50% stock | 879.8 | 439.0 | 2.004 | +0.2% | n/a |
| All stock | 950.0 | 478.0 | 1.987 | −0.6% | +4.1% |

*Interpretation.* The ranking by EPS (debt, mixed, stock) is the opposite of the ranking by risk, and it says nothing about value. In Example 22.3 the stock deal was better for Aventra's holders if synergies disappointed, because the target holders share the downside. The EPS test tells us only that debt is cheaper than equity on an after-tax accounting basis. Cheap debt is not free: a 3.1 billion borrowing raises leverage, interest burden, and distress risk, which the EPS metric ignores. A prudent analysis also shows the **cash flow** and **credit statistics** (net debt/EBITDA, interest cover) before and after the deal.

### 22.5.4 Pitfalls

- **Write-ups and amortisation.** Fair-value step-ups of fixed assets and amortisable intangibles reduce pro forma earnings; many firms report "cash" or "adjusted" EPS excluding acquisition amortisation. Read the definitions.
- **Timing.** Synergies and integration costs are uneven; first-year EPS may be dilutive even in a value-creating deal.
- **Ratios of P/E are not constants.** A merger of a high-P/E growth company with a low-P/E mature one may lower the combined P/E because the growth mix changes.
- **Contribution analysis.** Comparing each party's contribution to revenue, EBITDA, and net income with its ownership share in the combined entity is a useful sanity check on the exchange ratio, but it should be adjusted for leverage, since net income and equity-value contributions differ with debt.

## 22.6 Tender Offers, Hostile Bids, and Takeover Defences

### 22.6.1 Routes to control

A bidder who does not obtain board support has three routes. In a **tender offer**, the bidder offers to buy shares directly from shareholders at a stated price for a limited period. In the United States, the Williams Act regulates these offers: a minimum offer period (20 business days), equal treatment of tendering holders, and disclosure when a holder crosses 5 percent of a class. In a **proxy contest**, the bidder or an activist solicits shareholder votes to replace directors who oppose the deal. In a **creeping acquisition**, the bidder purchases shares on the market, subject to disclosure rules and, in some jurisdictions, mandatory-bid rules (for example, under the UK Takeover Code an acquirer crossing 30 percent of the voting rights must bid for the remainder at not less than the highest price recently paid).

A **toehold**, a pre-bid stake below the disclosure threshold, lets the bidder buy shares at undisturbed prices and receive a share of the profit from a rival bid, thereby partly offsetting the premium.

### 22.6.2 The free-rider problem

Grossman and Hart showed that a tender offer for a widely held target faces a free-rider problem. If a bid at price b would raise the post-takeover value of the target to V* > b, each atomistic shareholder reasons that her single share cannot determine the outcome, and so she holds out to enjoy V* rather than sell at b. For the bid to succeed, it must offer essentially all of the improvement; the bidder then earns nothing on the target shares and must recoup its costs from other sources, such as a toehold, **dilution** (the ability to squeeze minority holders at a lower price after gaining control), or private benefits. This theory explains toeholds, two-tier and back-end offers, squeeze-out provisions, and why takeover law regulates the minority's treatment.

### 22.6.3 Defences

*Poison pills (shareholder rights plans).* The target issues rights that, when an outsider crosses an ownership threshold (typically 10 to 20 percent), allow all other holders to buy shares at a deep discount, massively diluting the bidder (a "flip-in"), or to buy the bidder's shares at a discount after a merger (a "flip-over"). The board can redeem the rights, so a bidder must negotiate with the board or win a proxy contest to replace it. In the US, courts upheld pills in principle (Moran v. Household International) but subject their use to enhanced scrutiny (Unocal).

*Staggered (classified) boards.* Only a fraction of directors is elected each year, so a bidder cannot take control of the board in a single proxy contest. Combined with a pill, this makes a hostile bid very hard.

*Supermajority provisions, dual-class shares, and golden shares.* They place control in the hands of insiders or the state.

*White knight and white squire.* The target seeks a friendlier acquirer or a friendly investor who will take a block.

*Pac-Man defence.* The target bids for the bidder.

*Restructuring defences.* Selling the "crown jewels" (asset lock-ups), leveraged recapitalisations, large buy-backs, or special dividends, each reducing the target's attractiveness or making it more expensive.

*Litigation and regulatory lobbying.* Challenging the bid on antitrust or disclosure grounds to delay it.

*Golden parachutes.* Severance payments for executives on change of control. These can make bids more expensive but, more importantly, may reduce the incentive for managers to resist a good offer.

### 22.6.4 Law and governance

The legal environment differs materially. In **Delaware** (the main US corporate forum), directors may resist a bid if they reasonably perceive a threat and respond proportionately (Unocal), but once the company is "for sale" (a breakup or change of control), their duty is to obtain the best price reasonably available (Revlon). Under the **UK Takeover Code**, once an offer is imminent, the board may not take actions that could frustrate the offer without shareholder approval, which makes pills and other structural defences essentially unavailable, and a bidder must announce a firm intention or withdraw within a short deadline after a leak ("put up or shut up"). In **continental Europe**, regimes lie between those, with significant variation in the role of employees, supervisory boards, and the passivity rule.

Whether takeover defences help or harm shareholders is debated. Defenders argue that they give the board bargaining power to extract a higher price and to resist opportunistic low-ball bids, and that long-term investment requires insulation from short-term pressure. Critics argue that entrenchment protects underperforming managers, and that the market for corporate control is the ultimate discipline on them. The evidence is mixed, and good governance practice generally prefers defences that increase the board's bargaining power but remain subject to shareholder approval and removal.

**Example 22.6 – What a poison pill does to a bidder.**
Target Ordinal Inc. has 100 million shares at 50 (market capitalisation 5,000). A hostile bidder has built a 15% stake (15 million shares). The pill is triggered at a 15% holding: each right entitles holders other than the bidder to buy, for an exercise price of 150, shares worth 300 at the pre-trigger market price (that is, 300 / 50 = 6 shares per right). Assume all other holders (85 million) exercise.

*Step 1 – Shares issued and cash raised.* 85 million × 6 = 510 million new shares. Cash received = 85 million × 150 = 12,750 million.

*Step 2 – Post-exercise price.* Value of the firm = 5,000 + 12,750 = 17,750 (assuming the cash does not change operating value). Shares = 100 + 510 = 610 million. Price = 17,750 / 610 = **29.10**.

*Step 3 – Effect on the bidder.* Ownership falls from 15 / 100 = 15.0% to 15 / 610 = **2.46%**. The value of the bidder's block falls from 15 × 50 = 750 to 15 × 29.10 = 436.5, a loss of **313.5** (41.8%).

*Step 4 – Effect on exercising holders.* Each pays 150 and receives 6 shares worth 6 × 29.10 = 174.6, a gain of 24.6. The gain is financed by the dilution of the bidder, not by the company.

*Interpretation.* The pill imposes a penalty on the bidder sufficient to deter an unfriendly accumulation. Since the board can redeem the rights, the bidder's only options are to negotiate or to remove the board. In practice, the pill is rarely triggered: it is a bargaining device. In real plans, the numbers are structured so that the dilution is serious but the cash raised is small relative to the firm, and exercise is not expected; the example's complete exercise is purely for illustration.

## 22.7 Post-Merger Integration

### 22.7.1 Why integration decides the outcome

The valuation assumes that synergies will be realised. That realisation depends on execution, which is why many analyses of acquisitions find the quality of integration a strong predictor of success. Value-destroying deals often fail because the buyer treats the signing as the finish line. The **integration agenda** typically includes:

- **Governance and leadership:** appointing the integration leader, naming the combined management team quickly (uncertainty drives talent loss), and setting a clear decision-making framework.
- **Synergy tracking:** assigning every synergy line a named owner, a budget, milestones, and a reporting cadence, in a dedicated integration management office. Synergies not tracked are not delivered.
- **Customers and revenue:** protecting the base. Revenue dis-synergies, from customers uncomfortable with the new owner or concerned about service continuity, are often larger than planned.
- **People and culture:** retaining key talent through retention bonuses and clarity about roles; resolving differences in compensation and decision styles. Cultural mismatch rarely shows in the model and often dominates the outcome.
- **Systems and operations:** ERP, supply chain, and IT integration, which are expensive and risky, with timelines that routinely exceed the plan.
- **Communication:** consistent messages to employees, customers, suppliers, regulators, and investors.

### 22.7.2 Speed versus care

Fast integration captures cost synergies sooner and limits the period of uncertainty, but it risks damaging customer relationships and losing key people. Two approaches are often seen: **absorption** (full integration of the target into the acquirer, suited to cost-driven deals in the same business) and **preservation** (keeping the target's brand, culture, and operations largely intact, suited to capability acquisitions, where autonomy is what preserves the value). The wrong approach (absorbing a creative business, or preserving a duplicate cost base) is a classic cause of failure.

**Example 22.7 – What happens to value if integration under-delivers?**
In Example 22.1, gross synergies were worth 898.3 (cost 622.2 plus revenue 276.1) and integration costs 99.8. Suppose that, because of integration friction, only 70% of the planned synergies are realised, they are delayed by one year, and integration costs overrun by 25%.

*Step 1 – Realised synergies.* 0.70 × 898.3 = 628.8.

*Step 2 – Delay.* Discount one extra year at 9%: 628.8 / 1.09 = **576.9**.

*Step 3 – Integration costs.* 99.8 × 1.25 = **124.8**.

*Step 4 – Net synergies.* 576.9 − 124.8 = **452.1**, compared with 798.5 planned (a reduction of 43%).

*Step 5 – Value to Aventra.* NPV = 452.1 − 720.0 (premium at 39 per share) = **−267.9**.

*Interpretation.* A deal with an apparently comfortable 78.5 of NPV turns to a loss of 267.9 under a moderately disappointing integration. This shows that the buyer's margin of safety is the difference between the net synergies and the premium; where the premium consumes 90% of synergies, any execution slippage is value-destroying.

## 22.8 Antitrust and Regulatory Review

### 22.8.1 Why regulators care

Competition authorities ask whether the merger will substantially lessen competition or create or strengthen a dominant position, leading to higher prices, lower quality, reduced innovation, or less choice. Many jurisdictions require **pre-merger notification** above specified size thresholds: in the United States, under the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act, the parties file with the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice and observe a waiting period during which the agencies may request more information (a "second request"); the legal substantive standard is in Section 7 of the Clayton Act. In the European Union, the European Commission reviews mergers with an EU dimension under the EU Merger Regulation, with a Phase I and, for serious concerns, a Phase II investigation. Other major jurisdictions (the UK, China, Japan, and many more) have their own regimes, and a large international deal may require a dozen or more clearances, with the slowest determining the timetable.

### 22.8.2 Theories of harm

*Horizontal mergers* (competitors) reduce the number of rivals and may allow **unilateral effects** (the merged firm raises prices because customers have fewer alternatives and diversion to the merging parties' products is high) or **coordinated effects** (the market becomes easier to collude in).

*Vertical mergers* may raise concerns about **foreclosure** (denying rivals inputs or customers) or access to competitors' sensitive information, but also deliver efficiencies by eliminating double marginalisation.

*Conglomerate mergers* may raise concerns about bundling or tying and portfolio power, though they are less often challenged.

*Potential competition and "killer acquisitions".* Buying a nascent competitor before it matures is scrutinised increasingly, particularly in technology and pharmaceuticals.

### 22.8.3 Measuring concentration

The **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)** is the sum of squared market shares (in percent) of all firms in a market. A monopoly has an HHI of 10,000. If firms with shares s₁ and s₂ merge, the HHI increases by 2 × s₁ × s₂. Under the 2023 US Merger Guidelines, markets with an HHI above 1,800 are considered highly concentrated, and a merger that produces such a market and raises the HHI by more than 100 points is presumed to substantially lessen competition, as is a merger creating a firm with a share above 30% when the HHI change exceeds 100. (Thresholds have changed over time; earlier guidelines used 2,500 and 200, and you should always check the current text.) The HHI is a screen, not a verdict; market definition, entry conditions, buyer power, and efficiencies are crucial.

**Example 22.8 – Computing HHI before and after.**
Five firms in a regional market have shares of 30%, 25%, 20%, 15%, and 10%. The firms with 20% and 15% propose to merge.

*Step 1 – Pre-merger HHI.* 30² + 25² + 20² + 15² + 10² = 900 + 625 + 400 + 225 + 100 = **2,250**.

*Step 2 – Change.* 2 × 20 × 15 = **600**.

*Step 3 – Post-merger HHI.* 2,250 + 600 = **2,850**. Check: shares are 30, 25, 35, 10, giving 900 + 625 + 1,225 + 100 = 2,850.

*Interpretation.* The post-merger HHI exceeds 1,800 and the increase of 600 is far above 100; the market is already highly concentrated, and the merged firm would have the largest share (35%). The merger would be presumptively problematic. The parties would need to provide evidence of efficiencies, entry, or buyer power to rebut the presumption, or propose remedies.

### 22.8.4 Remedies and process

Authorities may clear the deal unconditionally, clear it with remedies, block it, or the parties may abandon it. **Structural remedies** require divestiture of overlapping businesses to a suitable buyer (preferred by most agencies as they restore competition directly). **Behavioural remedies** (price caps, access commitments, firewalls) are harder to monitor and so used more sparingly. Merger agreements typically contain *efforts covenants* (how far the buyer must go in remedies), a long-stop date, and *reverse break fees* if the deal fails on regulatory grounds. From a valuation perspective, regulatory risk lowers the expected value of the deal, since there is a probability of failure and the cost of delay, and requires scenario analysis: the cost of divesting the overlap and the probability that the deal is cleared.

Beyond competition review, deals may face foreign-investment screening on national security grounds, sector regulators (banking, telecoms, energy), employee consultation requirements, and shareholder approvals.

## Case Study: Meridian Components and the Sunvale Bid

*Illustrative scenario; Meridian Components, Sunvale Systems, and their executives are fictional.*

Meridian Components is a listed manufacturer of industrial sensors with 150 million shares trading at 24, giving a market capitalisation of 3.6 billion, EPS of 1.20 (P/E 20.0×), and debt of 400 million. Its chief executive, Dalia Karimi, sees a strategic opportunity in Sunvale Systems, a smaller developer of embedded software for the same customers, with 40 million shares at 18 (market capitalisation 720 million), EPS of 0.90 (P/E 20.0×), no debt, and a high share of recurring licences. Sunvale's founders hold 30 percent of the shares and are lukewarm: they like Meridian's reach but worry about losing control of their product team.

Meridian's team estimates that bundling hardware and software could add 60 million of annual after-tax operating profit after three years, with a 25 percent probability of failing to materialise because several of Sunvale's customers compete with Meridian's own clients. Cost savings of about 15 million after tax per year are more certain. Integration would cost 50 million after tax over two years. Using Meridian's 8.5 percent cost of capital and a conservative terminal growth of 2 percent, the team values the cost savings at 15 / 0.065 = 231 million, probability-weighted bundling profit at about 330 million, and integration costs at 47 million, so net synergies of roughly 514 million, or 71 percent of Sunvale's market value.

The investment bankers propose an offer at 24.50 per share, a 36 percent premium (total 980 million), all in Meridian stock at an exchange ratio of 24.50 / 24 = 1.0208 Meridian shares per Sunvale share. That would give Sunvale holders 40.83 / 190.83 = 21.4 percent of the combined company, and since both companies trade at 20 times earnings the deal is EPS-neutral before synergies. The board is attracted to this, but a director points out that a cash offer financed by debt at 6.5 percent pre-tax (4.9 percent after tax at a 25 percent rate) against Sunvale's earnings yield at the offer price of 0.90 / 24.50 = 3.7 percent would be dilutive, and that Meridian's leverage would rise.

A second complication is that Meridian's largest customer has warned that it would view a combination with Sunvale as a competitive threat to its own software efforts, and a rival, Orbis Controls, with 5 percent of Sunvale's shares already, may make a competing bid. The Sunvale founders have signalled they would support a deal if the exchange ratio is fixed, retention packages are guaranteed for the product team, and the software unit is run as a separate division for at least three years. Meridian's lawyers consider the antitrust risk low (combined share below 15 percent in any relevant market) but caution that the customer's complaint might prompt a longer review.

Karimi must decide on the price, the form of consideration, the integration model, and how to respond if Orbis bids.

**Discussion Questions**

1. Compute the maximum premium Meridian can pay from the net synergy estimate. How much of the synergy surplus does the proposed 36 percent premium hand to Sunvale's holders, and how sensitive is the conclusion to the 25 percent failure probability on the bundling profit?
2. Compare cash and stock consideration for this deal on three dimensions: EPS accretion, allocation of synergy risk, and signalling. Which would you recommend, and would you change your answer if Meridian were more confident in the bundling synergies?
3. The founders want the software unit to remain autonomous for three years. What does that imply for the realisable synergies, and how would you structure the integration (absorption versus preservation) and the incentives (retention, earn-out) to balance the two?
4. How should Meridian respond if Orbis bids higher? Discuss walk-away discipline, the winner's curse, and the deal-protection devices (break fees, matching rights) you would seek in the merger agreement.

## Key Takeaways

- Acquisitions create value only through identifiable, controllable sources of gain (cost savings, capability, better management) that exceed the premium; diversification and EPS bootstrapping are not sources of value.
- The empirical regularity is that target shareholders capture most of the gain and acquirer shareholders earn roughly zero on average, with large variation; discipline in price is the main determinant of buyer returns.
- Value synergies by type, tax-effect them, net out integration costs, discount at risk-appropriate rates, and treat revenue synergies with scepticism and probability weights.
- The maximum premium equals net synergies divided by the target's stand-alone value; the closer the price to that bound, the smaller the margin of safety.
- Stock consideration shares both the upside and the downside with target holders, and also signals management's view; cash retains all upside and risk for the acquirer.
- Fixed-ratio, fixed-value, and collar structures allocate share-price risk between signing and closing; each party should know its worst case.
- EPS accretion is mechanical: cheap after-tax debt or a lower target P/E produces it. It is not a test of value creation, and it neglects risk and the cost of capital.
- Hostile bids face the free-rider problem; defences such as poison pills and staggered boards raise the bidder's cost and strengthen the board's bargaining position, but governance regimes differ greatly across jurisdictions.
- Integration is where synergies are delivered or lost; modest execution shortfalls can turn an apparent gain into a loss when the premium absorbs most of the synergies.
- Antitrust review screens concentration, for example with the HHI, and examines theories of harm; remedies and delay are part of the deal's expected value.
