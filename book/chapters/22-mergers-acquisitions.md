# Chapter 22: Mergers and Acquisitions

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter, you should be able to:

- Distinguish value-creating motives for mergers from motives that benefit managers or advisers at the expense of shareholders.
- Value operating and financial synergies, net of integration costs, and compute the maximum premium a buyer can pay without destroying value.
- Decompose the division of merger gains between acquirer and target shareholders under cash and stock consideration, and explain how stock consideration shares valuation risk.
- Calculate EPS accretion and dilution, break-even synergies, exchange ratios and collar mechanics, and explain why EPS accretion is not a test of value creation.
- Describe tender offers, proxy contests, and takeover defences, and analyse the effect of a poison pill on a bidder.
- Explain the principal reasons integration fails, and summarise how antitrust review measures concentration and what remedies are available.

## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario. Aventra Foods and Lumio Beverages are fictional.*

Aventra Foods, a packaged-foods group with a market capitalisation of 16 billion, has just announced the acquisition of Lumio Beverages, a faster-growing premium drinks maker, for 3.12 billion. The price is 39 per share against an undisturbed price of 30, a premium of 30 percent. Aventra will pay entirely in its own shares, and its chief executive tells analysts that the deal will deliver 60 million of annual cost savings and open Lumio's brands to Aventra's distribution network.

On announcement day, Aventra's stock falls 3 percent, wiping out about 480 million of market value, while Lumio's stock jumps 27 percent. A fund manager asks the question that every acquirer should be able to answer: "You are paying 720 million more than Lumio was worth yesterday. Where is the 720 million of value coming from, and who is going to deliver it?"

The CFO of Aventra, Tobias Reyes, has done the work. He has valued the synergies at roughly 800 million after integration costs, so on paper the deal creates value even after the premium. But he also knows three things. First, about 275 million of that figure depends on revenue synergies, which he trusts the least. Second, because Aventra is paying in shares, Lumio's shareholders will own 16 percent of the combined company and so will share both in the gains and in any shortfall. Third, EPS accretion, which the board has been fixated on, is slightly negative in the stock deal and slightly positive in a hypothetical cash deal, which says nothing about which is better for shareholders.

This chapter builds the toolkit to answer the fund manager: why firms buy other firms, how to put a value on synergies and a limit on the price, how consideration and deal structure allocate risk, how to calculate accretion and dilution without being misled by it, how hostile bids and defences work, how integration destroys or preserves value, and how regulators decide whether a combination may proceed.

## 22.1 Why Companies Merge

### 22.1.1 Terminology

A **merger** combines two companies into one legal entity; in an **acquisition** one company buys the shares or assets of another and may keep it as a subsidiary. A **tender offer** is a public offer to buy shares directly from shareholders, often bypassing the target's board. A **hostile** bid is one the target's board does not endorse; a **friendly** deal is negotiated. In practice, "M&A" covers all of these, plus divestitures, spin-offs, and leveraged buy-outs.

### 22.1.2 Motives that can create value

*Economies of scale and scope.* Combining plants, distribution, procurement, or overheads can cut unit costs. These are the most reliable synergies because they are within management's control.

*Market access and revenue enhancement.* Selling one firm's products through the other's channels, cross-selling, or entering new geographies. These are real but less certain, and take longer to realise.

*Vertical integration.* Acquiring a supplier or customer can reduce transaction costs and hold-up risk, especially where assets are specific and contracts are incomplete (Coase, Williamson).

*Acquiring capabilities.* Buying technology, talent, or brands is often faster than building them (the "buy versus build" decision), though retention of key people is a major risk.

*Tax benefits.* Use of tax losses or interest tax shields. These are limited by anti-abuse rules and rarely a primary rationale, and a deal that makes sense only because of tax should be examined sceptically.

*Replacing poor management.* The market for corporate control (Manne) lets outsiders buy underperforming firms and change strategy. The gain is the difference between value under better management and current value.

*Market power.* Combining competitors may let the firm raise prices. This is a private gain but a social cost, and it is exactly what antitrust law examines.

### 22.1.3 Motives that rarely create value

*Diversification.* Shareholders can diversify more cheaply than the firm can. Conglomerate acquisitions that diversify cash flows without improving them often lead to a "conglomerate discount."

*EPS bootstrapping.* A high-P/E company buying a low-P/E target with shares increases EPS mechanically, because it issues fewer shares than the target's earnings are "worth" at the acquirer's multiple. No economic value is created, and the market, if rational, adjusts the P/E downward on the combined earnings.

*Empire building and hubris.* Managers benefit from size (pay, prestige), and Jensen's free cash flow argument suggests that firms with excess cash tend to spend it on acquisitions. Roll's hubris hypothesis holds that bidders overestimate their ability to find synergies and so overpay. Both predict that acquirers' shareholders earn little or lose on average.

*Misvaluation.* Overvalued firms may use their shares as currency to acquire real assets. This is a rational choice for managers if it is correct, and a value-neutral transfer between old and new shareholders; it is not an operational synergy.

*Merger waves.* Bids cluster in time, with high valuations and abundant credit, and are often driven by industry shocks such as deregulation or technology change, or by the fear of being left behind.

### 22.1.4 What the evidence says

Empirical studies, across periods and countries, consistently report that target shareholders earn large positive abnormal returns around announcement, while acquirer shareholders earn returns close to zero or slightly negative on average, with wide dispersion across deals. Combined returns are typically positive, so deals create value in aggregate, but the premium transfers most of it to the target. Evidence also suggests that deals financed with stock tend to do less well for acquirers than cash deals, consistent with the idea that stock issuance signals overvaluation and shares risk. These are tendencies, not laws; carefully prepared acquisitions of focused scope, such as bolt-on deals in a core business, tend to do better than large transformational ones.

The managerial conclusion is simple: the buyer must be able to identify specific, controllable sources of value, quantify them, and compare them with the premium. If it cannot, the default is that the acquisition will not create value for the buyer's shareholders.

## 22.2 Valuing Synergies

### 22.2.1 The basic equation

The standard logic starts from the value of the combined firm:

```
V(A+B) = V(A) + V(B) + Synergies − Integration costs
Gain to acquirer's shareholders = Synergies − Integration costs − Premium
Premium = Price paid − Target's stand-alone market value
```

Equivalently, the acquisition has a positive net present value (NPV) for the buyer only if the **net synergies** exceed the **premium**. Because the target's stand-alone value is already reflected in its market price, only the incremental value counts.

### 22.2.2 Types of synergies

| Type | Source | Typical risk | Discount-rate view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost synergies | Overhead, procurement, footprint consolidation | Lowest | Rate for the combined business |
| Revenue synergies | Cross-selling, distribution, pricing | High; competitors respond | Higher rate, or probability-weight |
| Financial synergies | Lower cost of debt, debt capacity, tax shields | Moderate | Discount per financing risk |
| Dis-synergies | Customer losses, key-person exits, culture clashes | Often ignored | Include explicitly |

Each synergy should be specified with a base, a driver, a timing profile, an owner, and a realisation cost. A statement such as "cost savings of 5 percent of combined SG&A" is a target; "elimination of two distribution centres and 180 positions, saving 60 per year after the second year, at a one-off cost of 150" is a plan.

### 22.2.3 Valuation steps

1. **Forecast synergies** by category, pre-tax, with the timing of ramp-up.
2. **Tax-effect** them at the marginal rate.
3. **Subtract integration costs** (severance, systems, rebranding, retention bonuses, advisory fees), which are real cash and typically come first.
4. **Discount** at a rate matching the risk: the combined firm's WACC for cost synergies, higher rates for revenue synergies, or equivalently probability-weight the cash flows.
5. **Capitalise** the sustainable run-rate at a growth rate no higher than inflation. Competition tends to pass on persistent synergies to customers, so perpetual growth assumptions on synergies should be conservative.

**Example 22.1 – Valuing the Aventra-Lumio synergies.**
Assumptions: tax rate 25%; discount rate 9% for cost synergies and 12% for revenue synergies; terminal growth 2% for both.

*Cost synergies.* Run-rate pre-tax savings of 60, phased 50% in year 1 and 100% in year 2 (growing at 2% thereafter). After tax: full run-rate = 60 × 0.75 = 45.

- Year 1: 0.5 × 45 = 22.5. Year 2: 45 × 1.02 = 45.9.
- Terminal value at end of year 2: 45.9 × 1.02 / (0.09 − 0.02) = 46.82 / 0.07 = 668.8.
- PV = 22.5 / 1.09 + 45.9 / 1.09² + 668.8 / 1.09² = 20.6 + 38.6 + 562.9 = **622.2**.

*Revenue synergies.* Incremental revenue of 40, 80, 120, 140, 150 in years 1 to 5, at a contribution margin of 30%, taxed at 25%. After-tax cash flows: 9.0, 18.0, 27.0, 31.5, 33.75. Discount at 12%:

- PV of years 1–5 = 9.0/1.12 + 18.0/1.12² + 27.0/1.12³ + 31.5/1.12⁴ + 33.75/1.12⁵ = 8.0 + 14.3 + 19.2 + 20.0 + 19.2 = 80.8.
- Terminal value at end of year 5 = 33.75 × 1.02 / (0.12 − 0.02) = 344.3; PV = 344.3 / 1.7623 = 195.3.
- Total = 80.8 + 195.3 = **276.1**.

*Integration costs.* Pre-tax 90 in year 1 and 60 in year 2; after tax 67.5 and 45.0. PV at 9% = 67.5 / 1.09 + 45.0 / 1.09² = 61.9 + 37.9 = **99.8**.

*Net synergies.* 622.2 + 276.1 − 99.8 = **798.5**.

| Component | PV (millions) | Share of gross |
|---|---|---|
| Cost synergies | 622.2 | 69.3% |
| Revenue synergies | 276.1 | 30.7% |
| Gross synergies | 898.3 | 100% |
| Less integration costs | (99.8) | |
| **Net synergies** | **798.5** | |

*Interpretation.* Cost synergies form more than two-thirds of the value, and each 1 percentage point change in the terminal growth rate would change the cost-synergy value by about 80. If the revenue synergies were zero, net synergies would be 522.4, so a revenue-synergy shortfall alone does not eliminate value but strongly affects the maximum price.

## 22.3 Premiums and Who Captures the Gain

### 22.3.1 The maximum price

The most the buyer should pay is the target's stand-alone value plus the net synergies it can capture with certainty. At any higher price the buyer's shareholders transfer wealth to the seller. The target's board, conversely, should hold out for most of the synergies, which it will argue the buyer can only realise because of its asset. The negotiation is essentially about how to divide the **synergy surplus**.

```
Maximum premium (%) = Net synergies / Target stand-alone market value
```

**Example 22.2 – How much can Aventra pay?**
Lumio has 80 million shares trading at 30 (market capitalisation 2,400). Net synergies are 798.5 (Example 22.1).

*Step 1 – Maximum premium.* 798.5 / 2,400 = **33.3%**, corresponding to a maximum price of 30 × 1.3327 = 39.98, about 40 per share.

*Step 2 – NPV to Aventra at various prices.*

| Offer price | Premium % | Premium (millions) | NPV to Aventra | Target's share of synergies |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 36.00 | 20.0% | 480.0 | +318.5 | 60.1% |
| 39.00 | 30.0% | 720.0 | +78.5 | 90.2% |
| 39.98 | 33.3% | 798.5 | 0.0 | 100.0% |
| 42.00 | 40.0% | 960.0 | −161.5 | 120.2% |

*Interpretation.* At the offer of 39, Aventra pays away 90.2% of the synergies and keeps 78.5 (9.8%). A buyer in this position has almost no margin for error: a shortfall of 10% in the net synergies (about 80) would eliminate the entire gain. This is why acquirers in competitive auctions tend to experience the "winner's curse": the winning bidder is typically the one with the most optimistic estimate of synergies, and therefore the one most likely to overpay.

### 22.3.2 Cash versus stock: sharing risk and value

When the buyer pays in cash, the target's shareholders receive a fixed amount; the buyer's shareholders hold all the uncertainty about synergies. When the buyer pays in stock, the target's shareholders become owners of the combined firm and share in the outcome. Stock also conserves cash and debt capacity, and may be tax-deferred for target shareholders in some jurisdictions. It also signals management's view: a buyer offering shares may be implying its stock is fully valued.

The split of value follows directly. Let the acquirer's market value be V_A, the target's V_T, synergies S, and the price in cash be P.

```
Cash deal:   Acquirer shareholders' value = V_A + V_T + S − P
Stock deal:  Target shareholders own a fraction x = New shares / (Old shares + New shares)
             Acquirer shareholders' value = (1 − x) × (V_A + V_T + S)
```

**Example 22.3 – Cash versus stock when synergies disappoint.**
Aventra has 400 million shares at 40 (V_A = 16,000). Lumio: V_T = 2,400. Offer: 39 per Lumio share, total 3,120. In a stock deal Aventra issues 3,120 / 40 = 78 million new shares, giving Lumio holders 78 / 478 = 16.3% of the combined firm. Evaluate for synergies of 0, 798.5, and 1,597 (assume the stock price reflects the true combined value).

*Combined value* = 16,000 + 2,400 + S = 18,400 + S.

| Synergies S | Cash: acquirer holders' value | Gain vs. 16,000 | Stock: acquirer holders' value | Gain vs. 16,000 | Stock: target holders' value | Target gain vs. 2,400 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 15,280.0 | −720.0 | 15,397.5 | −602.5 | 3,002.5 | +602.5 |
| 798.5 | 16,078.5 | +78.5 | 16,065.7 | +65.7 | 3,132.8 | +732.8 |
| 1,597 | 16,877.0 | +877.0 | 16,733.9 | +733.9 | 3,263.1 | +863.1 |

*Calculation checks.* Cash, S = 0: 18,400 − 3,120 = 15,280. Stock, S = 0: 18,400 × (1 − 0.16318) = 18,400 × 0.83682 = 15,397.5. Stock, S = 798.5: 19,198.5 × 0.83682 = 16,065.7. Target holders in the stock deal with S = 798.5: 19,198.5 × 0.16318 = 3,132.8.

*Interpretation.* If synergies are nil, the stock deal costs Aventra's shareholders 602.5 instead of 720, because Lumio's holders bear 16.3% of the shortfall. If synergies are double, Aventra's holders in the cash deal gain 877 against 733.9 in the stock deal: stock gives away part of the upside. Stock is thus a hedge against disappointment, for which the buyer pays by sharing the upside. Where the buyer is highly uncertain about synergies (large revenue-synergy content, cross-border, different business models), stock is the better risk-sharing instrument; where the buyer is confident, cash keeps all the upside.

An additional consideration is **signalling and information asymmetry** (Myers-Majluf; Hansen): the target's holders know that a buyer who is confident about large synergies would prefer cash, so a stock offer may be read as bad news about the acquirer or about synergies. The announcement-day decline of 3 percent in Aventra's shares in the vignette is consistent with that.

## 22.4 Deal Structure and Consideration

### 22.4.1 Asset deal versus share deal

In a **share purchase**, the buyer acquires the target company with all its assets and liabilities, known and unknown, and its contracts, licences and tax attributes (subject to change-of-control clauses). In an **asset purchase**, the buyer chooses the assets and liabilities it takes. Asset deals allow ring-fencing of liabilities and, in some tax systems, a step-up in the tax basis of assets, but they require re-papering contracts and employee transfers, and may be taxed twice for the seller. The choice reflects tax, liability exposure, and the need for third-party consents. Two further structures are the **merger** by operation of law (forward, reverse, or triangular, where the target merges into a subsidiary of the buyer to isolate liabilities) and the **tender offer** (section 22.6).

### 22.4.2 Accounting for the acquisition

Under IFRS 3 (and its US counterpart, ASC 805), acquisitions are accounted for under the **acquisition method**. The buyer measures the identifiable assets acquired and the liabilities assumed at fair value on the acquisition date. **Goodwill** is the excess of consideration transferred (plus the fair value of any non-controlling interest and any previously held interest) over the net identifiable assets at fair value. Goodwill is not amortised under IFRS but is tested for impairment at least annually; an impairment charge a few years later is the accounting trace of an overpayment. Transaction costs are expensed as incurred, so advisory fees reduce reported earnings, and contingent consideration is recognised at fair value, with changes in value flowing through profit or loss. Because goodwill sits on the balance sheet, a high-premium deal also affects leverage ratios (equity is unchanged but total assets grow) and later impairment risk.

### 22.4.3 Consideration choices and protections

*Fixed exchange ratio.* The target receives a fixed number of acquirer shares per target share. The headline value floats with the buyer's price; the target bears the price risk between signing and closing.

*Fixed value (floating ratio).* The target receives a fixed value in shares, with the number of shares adjusting to the acquirer's price. The acquirer bears the price risk through the ownership dilution it concedes.

*Collar.* A band combining the two: within the band the ratio is fixed, and outside it the ratio floats to keep the value at a floor or a cap. Collars limit both parties' exposure to share price swings during the (often months-long) period between signing and closing.

*Earn-outs and contingent value rights.* Part of the price is deferred and paid if the target meets targets (revenue, EBITDA, regulatory approval). They bridge differences in valuation and align interests of sellers who stay on, but invite disputes about how the metrics are measured and manipulated.

*Deal protections.* Break-up fees (commonly a few percent of deal value), no-shop clauses with "fiduciary out" exceptions, matching rights, and lock-ups deter competing bidders; courts examine them for reasonableness. *Material adverse change* (MAC) clauses allow the buyer to walk away in extreme circumstances, but the bar is high.

**Example 22.4 – Fixed ratio versus fixed value and a collar.**
Aventra's price at signing is 40, the exchange ratio is 39 / 40 = 0.975 Aventra shares per Lumio share, and Lumio has 80 million shares. Compare outcomes at closing for different Aventra prices.

| Aventra price at close | Fixed ratio: value per Lumio share | Premium to 30 | Fixed value (39): ratio | Shares issued (m) | Lumio holders' ownership |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 44 | 42.90 | 43.0% | 0.886 | 70.9 | 15.1% |
| 40 | 39.00 | 30.0% | 0.975 | 78.0 | 16.3% |
| 36 | 35.10 | 17.0% | 1.083 | 86.7 | 17.8% |
| 32 | 31.20 | 4.0% | 1.219 | 97.5 | 19.6% |

*Calculations.* Fixed ratio at 36: 0.975 × 36 = 35.10; premium 35.10 / 30 − 1 = 17.0%. Fixed value at 36: ratio = 39 / 36 = 1.0833; shares = 80 × 1.0833 = 86.7; ownership = 86.7 / (400 + 86.7) = 17.8%.

*A collar.* Suppose the parties agree to a fixed ratio of 0.975 for Aventra prices between 36 and 44, a floor of value 35.10 below 36, and a cap of 42.90 above 44. At 32: the ratio floats to 35.10 / 32 = 1.0969, so Aventra issues 80 × 1.0969 = 87.75 million shares and Lumio holders own 87.75 / 487.75 = 18.0%. At 48: ratio = 42.90 / 48 = 0.894, Aventra issues 71.5 million shares.

*Interpretation.* Under a fixed ratio, Lumio's holders bear a swing from a 43% to a 4% premium. Under a fixed value, Aventra's holders concede ownership from 15.1% to 19.6%, a larger share of the synergy surplus when its stock has fallen. The collar splits the risk and provides a defined worst case for each party. Note that the share price may fall precisely because the market doubts the deal, so a fixed-value structure can create a "death spiral" in which a falling price leads to more issuance.
