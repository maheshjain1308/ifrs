# Chapter 11: Shareholder Activism, Proxy Contests and Shareholder Rights

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why shareholder activism exists in terms of agency costs, the free-rider problem and the economics of concentrated blocks, and compute the break-even stake and success probability for an activist campaign.
- Describe the stages of the modern activist playbook, the main categories of activist demands, and the evidence on how markets react to activist involvement.

## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario. Harlow Brands is a fictional consumer-products company.*

On a Tuesday morning in March, the chair of Harlow Brands received a courteous four-page letter. Tidewater Capital, a fictional activist fund, disclosed that it owned 6.1 percent of Harlow's shares and wished to "share its perspective on unlocking value." The letter was polite. The attached presentation was not. It showed that Harlow's operating margin trailed its closest peers by six points, that the company carried a household-appliance division that had consumed capital for a decade without earning its cost of capital, and that three of the nine directors had served for more than fifteen years. It proposed a sale or spin-off of the appliance division, a margin plan, an additional $400 million of buybacks, and two new independent directors with operating experience.

Harlow's CEO was initially inclined to dismiss the letter. The share price was only 8 percent below its five-year high, the board was staggered, and the company had a poison pill with a 15 percent trigger. But the chief financial officer ran a quick exercise: if Tidewater's numbers were right, the stock was worth 25 percent more under the activist plan. Harlow's top ten holders owned 48 percent of the shares, and two of them were index managers whose published voting guidelines favored board refreshment after twelve years of tenure. Within a week the stock rose 7 percent on volume, the proxy advisors had asked for meetings, and three large holders had called the investor relations desk.

The board now faced a set of decisions that no single chapter of finance theory fully answers. Should it engage privately or fight publicly? Is the pill a shield or a provocation? What will institutional holders actually do when the ballot arrives? And underneath every tactical question lay a more basic one, which is whether Tidewater had found genuine value or merely a good story.

This chapter provides the tools to answer such questions: the economics of the activist's position, the mechanics of voting, the legal architecture of takeover defense, and the incentives of the institutions that decide the outcome.

## 11.1 The Governance Problem Behind Activism

### 11.1.1 Agency costs and the bundle of shareholder rights

Volume 1 (Chapter 15) treated the separation of ownership and control as a source of agency costs: managers may over-invest, retain cash, entrench themselves, or take perquisites, because they bear only a small part of the value they destroy. Corporate law responds with a bundle of shareholder rights whose purpose is to make the agency problem tolerable. The core rights are: to vote on the election and removal of directors; to vote on fundamental transactions such as mergers, charter amendments and asset sales; to propose resolutions under defined conditions; to receive information; to sue directors for breach of fiduciary duty; and to sell shares freely. The economic content of these rights is simple. Shareholders are the residual claimants, so they have the strongest incentive to maximize firm value, and the law gives them the power to replace the people who run the firm.

The difficulty is that the power is rarely used. A shareholder owning 0.01 percent of a company captures 0.01 percent of any value improvement, yet pays 100 percent of the cost of analyzing the firm, mobilizing other investors and voting an informed ballot. This is the rational apathy and free-rider logic formalized by Grossman and Hart in their 1980 analysis of takeovers: each small holder prefers that someone else pay the cost of change, because the gain accrues to all holders in proportion to their shares regardless of who incurred the cost. Attempted takeovers and activist campaigns are public goods for the shareholder base, and public goods are under-supplied.

### 11.1.2 Voice, exit and the value of a block

Albert Hirschman distinguished between exit (selling) and voice (arguing for change). For a diversified investor with a small position, exit is almost always cheaper than voice. For an investor with a large block, exit is costly because selling depresses the price and surrenders the potential gain, while voice has a payoff that scales with the block. Shleifer and Vishny's 1986 analysis of large shareholders makes this point precisely: a block holder internalizes enough of the benefit of monitoring to justify the cost, and so concentrated ownership can partly solve the free-rider problem.

Activist funds are professional block holders designed to exploit this logic. They accept concentration risk, typically holding a handful of positions of 3 to 10 percent, and they build specialized capabilities in valuation, legal strategy, communications and director recruitment that make the cost of voice low and the probability of success higher than for a passive investor. Their compensation, a management fee plus a share of profits (commonly 20 percent) above a hurdle, rewards them for converting a share-price gap into realized gains.

Gilson and Gordon have emphasized that activists therefore occupy a particular institutional niche. Diversified institutions, such as index funds and many mutual funds, are "rationally reticent": they do not originate campaigns, but they hold the votes. Activists originate and finance campaigns, but hold few votes. The outcome of a contest is often determined by whether the reticent majority is persuaded that the activist's plan beats management's. This division of labor, rather than any ideology, explains most of the behavior discussed in this chapter.

### 11.1.3 What activism is trying to fix

Activists tend to target a recognizable set of governance and strategic failures:

- **Capital allocation.** Persistent investment in divisions earning less than the cost of capital, acquisitions at high premiums, and cash hoards. In the language of Volume 1 (Chapter 6 and Chapter 11), the firm accepts projects with negative NPV or fails to return capital whose best use is outside the firm.
- **Portfolio structure.** Conglomerate discounts, where the sum of the parts is worth more than the whole, and divisions that would be worth more to another owner.
- **Operating performance.** Margin gaps against peers that management cannot explain.
- **Governance and incentives.** Long-tenured boards, pay that is weakly linked to returns on capital, and entrenchment devices.
- **Balance sheet.** Under-leveraged companies whose capital structure leaves tax shields unused (Volume 1, Chapter 15), or over-leveraged ones that need deleveraging.

A recurring analytical task for boards is to separate these claims into those that are verifiable, those that are arguable, and those that are wrong. Activism is not self-validating: a campaign can be well-organized and still be mistaken about value. The activist's incentive is to profit, not to be right, though over a repeated game the two are correlated.

## 11.2 The Activist Playbook

### 11.2.1 Stages of a campaign

Although each campaign is different, the structure is recognizable.

**1. Target selection.** The activist screens for firms with identifiable gaps: low valuation relative to peers, a diversified portfolio, poor returns on invested capital, a weak or entrenched board, and a share register in which institutions hold a high percentage of the votes. A company with a staggered board and a majority-vote vulnerability may be less attractive than one with annual elections.

**2. Accumulation.** The fund accumulates shares up to the disclosure threshold. In the United States, a person who acquires beneficial ownership of more than 5 percent of a class of voting equity must file a Schedule 13D, and under the amended rules this filing is due within five business days of crossing the threshold. Between the first purchase and the filing date, the activist can keep buying quietly, which allows it to build a stake before the market learns of its interest. Derivatives, including cash-settled swaps and options, can provide economic exposure without counting toward the threshold in some jurisdictions, though this practice is controversial and subject to evolving regulation. Regulatory merger-control filings (the Hart-Scott-Rodino rules in the United States) can also apply to acquisitions above specified sizes, even for investors with no intention of control.

**3. Private engagement.** The activist typically approaches the board or CEO privately to present its thesis. Many campaigns end here, with management adopting some or all of the proposals. The activist's credibility depends on a readiness to go public.

**4. Public escalation.** If private talks fail, the activist publishes an open letter and a detailed presentation, often with a website. It lobbies other shareholders, sell-side analysts and the press. The public stage sets the narrative that proxy advisors and large holders will evaluate.

**5. Formal action.** The activist can nominate directors, submit shareholder proposals, call a special meeting where charter and bylaws permit, or solicit written consents. Nomination is subject to advance-notice bylaws that fix a window (commonly 90 to 120 days before the anniversary of the previous annual meeting). Missing the window is fatal, which is why boards draft bylaw provisions carefully and why courts scrutinize them.

**6. Settlement or vote.** Most campaigns that reach the formal stage settle. A typical settlement adds one to three directors (some of them activist nominees, others mutually agreed independents), establishes a board committee to review strategy, and includes a standstill: the activist agrees not to exceed a specified ownership level or run another campaign for a period such as twelve months, and to vote with the board on certain matters. Fewer campaigns go to a vote, and those that do are expensive.

**7. Exit.** After the strategic review or buyback, the activist sells shares into the price recovery. A holding period of one to three years is typical, though there are many exceptions.

### 11.2.2 Types of activists

| Activist type | Typical stake | Primary tools | Time horizon | Principal constraint |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hedge fund activist | 3-10% | Letters, board nominations, proxy fights, settlement | 1-3 years | Concentration and liquidity risk; fund redemptions |
| Wolf pack (loosely coordinated funds) | Combined 8-15%, each below thresholds | Parallel purchases, shared thesis | Months to 2 years | Group-filing rules; legal risk if deemed coordinated |
| Public pension fund | Small (under 1%) | Votes, proposals, engagement, letters | Long | Political pressures; diversified portfolio |
| Strategic or corporate activist | 5-20% | Board seats, bid threat | Long | Antitrust and regulatory approval |
| Retail or "gadfly" proponent | Minimal | Shareholder proposals, questions at meetings | Variable | Eligibility thresholds and low vote shares |
| Thematic or ESG-oriented investor | Small to moderate | Proposals, engagement, vote-no campaigns | Long | Depends on the support of large institutions |

The key distinction is between investors who can credibly threaten to take control of the board and those who can only signal views. The second group depends almost entirely on persuading others.

A "wolf pack" is a loosely coordinated set of funds that each buy below the 5 percent threshold, or file separately, yet together hold a large block. Legal treatment depends on whether the investors have formed a "group" under securities law: coordination is permitted only up to the point at which it amounts to an agreement to act together in acquiring, holding or voting shares. This is a gray area that regulators and litigants dispute.

### 11.2.3 Types of demands and the value mechanism

| Demand | Mechanism of value | Primary risk |
|---|---|---|
| Divest or spin off a division | Removes conglomerate discount; redeploys capital | Stranded costs; tax leakage; the "sum of parts" may be illusory |
| Return capital (buyback, special dividend) | Reduces agency cost of free cash flow; raises ROE | Leverage rises; buybacks near highs destroy value |
| Cost and margin program | Closes the gap to peer margins | Underinvestment; short-term focus |
| Sale of the company | Control premium captured by shareholders | Limited buyers; deal risk; undervaluation if timing is poor |
| Board change | Better oversight and capital discipline | Activist directors may serve the activist's horizon |
| Leveraged recapitalization | Tax shield and discipline (Volume 1, Chapter 15) | Financial distress costs |

The managerial lesson is that the demand set maps onto classical corporate finance questions: Is the firm earning its WACC on each business? Is it over- or under-leveraged? Is there a buyer who can use the assets better? An activist who asks these questions is not inventing a new theory. The more serious debate is about horizon and execution.

### 11.2.4 The economics of the activist's position

The activist's calculation can be written down. Let the market capitalization be V, the stake fraction s, the proportional value uplift if the campaign succeeds be u, the probability of success be p, the price impact of accumulating the stake (the premium paid over the undisturbed price) be π, and the fixed cost of the campaign be C. Then:

```
Expected profit = p × u × V × s  −  π × V × s  −  C
```

The first term is the stake's share of the value created. Note what is *not* in the formula: the activist captures only fraction s of the uplift, while the other 1 − s of the shareholders obtain the rest for free. That is the free-rider problem, and it sets a floor on the stake the activist must hold.

**Example 11.1 – Break-even economics of an activist campaign.**

Harlow Brands has a market capitalization of V = $5,000 million. Tidewater buys s = 6% ($300 million at the undisturbed price) and, because its buying lifts the price, pays an average premium of π = 4% above the undisturbed price. It estimates the campaign (advisers, solicitation, legal) will cost C = $12 million. If the plan succeeds, it believes the equity value rises by u = 15%.

Step 1: cost of the stake over and above undisturbed value.
```
Price-impact cost = π × V × s = 0.04 × 5,000 × 0.06 = $12.0 million
```
Step 2: gain on the stake if successful.
```
Uplift on whole firm = 0.15 × 5,000 = $750 million
Tidewater's share      = 750 × 0.06  = $45 million
Other shareholders     = 750 × 0.94  = $705 million (free ride)
```
Step 3: expected profit as a function of p.
```
E[profit] = p × 45 − 12 − 12 = 45p − 24
p = 0.3:  13.5 − 24 = −$10.5 million
p = 0.5:  22.5 − 24 = −$1.5 million
p = 0.7:  31.5 − 24 = +$7.5 million
```
Step 4: break-even success probability.
```
p* = 24 / 45 = 53.3%
```
Step 5: break-even stake at p = 0.5. Profit per unit of stake is 0.5 × 0.15 × 5,000 − 0.04 × 5,000 = 375 − 200 = 175 per unit of s, so
```
E[profit] = 175 × s − 12  =>  s* = 12 / 175 = 6.86%
```
At s = 9% the profit would be 175 × 0.09 − 12 = +$3.75 million; at 6% it is −$1.5 million.

Interpretation: At 6% and a 50 percent chance, Tidewater loses money in expectation, even though the campaign creates $750 million of value for shareholders in total if it succeeds. The campaign is socially valuable but privately marginal. This arithmetic explains why activists seek larger stakes, lower accumulation costs (derivatives, quiet buying before the 13D filing), cooperative co-investors that share the campaign cost, and settlements that make success probable. It also explains why the market often assigns a premium merely on the news of an activist stake: investors are pricing the probability p, not the outcome.

### 11.2.5 Common mistakes in analyzing activist claims

Three errors recur.

1. **Confusing the headline gap with achievable value.** A comparison of the firm's multiple to the average peer multiple ignores the reasons for the gap. Boards should decompose any claimed uplift into assumptions about margin, growth, capital intensity and risk, and test each.
2. **Ignoring execution cost.** Spin-offs create stranded costs and separation expenses; buybacks reduce financial slack. The cost of a transaction shows up in the denominators of the value case.
3. **Treating the share price reaction as proof.** A positive abnormal return on announcement shows that the market assigns some probability to value creation. It does not show that the plan is correct, and it tells you little about the long-term outcome.

### 11.2.6 What the evidence says

A sizeable empirical literature, including work by Brav, Jiang, Partnoy and Thomas and by Bebchuk, Brav and Jiang, finds that the announcement of a hedge fund activist stake is associated with positive abnormal returns of several percent on average, and that targets tend to show improved operating performance and payouts over the following years. Critics argue that some of the improvement reflects selection (activists choose firms about to improve), that effects on long-term investment and employment are contested, and that the average masks a wide dispersion of outcomes. A balanced reading is that activism is, on average, associated with positive abnormal returns without long-term reversals in the typical sample, that it varies greatly across campaigns, and that its causal effect is hard to isolate. Boards should therefore neither dismiss activism as short-termism by default nor accept it as invariably value-creating.

The following example shows how the standard tool, the event study, is applied.

**Example 11.2 – Measuring the market reaction to an activist disclosure.**

Tidewater's 13D filing becomes public on day 0. Using a market model estimated on prior data, Harlow's expected return is α + βRm with α = 0.03% per day and β = 1.1. The daily standard deviation of Harlow's abnormal returns in the estimation window is 1.5%. Observed data:

| Day | Harlow return | Market return | Expected return (0.0003 + 1.1 × market) | Abnormal return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| −1 | 0.4% | 0.2% | 0.03% + 0.22% = 0.25% | 0.15% |
| 0 | 6.2% | 0.1% | 0.03% + 0.11% = 0.14% | 6.06% |
| +1 | 1.1% | −0.3% | 0.03% − 0.33% = −0.30% | 1.40% |

Step 1: cumulative abnormal return over the window [−1, +1].
```
CAR = 0.15% + 6.06% + 1.40% = 7.61%
```
Step 2: test statistic. Under independence the standard deviation of the three-day CAR is σ√n.
```
SE = 1.5% × √3 = 2.598%
t  = 7.61 / 2.598 = 2.93
```
A t-statistic of 2.93 is significant at the 1 percent level.

Step 3: dollar value.
```
7.61% × $5,000 million = $380.5 million
```
Interpretation: The market added about $380 million to Harlow's equity value. Compare this with Example 11.1: the 6 percent stake is worth 6% × 380.5 = $22.8 million immediately, against $24 million of price impact and campaign costs. The announcement return alone does not cover Tidewater's costs, so it still needs the plan to succeed. Note the small positive abnormal return on day −1: leakage or pre-announcement buying by others shows up before the formal disclosure, a pattern commonly observed.


## 11.3 Voting Mechanics and the Proxy Contest

### 11.3.1 From record date to certified result

Shareholders vote on matters at annual or special meetings, almost always by proxy. The process has a precise sequence, and activists and boards both win or lose on details.

1. **Record date.** The board fixes the date on which ownership determines who may vote. Shares bought after the record date do not carry votes for that meeting. This matters for activists accumulating late and for the phenomenon of "empty voting," discussed below.
2. **Solicitation.** Each side distributes proxy materials and a proxy card. A dissident running its own slate files a proxy statement with the securities regulator and mails or e-delivers it to holders. The cost is large: legal fees, proxy solicitors, printing and communication can run into many millions for a large company, and the incumbent board usually spends company money while the dissident spends its own unless it wins and seeks reimbursement.
3. **Quorum.** The meeting needs a minimum percentage of shares represented (often a majority). Retail apathy makes quorum a real issue for some companies, which is why they hire solicitors to chase turnout.
4. **Voting standard.** The company's charter or bylaws determine how much support wins. The key variants are in the table below.
5. **Tabulation.** An independent inspector of elections counts the proxies, resolves disputes over the validity of cards, and certifies the result. When two proxy cards are submitted by one holder, the latest dated card controls.

### 11.3.2 Voting standards

| Standard | How it works | Implication for contests |
|---|---|---|
| Plurality | Nominees with the most votes win, up to the number of seats. "Withhold" votes do not defeat a nominee in an uncontested election. | In a contested election, the dissident needs more votes than the incumbent nominee on each seat. |
| Majority voting | In an uncontested election, a nominee must receive more "for" than "against" or "withheld." Resignation policies often apply. | Gives shareholders a real vote-no weapon. Contested elections generally revert to plurality. |
| Cumulative voting | Each share gets as many votes as there are seats; the holder can concentrate them on fewer candidates. | Allows a minority to elect representatives. |
| Staggered (classified) board | Directors serve multi-year terms, with only a fraction elected each year. | Slows any change of control of the board to two annual meetings or more. |
| Supermajority | A higher threshold (for example two-thirds) for certain actions. | Raises the cost of removal, merger approval or charter change. |

Broker non-votes are another detail. In the United States, brokers may vote uninstructed shares only on "routine" matters. Director elections are not routine, so uninstructed retail shares do not count as votes cast for director elections. In an uncontested election, this reduces turnout; in a contested election, retail participation is driven by solicitation and by the incumbent's access to the retail list.

### 11.3.3 The universal proxy card

For decades, a dissident and the incumbent each had their own card, and a holder voting on one card could not pick individual nominees from both slates. This was a significant advantage for incumbents, who could attack the dissident's whole slate without worrying that a few of their own directors would be dropped. The United States adopted a universal proxy rule, applicable to contested meetings after August 2022, that requires both sides to list all nominees on a single card so that each holder can choose among the whole field. Several other jurisdictions already allowed split voting, since in the UK, for example, voting is on individual resolutions at a meeting.

The consequence is a competition on the merits of individual candidates. Activists tend to nominate fewer candidates, aimed at a minority of seats, and put forward highly qualified people, because a universal card allows holders to vote for the strongest of both sides. Boards can no longer argue that a vote for the activist's nominee means a vote against the entire incumbent team. The result has been more settlements, since the incumbents' risk of losing individual seats rose.

### 11.3.4 The arithmetic of a contest

Contest outcomes follow from three quantities: the number of shares that actually vote, the support within each constituency, and the influence of advisors.

**Example 11.3 – Counting votes in a proxy contest.**

Harlow has 200 million shares outstanding. Tidewater holds 16 million (8%) and will vote them for its nominees. The remaining shares are held by other institutions (124 million) and retail investors (60 million). Turnout is expected to be 92% for institutions and 45% for retail. Retail holders tend to favor incumbents; assume that only 20% of retail votes cast support the dissident.

Step 1: votes cast.
```
Institutions voting = 124 × 0.92 = 114.08 million
Retail voting       =  60 × 0.45 =  27.00 million
Tidewater           =               16.00 million
Total votes cast    =              157.08 million
Majority threshold  = 157.08 / 2  =  78.54 million
```
Step 2: retail support for the dissident.
```
Retail for Tidewater = 27.00 × 0.20 = 5.40 million
```
Step 3: institutional support Tidewater needs.
```
Needed = 78.54 − 16.00 − 5.40 = 57.14 million
As a share of institutional votes cast = 57.14 / 114.08 = 50.09%
```
So the institutions are the swing vote, and Tidewater needs just over half of them.

Step 4: two scenarios driven by the proxy advisor recommendations. Suppose that when the leading advisors recommend the dissident slate, 65% of institutional votes follow; when they recommend against, only 25% do.
```
Advisor supports:  16 + 0.65×114.08 + 5.40 = 16 + 74.15 + 5.40 = 95.55 million → 60.8% (win)
Advisor opposes:   16 + 0.25×114.08 + 5.40 = 16 + 28.52 + 5.40 = 49.92 million → 31.8% (lose)
```
Interpretation: A swing of 40 percentage points in the institutional vote, from the advisor recommendation, flips the result, and the activist's own 8 percent and the retail vote are tiny by comparison. This is why both sides spend the campaign courting a few dozen institutional holders and two advisory firms, and why index managers' voting policies matter. It is also why the sensible unit of analysis is the share register, not the shareholder count.

### 11.3.5 Cumulative voting and minority representation

With cumulative voting, a holder multiplies the number of shares by the number of seats up for election and may allocate that total across candidates as desired. The minimum share of voting stock needed to guarantee k of N seats is:

```
Threshold = k / (N + 1)    (plus one share)
```

**Example 11.4 – How little it takes under cumulative voting.**

A company has 60 million voting shares and elects N = 9 directors in a single election. A dissident wants k = 2 seats.

Step 1: threshold.
```
Shares needed = 60,000,000 × 2 / (9 + 1) + 1 = 12,000,001 shares  (20% of votes cast)
```
Step 2: verification. The dissident casts 12,000,001 × 9 ≈ 108.0 million votes, split over two candidates at about 54.0 million each. The other holders have 48,000,000 × 9 = 432.0 million votes. To elect seven candidates they can allocate about 61.7 million to each (432 ÷ 7), above the dissident's 54.0 million, so they win seven seats. To elect an eighth candidate they would need over 54.0 million votes for it and for each of the other seven, a total of 8 × 54.0 = 432 million plus one vote, which they do not have. The dissident therefore guarantees two seats.

Step 3: compare with other regimes.
```
Straight voting, annual election of all 9: the majority holder (50%+1) elects all 9 seats.
Cumulative, N = 9, k = 1: 10% threshold.   k = 3: 30% threshold.
Cumulative with staggered board, N = 3 seats up, k = 1: 25% threshold.
```
Interpretation: Cumulative voting converts a 20 percent block into two board seats, where straight voting gives it none. It is rare among large public companies, in part because management and boards dislike factional boards; staggering the board raises the threshold since fewer seats are up in any year (the denominator N + 1 shrinks). Boards that want to dilute minority power therefore combine a staggered board with a small number of seats per class.

### 11.3.6 Votes without economic interest

Votes and economic interest can be separated. A holder who borrows shares just before the record date and returns them just after, or who hedges the economic exposure with derivatives while keeping the votes, has "empty voting" power. Hu and Black described this phenomenon, which can lead to votes cast in ways that reduce the value of the shares being voted. Securities lending by index funds also removes votes from the register unless the lender recalls shares before the record date. Regulators and large institutions now often recall loans before important votes. For the board, the point is practical: reconcile the record-date register with the actual economic holders before a contest.

## 11.4 Takeover Defenses

### 11.4.1 The conflict of interest

A hostile bidder offers shareholders a premium, usually 25 to 40 percent over the undisturbed price, to sell directly to the bidder, bypassing the board. Directors have an obvious conflict: they may lose their positions. The legal and economic question is whether defenses protect shareholders by allowing the board to bargain for a higher price and resist coercive or opportunistic bids, or protect managers from discipline. Both effects exist. The empirical literature is mixed and depends on the defense: staggered boards, in particular, have been associated in several studies with lower valuations, while the role of poison pills in raising bid premia is a classic subject of debate.

### 11.4.2 The poison pill

The shareholder rights plan, or poison pill, was designed in the 1980s by the law firm Wachtell, Lipton. The board issues one right per share as a dividend. The rights are initially worthless and cannot trade separately. If a person or group acquires more than a threshold percentage (commonly 10 to 20 percent) without board approval, the rights become exercisable:

- **Flip-in.** All holders other than the acquirer may buy additional shares of the target at a deep discount, typically half the market price. The acquirer's stake is diluted and its economic loss is large.
- **Flip-over.** If the target is merged into the acquirer afterward, holders may buy shares of the acquirer at a discount.

The board can redeem the rights for a nominal amount (such as $0.001 each) at any time before they are triggered. So the pill does not bar a deal; it bars an *unapproved* acquisition and forces the bidder to negotiate with the board, which can then redeem the pill for a bidder that offers an acceptable price.

**Example 11.5 – How a flip-in right dilutes an acquirer.**

Harlow has 100 million shares at $40, so equity value is $4,000 million. A rights plan sets a 15% trigger. A hostile acquirer crosses the trigger and holds 15 million shares ($600 million at market). Each share carries one right. Upon triggering, each right (except the acquirer's) allows its holder to pay an exercise price of $80 and receive shares with a market value of twice the exercise price, $160 — that is, 4 shares at the $40 pre-trigger price.

Step 1: shares issued if all other holders exercise.
```
Rights held by others = 100 − 15 = 85 million
New shares issued     = 85 × 4 = 340 million
Cash received         = 85 × $80 = $6,800 million
```
Step 2: post-dilution price (assuming the firm's operating value is unchanged and the cash is added to its assets).
```
Total equity value = 4,000 + 6,800 = $10,800 million
Total shares       = 100 + 340 = 440 million
Price per share    = 10,800 / 440 = $24.55
```
Step 3: the acquirer.
```
Acquirer's stake     = 15 / 440 = 3.41% (down from 15%)
Value of its holding = 15 × 24.55 = $368.2 million (down from $600 million)
Loss                 = $231.8 million, or 38.6%
```
Step 4: the other holders. Each exercising holder pays $80 and receives 4 shares worth 4 × 24.55 = $98.18, a gain of $18.18 per right. Holders who do not exercise would suffer the same dilution as the acquirer, which is why the exercise right is crucial.

Interpretation: The pill does not make the acquisition impossible, it makes it ruinous without the board's consent. In practice the rights are never exercised, because no rational bidder crosses the trigger. The pill's real function is to give the board time and bargaining power, and to deter creeping accumulation of control. Observe that an activist who stays below the trigger (say 9.9 percent) is not affected. For this reason, some companies set a low trigger, even 10 percent or less, against activists, and courts have scrutinized whether such pills are a proportionate response to a threat. The Delaware Chancery Court in the Sotheby's litigation upheld a pill with a lower threshold for active investors than for passive ones, on the facts presented to it, but boards should not infer that every such pill would survive.

### 11.4.3 Staggered boards and other structural defenses

| Defense | Mechanism | Effect on a bidder or activist | Typical critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Staggered (classified) board | Three classes, one elected per year | Control needs two annual meetings; proxy fight takes at least 24 months to win majority | Entrenchment; associated with lower valuation in several studies |
| Poison pill | Dilution on unapproved crossing of trigger | Forces negotiation with the board | Can be used to block premium bids |
| Supermajority provisions | Two-thirds or more to approve merger or remove directors | Raises vote hurdle | Gives a small block veto power |
| Limits on written consent and special meetings | Shareholders cannot act between annual meetings | Slows an activist | Reduces accountability |
| Advance notice bylaws | Strict deadlines and information requirements for nominations | Procedural obstacles for dissidents | Can be used to disqualify late or imperfect nominations |
| Dual-class shares | Founders hold super-voting stock | Control cannot be acquired without the controlling holder | Wedge between votes and cash flow (see Section 11.5) |
| Golden parachutes | Large payments to executives on change of control | Raises the cost of acquisition | Rewards failure if poorly designed; can align executives with a sale if well designed |
| Anti-takeover statutes | For example Delaware Section 203, which bars certain business combinations for three years after a holder crosses 15% without board approval | Deters creeping control | Limits market for control |

Staggering is especially potent combined with a pill. A bidder who wins a tender offer but cannot replace the board for two years cannot redeem the pill, and so cannot complete a merger. This was the structure tested in the Airgas litigation, where the Delaware courts permitted a board with a staggered structure and a pill to decline a bid it considered inadequate until the shareholders elected a new majority, a ruling that confirmed the strength of the combination.

### 11.4.4 Law: Delaware and the United Kingdom

| Feature | Delaware (United States) | United Kingdom |
|---|---|---|
| Board's power to defend | Allowed, subject to enhanced scrutiny (*Unocal*): the board must show a reasonable threat and a proportionate response | Board may not take frustrating action without shareholder approval once an offer is imminent (Takeover Code, General Principle and Rule 21) |
| Poison pills | Permitted; upheld in *Moran v. Household International* (1985) and subsequent cases | Effectively unavailable for the same reason |
| Duty when selling the company | *Revlon* duties: maximize price when a sale or break-up is inevitable | Board must give independent advice and cannot prefer a bidder without shareholder consent |
| Mandatory bid | No general rule | A holder reaching 30% must make an offer to all shareholders (Rule 9) |
| Timetable | Flexible | Tight timetable; bidder must "put up or shut up" within 28 days of a public approach (Rule 2.6) |
| Nomination procedures | Governed by bylaws and charter | Shareholders holding 5% or more may requisition a general meeting; directors may be removed by ordinary resolution |
| Voting on directors | Typically annual or staggered | Annual re-election is standard under the corporate governance code for large companies |

The contrast reflects different philosophies: the Delaware approach trusts the board, subject to judicial review of its motives and proportionality, while the UK approach reserves the decision to shareholders. Delaware law also recognizes that where a board's actions interfere with shareholder voting, the board must show a compelling justification, as in *Blasius Industries v. Atlas Corp.* (1988). A manager of an international firm should therefore treat "takeover defense" as a jurisdiction-specific topic.

### 11.4.5 Valuing the decision to defend

Defenses cost shareholders something when the bidder withdraws and the price falls back, but they may increase the price when they allow the board to negotiate. The decision is an expected-value problem with a time dimension, which the next example makes explicit.

**Example 11.6 – Should the board use the pill to negotiate?**

Harlow's unaffected price is $50. A hostile bidder offers $62 in cash. The board's cost of equity is 10% per year. Two paths are available.

*Path A (no defense).* The offer closes with probability 0.85 in three months (0.25 year). If it fails, the price returns to $50.

*Path B (use the pill and negotiate).* The board negotiates for nine months (0.75 year). Outcomes: with probability 0.60 the bidder raises to $68; with probability 0.15 a white knight pays $65; with probability 0.25 the bidder walks away and the price returns to $50 (taken as received immediately, since the share is simply held).

Step 1: value of A.
```
PV of deal   = 0.85 × 62 / 1.10^0.25 = 52.70 / 1.0241 = $51.46
Failure      = 0.15 × 50 = $7.50
Value A      = 51.46 + 7.50 = $58.96
(Undiscounted expectation: 0.85 × 62 + 0.15 × 50 = $60.20)
```
Step 2: value of B.
```
Deals: (0.60 × 68 + 0.15 × 65) = 40.80 + 9.75 = $50.55 received in 0.75 year
PV   = 50.55 / 1.10^0.75 = 50.55 / 1.0741 = $47.06
Walk-away = 0.25 × 50 = $12.50
Value B   = 47.06 + 12.50 = $59.56
(Undiscounted expectation: $63.05)
```
Step 3: break-even raised price. The value of B equals A when the discounted deal term equals 58.96 − 12.50 = 46.46, i.e. undiscounted deal receipts of 46.46 × 1.0741 = 49.90, so that 0.60 × X + 9.75 = 49.90 and X = $66.92.

Interpretation: The undiscounted expected values (60.20 versus 63.05) overstate the advantage of resistance by about $2.85 per share, because negotiation takes longer and has a failure risk. After discounting, B beats A by only $0.60, and the board needs to extract at least $66.92 in the "raised bid" outcome to justify resistance. If the walk-away probability were higher than 25 percent, or if the price following a failed bid would fall below $50 (a typical outcome when the market was pricing a premium), resistance would not pay. Boards should run this calculation explicitly rather than assuming that "we got a better price" justifies the delay, and should recognize that their own sense of the probabilities is subject to the optimism discussed in Chapter 12.


## 11.5 Dual-Class Shares and the Control Wedge

### 11.5.1 Votes without cash flow rights

A dual-class structure gives one class of shares (often held by founders) multiple votes per share and the other class one vote or none. Several large technology and media companies have used such structures, and some issue non-voting shares to the public. The effect is a wedge between control rights and cash-flow rights. The founder controls the firm with a small fraction of the economic claim, and so bears only a small part of the cost of a value-reducing decision.

Grossman and Hart's analysis of the private benefits of control, and later work by Bebchuk, Kraakman and Triantis on control-enhancing mechanisms, show why the wedge matters. A controlling holder will take an action when its private benefit exceeds its share of the loss in firm value. With a cash-flow stake c, the founder takes any action with private benefit B and value loss L when:

```
B > c × L        i.e.  L < B / c
```

The lower c is, the larger the value-destroying actions the founder will tolerate.

Supporters of dual-class structures respond that founders may have a unique vision, that short-term pressure from the market can harm long-run innovation, and that investors know the structure at the time of purchase and price it accordingly. These arguments are more persuasive for a young firm than for a mature one, which is why many index providers, institutional investors and governance advocates favor *sunset* provisions that convert the super-voting shares to ordinary shares after a fixed period or when the founder's stake falls below a threshold, and why some index providers have restricted the inclusion of companies with unequal voting rights.

**Example 11.7 – The wedge in numbers.**

A company has 100 million Class A shares carrying 10 votes each and 900 million Class B shares carrying one vote each. The founder holds all of the Class A shares and 45 million Class B shares.

Step 1: cash-flow rights.
```
Founder shares   = 100 + 45 = 145 million
Total shares     = 1,000 million
Cash-flow stake c = 145 / 1,000 = 14.5%
```
Step 2: voting rights.
```
Founder votes = 100 × 10 + 45 × 1 = 1,045 million
Total votes   = 100 × 10 + 900 × 1 = 1,900 million
Voting share  = 1,045 / 1,900 = 55.0%
```
Step 3: wedge ratio.
```
Wedge = 55.0% / 14.5% = 3.79
```
Step 4: incentives. Suppose the founder can pursue an acquisition that benefits the founder privately by B = $20 million but reduces firm value by L = $100 million.
```
Founder's share of the loss = 0.145 × 100 = $14.5 million < $20 million benefit → the founder proceeds
With one-share-one-vote and 55% ownership: 0.55 × 100 = $55 million > $20 million → the founder declines
Break-even loss the founder will tolerate: L* = 20 / 0.145 = $137.9 million
```
Interpretation: Under dual-class, the founder will accept a loss of up to $137.9 million to gain a private benefit of $20 million; under one-share-one-vote at the same control, the limit is $20 / 0.55 = $36.4 million. The wedge thus multiplies the loss that the controller will impose on minority holders by about 3.8. Investors price this risk through a lower multiple, and governance structures such as independent directors, related-party transaction approvals by minority holders, and sunset clauses are the tools that reduce the discount.

## 11.6 Institutional Investors and Proxy Advisors

### 11.6.1 Who decides

In most large public companies, the shareholder register is dominated by institutions: index and passive managers, active mutual funds, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers and hedge funds. Their incentives differ in ways that affect voting.

| Investor type | Typical incentive | Approach to voting and engagement |
|---|---|---|
| Passive index manager | Low fees; no ability to exit a company without leaving the index | Policy-driven voting guided by published guidelines; engagement through stewardship teams; rarely originates campaigns |
| Active mutual fund | Outperform benchmark; can sell | Analysis-driven; may support activists if the thesis is credible; sometimes sells instead of voting |
| Public pension fund | Long-term liabilities; political oversight | Principled positions on governance; sometimes initiates proposals |
| Sovereign wealth fund | Long horizon; reputational concerns | Often low-profile; votes by guidelines |
| Hedge fund (event-driven) | Short-term profit | May support or oppose depending on the payoff structure |
| Insurers | Liability matching | Engagement, but limited activism |

The largest index managers collectively hold a substantial share of most large-cap registers, which gives them great influence on outcomes. Bebchuk and Hirst, in "The Specter of the Giant Three," argue that their incentives to invest in stewardship are weak because they earn a small fee per dollar and their funds compete on cost. Defenders argue that large index managers have expanded their stewardship teams and that their long-term horizon and inability to sell make engagement rational. The truth is probably between: large investors do engage, but their capacity is thin relative to the number of companies they hold, and they depend on policy-based voting.

### 11.6.2 Incentives of passive holders

A passive manager cannot sell a poorly performing company without tracking error. This gives it a stake in company improvement, but the return from improving any single company is small relative to the cost of engagement because the manager holds thousands of positions and its fee does not depend on relative performance. Passive managers therefore adopt standardized policies, such as board diversity targets, limits on director over-boarding, or voting against directors at companies with unequal voting rights, and apply them at scale. For a board, these policies become de facto governance standards.

When an activist campaign arises, passive managers analyze the issues using a standard framework: Is the company's performance poor relative to peers? Are the activist's proposed changes plausible? Does the board's response appear credible? Many have indicated that they prefer settlement to a vote, and they often ask for a face-to-face meeting with the director nominees. In the vignette, Harlow's index holders had already published a policy favoring board refreshment after long tenures, which put the incumbent board on the defensive before the campaign began.

### 11.6.3 Proxy advisors

Institutions that vote thousands of ballots every spring outsource part of the analysis to proxy advisory firms, the best known of which are ISS and Glass Lewis. The advisors publish voting policies, analyze each contest, and issue recommendations. Because votes follow recommendations to a significant degree, an advisor's call can influence a contest, as Example 11.3 illustrated. The influence is a subject of controversy: companies complain of errors, conflicts of interest and one-size-fits-all policies, while institutions emphasize that they make their own decisions and use advisors for research and operational support. Many large managers have reduced their reliance on advisory recommendations, devoting internal analysis to the biggest holdings. Regulators have considered rules covering proxy advisor conflicts and disclosure; the rules and their enforcement have changed over time, so managers should consult current requirements.

For boards, the lesson is practical: communicate directly with the largest holders and advisors well before the contest, and respond to published policies by explaining departures rather than assuming they will be ignored.

### 11.6.4 Say-on-pay and engagement

Dodd-Frank (2010) required advisory shareholder votes on executive compensation in the United States, and the EU's revised Shareholder Rights Directive (2017) created similar rights across member states. Say-on-pay votes are non-binding but have a strong signaling effect: a failed vote prompts a board response, often changes to pay design, and is a leading indicator of weak institutional confidence. In an activist campaign, compensation is a common line of attack, because it is easy to illustrate a gap between pay and performance. A compensation committee that has already engaged with holders and aligned pay with returns on capital is harder to attack.

## 11.7 Stewardship, Engagement and the Evolving Rights Agenda

### 11.7.1 Stewardship codes

Stewardship codes set expectations for how institutional investors monitor, engage with and vote at investee companies. The UK Stewardship Code, first issued by the Financial Reporting Council in 2010 and substantially revised in 2020, asks signatories to report on how they integrate stewardship and investment, how they engage, how they escalate, and what outcomes they achieve. Japan introduced a stewardship code in 2014, followed by a corporate governance code in 2015, as part of an effort to raise returns on capital in listed companies. The EU's Shareholder Rights Directive, as revised in 2017, requires institutional investors and asset managers to disclose engagement and voting policies on a comply-or-explain basis. These codes are generally voluntary or comply-or-explain, and they work by disclosure and reputation, not by legal compulsion.

The economic logic is to reduce the cost to institutions of acting as monitors and to encourage "voice" over "exit." Criticism has focused on box-ticking: reports can describe process more than substance. Still, the codes have changed the market by normalizing engagement, making voting records public, and creating an expectation that large holders will escalate when private dialogue fails.

### 11.7.2 Rights that change the contest

Several shareholder rights shift the balance between boards and holders, and boards should know where they stand on each.

- **Majority voting for directors.** Replaces plurality in uncontested elections so that a director who fails to win majority support is expected to offer a resignation.
- **Proxy access.** Allows long-term holders meeting an ownership threshold to include their own nominees on the company's proxy statement. A common market design in the United States requires holdings of 3 percent for three years and permits nominees up to 20 percent of the board, with a cap on the number of holders who may aggregate their shares into a group.
- **Declassification.** Moves the board to annual elections. Many large companies have done so following shareholder pressure.
- **Right to call a special meeting and to act by written consent.** Reduces the time an activist must wait for the annual meeting.
- **Shareholder proposals.** In the United States, Rule 14a-8 allows eligible shareholders to include a proposal in the company's proxy statement, subject to ownership thresholds and subject matter exclusions. Such proposals are generally precatory, meaning they ask but do not bind, but high votes pressure boards to respond.
- **Removal rights.** Shareholders may remove directors by simple majority in many jurisdictions; in others, removal is allowed only for cause when the board is classified.

### 11.7.3 Environmental, social and governance proposals

Shareholder proposals on climate, human capital and political spending have become more common. Their effect depends on the support of large institutions, which vary in how they treat them: some evaluate each proposal for materiality to long-term value and the company's current disclosures, and others have become more cautious in recent years. The corporate finance question is the same as for any activist demand: does the proposal identify a risk or opportunity that affects cash flows or discount rates, and is the board's response proportionate? Treating ESG proposals as an "activism" category separate from valuation, rather than as inputs to the cost of capital and growth assumptions (Volume 1, Chapter 11 and Chapter 21), is a mistake in both directions.

### 11.7.4 The short-termism debate

A central objection to activism is that it shortens corporate horizons: activists press for buybacks and cost cuts, investment suffers, and long-run value is sacrificed for quick returns. Advocates reply that managers also have short horizons (pay, tenure, reputational concerns), that capital returned to investors is not destroyed but reallocated, and that the evidence for long-term harm is weak. Both can be partly right: activism is beneficial when it removes empire-building and discipline failure and harmful when it pressures a firm with genuine long-horizon investment opportunities to under-invest. The relevant test is the one applied throughout this book: does the proposed action raise the present value of expected cash flows after accounting for risk? A board that can show that its capital allocation clears the cost of capital, with explicit hurdle rates and post-audit results, has the best defense against the short-termism charge being turned against it.

## 11.8 Preparing for and Responding to an Activist

### 11.8.1 Think like the activist

The most effective preparation is a self-assessment that adopts the activist's lens, often done with outside advisors. It examines:

1. **Valuation gap.** Compare the firm's multiples and returns on capital with peers and with a sum-of-the-parts valuation. Identify the divisions with the lowest returns.
2. **Capital allocation record.** Review acquisitions and capital projects against the promised returns. Incorporate post-audit evidence.
3. **Governance profile.** Board tenure, skills, independence, committee structure, and the link between pay and performance.
4. **Share register.** Who owns the stock, how they vote, how they have voted in other contests, and which holders are swing voters.
5. **Structural defenses.** Which provisions exist, how a court or the holders would view them, and which would provoke more opposition than they prevent.
6. **Communication.** Whether the investor narrative is credible and consistent, and whether the board speaks to holders directly.

### 11.8.2 Engagement choices

| Response | When it makes sense | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Private engagement and partial adoption | The activist's analysis has merit; the demands are compatible with the board's strategy | Appearing weak; the activist returns for more |
| Settlement with board seats | The vote is uncertain; the cost of a contest is large; the nominees are credible | Dilutes board cohesion; sets a precedent |
| Public rebuttal with data | The thesis rests on errors that can be demonstrated | Escalation; focuses on personalities |
| Strategic review | Value of a sale or separation is genuinely uncertain | Signals that the company is for sale |
| Structural defense (pill, bylaw changes) | A threat to control without a premium; creeping accumulation | Governance backlash; legal exposure if disproportionate |
| Full contest | Board is confident of the value case and the vote | Cost; distraction; risk of losing seats |

### 11.8.3 Settlement terms

Settlements usually specify the number and identity of new directors, board committee assignments, a standstill period during which the activist may not increase its stake beyond a stated percentage or launch a campaign, a voting commitment (to support the board's slate at the next meeting), non-disparagement, and expense reimbursement. Each term shifts risk. A standstill protects the board but may reduce the activist's incentive to cooperate, and a short standstill invites a second campaign. Boards should evaluate the settlement the same way they would evaluate any contract: what is the cost of the concession, what do they gain, and what is the alternative if they refuse?

### 11.8.4 Common mistakes by boards

- **Dismissing the thesis because of the messenger.** The activist's motives do not change the validity of its analysis.
- **Adopting defenses after the campaign begins.** Late defensive moves attract litigation and criticism and may be judged in a harsher light than defenses adopted in calm.
- **Neglecting the swing holders.** Spending resources on rebuttal rather than on talking to the ten investors whose votes determine the outcome.
- **Over-promising.** A board that responds with aggressive targets it cannot meet has made the activist's case at the next earnings call.
- **Inconsistent messages.** Management says the strategy is working; the board agrees to review it.

The managerial lesson is that the best defense against activism is a business that earns its cost of capital, a board that holds management to account, and a share register that understands the strategy. Structural defenses can buy time, but time is useful only if it is used to address the gap that attracted the activist.

## Case Study: Harlow Brands and Tidewater Capital

*Illustrative case. All names and figures are fictional.*

Harlow Brands (market capitalization $5.0 billion; 200 million shares; price $25) is a consumer-products company with two segments. Branded household goods (about 70 percent of operating profit) earn a return on invested capital of 13 percent and have a WACC of 8 percent. The appliance division, with $900 million of invested capital, earns 4.5 percent against a WACC of 8.5 percent. Group operating margin is 11 percent against peer median 17 percent. Over the past decade, Harlow has made three appliance acquisitions at average premiums of 35 percent, each described as "transformational." The board has nine directors in three classes; the average tenure is eleven years, and two directors have served for eighteen. The CEO has been in office for twelve years, and long-term incentive pay is based on adjusted earnings per share growth. A rights plan with a 15 percent trigger was adopted two years ago in response to a rumored approach.

Tidewater Capital discloses a 6.1 percent stake (acquired at an average of $24.20) and proposes: (1) sale or spin-off of the appliance division, which it values at 0.9 times invested capital, or $810 million, against the current implied carrying value of $900 million; (2) a margin plan to reach 14 percent in three years; (3) a $400 million buyback; (4) two new directors; and (5) incentive pay tied to return on invested capital. Tidewater estimates that these steps would raise the equity value by 25 percent over two years.

The CEO's initial reaction is to refuse to meet. The chair, a director for fifteen years, notes the staggered board and says that it would take Tidewater two years to win control. The lead independent director argues that the appliance numbers cannot be dismissed and that the board's own reputation depends on its response. The CFO prepares an analysis showing that a spin-off would leave $55 million of stranded corporate costs and trigger tax leakage of around $60 million, partly offsetting the benefit. She also notes that the $400 million buyback would raise net debt to 2.3 times EBITDA, from 1.5 times, which is within the covenant but would reduce the room for the next acquisition. Tidewater's analysts respond that stranded costs can be eliminated over eighteen months and that the divisions' tax basis is higher than the company assumes.

The share register shows 48 percent held by the top ten institutions, of which three are index managers with published policies on board tenure, two are active managers with a history of supporting engagement, and one is a pension fund that has voted against directors at companies with persistent underperformance. After Tidewater's letter, the stock rises to $26.70. The proxy advisors request meetings. An analyst note raises the possibility that a strategic buyer might be interested in the appliance division at about one times invested capital.

Three weeks later Tidewater signals that it will nominate two directors at the next annual meeting, within the advance-notice window, and publishes a presentation titled "Harlow: Closing the Gap." The board must decide whether to engage, negotiate or contest. It also must consider whether to bring forward a declassification proposal and whether the pill should be amended.

## Key Takeaways

- Activism exists because diversified shareholders face a free-rider problem. Concentrated, specialized holders can internalize enough of the gain to pay for monitoring and for a campaign.
- An activist captures only its pro rata share of any value created. The break-even stake and probability of success (Example 11.1) explain accumulation tactics, co-investment and the preference for settlement.
- The activist playbook has defined stages: accumulation to the disclosure threshold, private engagement, public escalation, formal nomination, and settlement or vote. Most campaigns settle.
- Announcement returns are positive on average, but they price the probability of success and do not prove that the plan is correct. Evidence on long-term effects is contested and varies widely across campaigns.
- Voting rules decide the contest: record date, plurality versus majority voting, cumulative voting, staggered boards and the universal proxy card. The arithmetic of turnout and swing holders determines the result.
- Institutional holders and proxy advisors are the swing vote. Passive managers vote by policy, so published guidelines are de facto governance standards.
- The poison pill raises the cost of unapproved accumulation by diluting the acquirer. It does not prevent a deal approved by the board, but combined with a staggered board it is a strong barrier.
- The value of defense is an expected-value, time-dependent question. Resistance pays only if the extra price exceeds the cost of delay and the risk of losing the bid (Example 11.6).
- Dual-class structures create a wedge between control and cash-flow rights, increasing the loss a controller will tolerate for a given private benefit. Sunset provisions and minority protections reduce the discount.
- Delaware and UK law give opposite answers on board power to defend. International firms must treat takeover defense as jurisdiction-specific.
- The best protection against activism is performance: returns above the cost of capital, a credible board and direct engagement with the share register before a campaign begins.
