# Chapter 20: Divestitures, Spin-offs, SPACs and Dual-Class Structures

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

- Compare the main forms of corporate separation (asset sale, spin-off, split-off, equity carve-out, tracking stock) and choose among them using value, tax, control and financing criteria.
- Quantify the value created or destroyed by a separation using a sum-of-the-parts analysis net of dis-synergies and one-time costs, and allocate debt and tax basis in a spin-off.
- Explain, in general terms, the logic of tax-free spin-offs, the conditions typically attached to them, and the restrictions on subsequent transactions.
- Analyze SPAC economics: the sponsor promote, warrants, redemption rights, and dilution, and compute net cash and value per share under different redemption scenarios.
- Evaluate dual-class share structures, measuring the wedge between voting and cash-flow rights and summarizing the governance arguments for and against them, including sunset provisions.
- Compare IPOs, direct listings, SPAC mergers and sales as routes to public markets, including the full cost of each.

## Opening Vignette

*(Illustrative scenario. Calder Industries and its subsidiaries are fictional.)*

Calder Industries is a diversified manufacturer with two divisions that share a corporate headquarters, a name and a balance sheet. Its Industrial division makes pumps and valves, a mature, cyclical business with EBITDA of 300 million. Its Consumer division makes premium kitchen appliances and has EBITDA of 150 million, growing faster, with higher margins and heavy marketing needs. Calder's consolidated enterprise value is 3.9 billion, about 8.7 times EBITDA, and net debt is 1.5 billion.

For three years the CEO, Priya Raman, has heard the same message from investors: the Consumer division deserves a multiple like that of branded-goods peers, and the Industrial division a multiple like that of industrial peers, yet the market blends them into a discount. An activist fund has bought 4 percent of the shares and published a letter proposing a separation. Raman's board has three alternatives: sell Consumer to a strategic buyer who has indicated an interest at a price that would trigger a large taxable gain; spin Consumer off to shareholders as a separate listed company; or sell a minority stake in Consumer in an IPO and keep control.

Meanwhile a founder-led company in a different industry, one Calder's venture arm has invested in, is deciding how to go public. Its founder wants to retain control and has been courted by a SPAC sponsor promising speed and certainty, while its bankers argue for a traditional IPO, and a third adviser suggests a direct listing. The founder wants a share structure in which her shares carry ten votes each.

Both situations raise the questions of this chapter. What does a separation do to value, and how much of the apparent gain is real? What are the tax logic and the economics of each route? What does a SPAC actually cost the company relative to an IPO? When does a dual-class structure create value and when does it entrench? We will use Calder throughout the first half of the chapter and the founder's company in the second half.

## 20.1 The Map of Corporate Separations

### 20.1.1 Why firms separate

Volume 1, Chapter 22 treated acquisitions as a way to create value through synergy. Separations are the mirror image. They are justified when a business is worth more to someone else, or on its own, than inside the current parent. The standard motivations are:

- **Focus.** Management attention, capital allocation and incentive pay can be tailored to a single business. Diversification discount research, associated with Lang and Stulz and with Berger and Ofek, found that diversified firms tended on average to trade at a discount to the sum of their stand-alone parts, though later work has debated how much of this is a measurement artifact or reflects the selection of firms that diversify when their opportunities are poor.
- **Investor clienteles and transparency.** A pure-play firm is easier to value, to cover by analysts and to compare with peers, and can attract investors who would not hold the conglomerate.
- **Capital allocation.** Internal capital markets may cross-subsidize weak divisions (the "socialism" critique of Scharfstein and Stein). Separation forces each business to compete for capital at its own cost.
- **Incentives and currency.** A separately listed business can grant equity compensation tied to its own performance and can use its own shares for acquisitions.
- **Strategic fit and regulation.** The business may be a drag on the parent's credit rating, face antitrust constraints, or need a different capital structure (a high-growth unit needs equity, a cash-generating unit supports debt).
- **Monetization.** The parent may simply need cash, to repay debt or fund a better opportunity.

Against these must be set the costs: lost synergies (shared procurement, shared overhead, cross-selling), **dis-synergies** from duplicated corporate functions, one-time separation costs (IT systems, legal entities, stranded costs), loss of diversification of cash flows (which may raise the cost of debt of each part), and, in a taxable transaction, tax.

### 20.1.2 The menu

| Form | What happens | Cash to parent? | Control after | Typical tax outcome (general) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset or subsidiary sale (divestiture) | Sold to a strategic or financial buyer for cash or stock | Yes | Buyer | Taxable to the seller on gain over tax basis |
| Spin-off | Subsidiary shares distributed pro rata to parent shareholders | No (except via debt raised by subsidiary) | Separate public company, same shareholders | Can be tax-free if conditions are met |
| Split-off | Parent shareholders offered subsidiary shares in exchange for parent shares | No | Subset of holders | Can be tax-free if conditions are met |
| Equity carve-out | Minority stake in subsidiary sold to public investors in an IPO | Yes (subsidiary or parent receives proceeds) | Parent keeps control | Often taxable only to the extent of gain on the shares sold |
| Tracking stock | Parent issues a class of shares whose dividends track a division | No | Parent retains legal ownership | Typically no tax event on issuance |
| Sale via reverse merger (e.g., Reverse Morris Trust) | Spin-off followed by merger of the spun entity with a third party | Possibly (via debt) | Third party plus old holders | Can be tax-free if ownership tests are met |

The tax column is a generalization. Tax-free treatment depends on detailed statutory conditions that vary by country and change over time; readers must consult tax counsel before relying on any of it.

### 20.1.3 Evidence in brief

Empirical studies of spin-offs, associated with Schipper and Smith, Hite and Owers, Cusatis, Miles and Woolridge, and others, have generally reported positive announcement-period returns on average, and some have found positive subsequent performance of both parent and spun-off firm, particularly where the separation increases focus. Studies of asset sales have also tended to find positive average announcement returns for sellers, in line with the view that sales redeploy assets to higher-valued uses. Later research has documented that some of the post-spin gains are associated with the spun-off company or the parent being acquired. These are statements about averages across samples; individual transactions vary widely, and no manager should assume that a separation creates value merely because it is a separation. The remainder of this chapter shows how to test whether a specific one does.

## 20.2 Divestitures: Selling a Business

### 20.2.1 The hold-sell decision

A divestiture is a capital budgeting decision in reverse. The parent should sell if the after-tax proceeds exceed the value of retaining the business, where value of retention is the present value of the after-tax cash flows the business will generate *to this owner*, including synergies. Since the buyer's value includes their own synergies, a sale happens when the buyer can generate more than the seller (Volume 1, Chapter 22). The parent's analysis should therefore compare:

```
Sell if:  Net after-tax proceeds  >  PV of retained cash flows (to this owner)
Net after-tax proceeds = Price − Tax on gain − Transaction costs − Stranded costs (PV)
Tax on gain = t × (Price − Tax basis)
```

Two points deserve emphasis. First, **tax basis** matters. A business bought long ago, or built organically, usually has a low tax basis, so a sale triggers tax on nearly the whole price. The same business separated in a qualifying tax-free spin-off, by contrast, may trigger no corporate-level tax. That is one reason spin-offs are popular. Second, **stranded costs**, the shared overhead formerly absorbed by the divested business, remain with the parent unless eliminated. Ignoring them overstates the benefit.

### 20.2.2 Consideration and structure

A buyer may pay in cash, stock, or both. Cash gives the seller certainty but crystallizes the tax; stock can sometimes defer tax but exposes the seller to the buyer's performance. Common contract elements include earn-outs (deferred contingent consideration tied to performance, discussed in Volume 1, Chapter 22 as a real option), transition services agreements (the seller supplies IT, payroll or logistics for a limited period), indemnities and escrows, and non-compete clauses. In a **carve-out sale** to a private equity buyer (Volume 1, Chapter 23), financing is usually largely debt, so the parent's price depends on credit market conditions.

### 20.2.3 Worked comparison: sell or spin

**Example 20.1 – Sale versus tax-free spin-off of Calder's Consumer division.** A strategic buyer offers 1,800 million in cash for the Consumer division. Consumer's tax basis is 600 million, the corporate tax rate on the gain is 25 percent, and transaction costs are ignored. The alternative is a spin-off that would be tax-free to Calder and to its shareholders if conditions are met, in which case Consumer would be worth 1,704 million as a stand-alone company (derived in Example 20.2).

Sale:

```
Gain             = 1,800 − 600 = 1,200
Tax on gain      = 25% × 1,200 = 300
After-tax cash   = 1,800 − 300 = 1,500
```

Spin-off: shareholders receive Consumer's stand-alone value, 1,704, with no corporate tax and no current shareholder tax in the stylized case, but Calder receives no cash.

At first sight the spin-off (1,704) beats the sale (1,500) by 204. The two are comparable on an enterprise-value basis; in practice the parent would also have Consumer borrow and pay a dividend to Calder before the spin, so that Calder can reduce its own debt, which shifts value between the two equity stubs without changing the total.

The taxable-sale alternative would be preferable only if the buyer's price exceeds the spin value by at least the tax: to match 1,704 after tax, the sale price P must solve P − 0.25(P − 600) = 1,704, so 0.75P + 150 = 1,704, P = 2,072. The buyer would have to pay about 2,072 million, 15 percent above its 1,800 offer, to leave Calder's shareholders as well off as the spin. Equivalently, the tax cost of 300 is a hurdle the sale price must clear. If the buyer's synergies are large enough to justify 2,072, the sale is better; if not, the spin is. Note that this ignores the possibility that a spin's value would be lower or higher than 1,704 in the market, which is uncertain, whereas a cash price is certain: certainty has value, as does a competitive auction.

## 20.3 Spin-offs

### 20.3.1 Mechanics

In a spin-off, the parent distributes shares of a subsidiary pro rata to its own shareholders. Each parent shareholder ends up with shares of both companies in proportion to the original holding, and no cash changes hands. The steps are: the parent establishes the subsidiary as a separate legal entity (the "separation"), contributes assets, negotiates a separation agreement (assets, employees, contracts, intellectual property, and the allocation of liabilities), allocates debt, arranges a tax ruling or opinion where appropriate, files a registration or information statement with the securities regulator, obtains stock exchange listing, declares a distribution with a record date, and settles the distribution. A "when-issued" market typically emerges before the distribution date, allowing price discovery.

Several decisions deserve care:

- **Capital structure of each entity.** Parents frequently have the spun entity borrow and pay a *cash dividend to the parent* just before the spin. This lets the parent reduce its own leverage while delivering a stand-alone capital structure to the new firm. Each entity's leverage should be set for its own business risk, which is where the Volume 1, Chapter 15 trade-off theory applies.
- **Governance.** Each firm needs its own board, management, and incentive plans. Antitakeover provisions of the spun-off firm are a common subject of criticism.
- **Commercial arrangements.** Transition services, supply agreements and shared intellectual property agreements govern the relationship after the spin. A poorly designed agreement can destroy value through conflict.
- **Employee and pension allocation.** Pension liabilities need to be allocated with an actuarial view.
- **Index and shareholder base effects.** After a spin, the new company may be too small, or in the wrong sector, for the funds that hold the parent, and these funds may sell. This *flowback* selling often creates temporary downward price pressure on the new shares, a source of the short-term underperformance and of the opportunities that spin-off specialists look for.

### 20.3.2 Tax-free logic, described generally

In many tax systems, a distribution of shares of a subsidiary can be tax-free to both the distributing corporation and its shareholders if it meets conditions designed to ensure that the transaction is a genuine reorganization and not a disguised cash distribution or a way to sell a business without tax. In the United States, the relevant provisions are in Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code. Broadly, and without attempting to state every requirement, the typical conditions include:

- the parent controls the subsidiary and distributes all or substantially all of its stock;
- both businesses are engaged in an active trade or business that has been conducted for a minimum period;
- the transaction has a genuine corporate business purpose other than tax avoidance, and is not a "device" for distributing earnings;
- the historic shareholders maintain a continuing interest in both firms; and
- there are limits on acquisitions of either company, or of sizeable stakes, shortly after the distribution, since a spin followed by a taxable sale would otherwise let the parent sell the subsidiary without paying tax (the so-called Morris Trust issue, and the reason for the Reverse Morris Trust ownership thresholds).

Other countries have different rules, and some tax distributions of shares as dividends in kind unless relief applies. The managerial points are these. First, qualification is valuable (in Example 20.1, it avoids 300 million of tax), so the company will plan the transaction to satisfy the conditions and will often obtain an external tax opinion or a ruling. Second, qualification restricts what the parent and the spun-off company can do for some period afterward, notably a sale to a third party, and these restrictions are normally enforced through a tax matters agreement that allocates liability if a later action causes the spin to be taxable. Third, for the *shareholder* the typical consequence of a qualifying spin is deferral: no tax at distribution, with the original tax basis allocated between the two holdings, usually in proportion to their relative market values immediately after the spin, and the holding period carried over.

### 20.3.3 Valuing the separation: sum of the parts

The central analysis is a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation, in which each division is valued separately using appropriate multiples or DCF (Volume 1, Chapter 21), the values are added, and the total is compared with the current consolidated value. The *gross* gain is the difference. The *net* gain subtracts dis-synergies and one-time costs. The analysis is only as good as its inputs: the multiples applied to each division must be those of truly comparable stand-alone peers, and the dis-synergies must be realistic.

**Example 20.2 – Calder's sum of the parts.** Calder's consolidated enterprise value is 3,900 million, net debt 1,500 million and shares 100 million. Industrial EBITDA is 300 and Consumer EBITDA is 150, so the blended multiple is 3,900 / 450 = 8.67 times. Pure-play peers trade at 9 times EBITDA for industrial businesses and 12 times for branded consumer businesses. Separation would create stand-alone dis-synergies of 20 million of annual EBITDA (12 in Industrial, 8 in Consumer) and one-time costs of 90 million.

Step 1 – Gross SOTP:

```
Industrial: 300 × 9  = 2,700
Consumer:   150 × 12 = 1,800
SOTP                 = 4,500
Conglomerate discount = 4,500 − 3,900 = 600  (13.3% of 4,500)
```

Step 2 – Capitalize the dis-synergies at each division's multiple:

```
Industrial: 12 × 9  = 108
Consumer:    8 × 12 =  96
Total                = 204
```

Step 3 – Net enterprise value after separation:

```
Post-separation EV = 4,500 − 204 = 4,296
Less one-time costs = 4,296 − 90 = 4,206
Net gain vs. current 3,900 = 306  (7.8% of 3,900)
```

Step 4 – Equity value per share. Net debt of 1,500 is allocated as 1,000 to Industrial and 500 to Consumer (3.5 times each division's post-dis-synergy EBITDA: 1,000 / 288 = 3.47 and 500 / 142 = 3.52). Equity values are:

```
Industrial equity: (2,700 − 108) − 1,000 = 1,592  → 15.92 per share
Consumer equity:   (1,800 −  96) −   500 = 1,204  → 12.04 per share
Combined                                    = 2,796 → 27.96 per share
Less one-time costs (90 / 100 shares)       = 0.90
Net value per original share                = 27.06
```

Current equity value is 3,900 − 1,500 = 2,400, or 24.00 per share. The spin therefore adds 3.06 per share, a 12.75 percent equity gain, from an enterprise value gain of 7.8 percent. This illustrates how leverage amplifies the equity effect: the same 306 million of enterprise value gain is spread over an equity base of 2,400.

Several cautions apply. The 13.3 percent conglomerate discount is the *gross* figure; 204 + 90 = 294 of it, or nearly half, is consumed by dis-synergies and costs, leaving a net gain of 306. A 1-turn lower multiple for Consumer (11 instead of 12) would reduce the Consumer value by 150, partly offset by 8 lower capitalized dis-synergies, cutting the net gain to about 164, so the conclusion is sensitive to the peer multiple. And, importantly, the *market* may already reflect the expected separation (activist pressure raises the share price), in which case the announcement return will be smaller. Finally, the figures above are values the market *could* assign; there is no guarantee. Separation changes the information environment and the investor base, but it does not by itself create cash flows.

### 20.3.4 Basis and per-share allocation

**Example 20.3 – Allocating tax basis and checking leverage in the spin.** Calder distributes one Consumer share for each Calder share held (a 1-for-1 distribution). Immediately after the spin the shares trade at 15.92 (Industrial, the continuing "RemainCo") and 12.04 (Consumer), per Example 20.2's values before the one-time cost, which we ignore for allocation. A shareholder who owned one Calder share with a tax basis of 18.00 allocates basis in proportion to market values:

```
Total post-spin value per original share = 15.92 + 12.04 = 27.96
Industrial share of value = 15.92 / 27.96 = 56.94%
Consumer share of value   = 12.04 / 27.96 = 43.06%
Basis allocated to Industrial = 18.00 × 0.5694 = 10.25
Basis allocated to Consumer   = 18.00 × 0.4306 =  7.75
```

If the shareholder later sells the Consumer share at 15.00, the gain is 15.00 − 7.75 = 7.25 (rather than the 15.00 − 18.00 = −3.00 loss that would result if she mistakenly assigned the whole basis to Consumer). The allocation matters for later tax. Leverage check: Industrial's net debt / post-dis-synergy EBITDA is 1,000 / 288 = 3.47 times and Consumer's 500 / 142 = 3.52 times, similar at the design stage, though Consumer's higher growth and need for marketing investment would argue for lower leverage, and Industrial's cyclicality argues for lower leverage too. A careful design would run each entity through a coverage stress test (Volume 1, Chapter 14) and might shift debt to the more stable cash generator. Credit ratings of both entities, and the cost of refinancing the parent's existing bonds (which may have change-of-control or asset-sale covenants), must be checked as well.

### 20.3.5 What goes wrong

The common failures are: separating businesses that are interdependent (shared plants, customers or technology) so that dis-synergies exceed the multiple arbitrage; loading the spun-off company with too much debt, leaving it unable to invest (a form of "dumping"); weak governance of the spun company; underestimating stranded costs at the parent, leaving RemainCo with an overhead too large for its size; and *pricing pressure from flowback selling* in the first months, mistaken by management for a verdict on the strategy. Creditors may also object: a spin that transfers value away from the borrowing entity can raise fraudulent transfer concerns if the parent is or becomes insolvent (Chapter 19), and bondholders sometimes seek protection through covenants on asset sales and distributions.

## 20.4 Carve-Outs, Split-Offs, Tracking Stocks and Related Structures

### 20.4.1 Equity carve-outs

In an **equity carve-out**, the parent sells a minority stake (typically 10 to 30 percent) in a subsidiary to public investors, often through an IPO, and keeps control. The proceeds may go to the subsidiary (a primary offering, funding growth) or to the parent (a secondary offering, a monetization). Carve-outs create a market price for the subsidiary, which can reveal value and permit equity-linked compensation. They have two natural uses: as a *stage* before a full separation (the parent sells a stake now and distributes the rest later), and as a way to raise capital against a business the market values more highly than the parent. Empirical evidence, associated with Schipper and Smith and with Allen and McConnell, finds positive average announcement returns on average, particularly where proceeds are used to reduce debt.

The costs are the IPO costs discussed in Section 20.5, plus the *governance conflicts* of a controlled company: minority shareholders of the subsidiary depend on a parent that may favor its own interests in transfer pricing, shared services and capital allocation. The parent also gives up part of the cash flows of a business without receiving the full price of control.

**Example 20.4 – Cost of carving out 20 percent of Consumer.** Calder considers listing 20 percent of Consumer in an IPO instead of a spin. Consumer's equity is worth 1,204 million at fair value (Example 20.2). Investors require a 10 percent IPO discount to the fair price, and the gross spread is 5 percent of proceeds.

```
Fair value of 20% stake   = 0.20 × 1,204          = 240.8
Offer price discount 10%  → gross proceeds         = 216.7
Gross spread 5%           = 0.05 × 216.7           =  10.8
Net proceeds to Calder    = 216.7 − 10.8           = 205.9
Retained 80% (fair value) = 0.80 × 1,204           = 963.2
Value to Calder           = 205.9 + 963.2          = 1,169.1
Cost of the carve-out     = 1,204 − 1,169.1        =  34.9 (2.9% of Consumer equity)
```

The transaction costs 34.9 million, equal to 14.5 percent of the 240.8 million of fair value sold. The 24.1 million discount is a transfer to new investors that may or may not be recovered through re-rating of the retained stake, while the 10.8 million spread is permanent. Carve-outs are therefore most valuable when there is a specific reason to sell now: to fund a high-return investment, to retire costly debt, or to create the price discovery needed for a later spin.

### 20.4.2 Split-offs

In a **split-off**, parent shareholders are offered the chance to *exchange* parent shares for subsidiary shares, typically at a premium. Because tendering shareholders give up parent stock, the parent's share count falls, so a split-off is similar to a share repurchase paid in subsidiary stock (Volume 1, Chapter 16). It can be tax-efficient for the same reasons a spin-off is, and it is attractive when the parent wants to reduce its share count while separating a business, but it requires shareholders to choose, and it typically leaves a share structure in which each participating investor ends up with one company or the other.

### 20.4.3 Tracking stocks

A **tracking stock** is a class of parent equity whose dividends and market price are intended to track the performance of a division. The parent retains legal ownership; the tracking shareholders have claims on the parent, not on the division. Advantages include a market price for the division and equity compensation tied to its performance without legal separation, which avoids tax and preserves synergies. Disadvantages are that governance conflicts between the classes are real: the board owes duties to all shareholders of the company, resources can be shifted between divisions, and creditors have recourse to all of the parent's assets. Many tracking stock structures have been unwound, which suggests that the structure has often been a half-measure.

### 20.4.4 Reverse Morris Trust

In a **Reverse Morris Trust**, the parent spins off a subsidiary and the subsidiary immediately merges with a third party, such that the parent's shareholders own a substantial minority stake in the combined business. Provided that ownership thresholds are met and that the spin otherwise qualifies, the parent can in effect sell a business to a third party in a transaction that is tax-free at the corporate level, and it can extract cash through debt raised by the subsidiary before the merger. The requirement that the old shareholders retain a minimum ownership stake in the combined company limits the size of the third party relative to the subsidiary, which is why such deals typically involve a partner smaller than, or of similar size to, the spun business.

## 20.5 Going Public: IPOs, Direct Listings and Their Costs

### 20.5.1 The traditional IPO

In a firm-commitment IPO, underwriters buy the shares from the issuer and resell them to investors, run a bookbuilding process to find the price, and receive a gross spread. Direct costs are the gross spread (often in the mid-single-digit percent range for mid-sized offerings, lower for very large ones) and other expenses (legal, accounting, listing fees). The *indirect* cost is **underpricing**: shares are on average priced below their first-day closing price, so existing shareholders sell shares at a discount. Explanations, from Volume 1, Chapter 13, include the winner's curse of uninformed investors (Rock), information production by underwriters through bookbuilding (Benveniste and Spindt), and the use of underpricing as a reward for investors who reveal information. Ritter's long-run data on US IPOs show average first-day returns that have varied substantially across periods. The key conceptual point for the issuer is that money left on the table is a real cost, equal to the first-day gain on the shares *sold by the company and by selling shareholders*, though the cost to *existing* holders is limited to shares sold, because retained shares benefit from the pop.

The IPO also brings **lock-up agreements** (typically restricting insiders from selling for a period such as 180 days), a stabilization mechanism (the "greenshoe" over-allotment option, normally up to 15 percent of the base deal), analyst coverage and a defined price discovery event.

### 20.5.2 Direct listings

In a **direct listing**, the company lists its shares on an exchange without a traditional underwritten offering. Existing holders may sell into the opening auction, the exchange publishes a reference price based on private-market information, and the opening price is set by the exchange's auction according to orders. No gross spread is paid to underwriters (financial advisers are paid advisory fees), there is typically no mandatory lock-up, and no underpricing in the sense of a discount set by bookbuilders, although the opening price can differ from fundamental value, up or down. Rules on whether a direct listing may include a primary capital raise have been developed by exchanges and regulators over time and vary, so a company should check current rules. The principal disadvantages are that there is no guaranteed capital or committed buyer base, that there may be greater price volatility at the start, and that there is no stabilization or marketing process by underwriters. It suits well-known firms with a broad base of investors who already know them and that have no immediate capital need.

### 20.5.3 Cost comparison

**Example 20.5 – IPO versus direct listing for the founder's company.** The company wants to raise 300 million of primary capital. In an IPO it would sell 15 million shares at 20. Gross spread is 6 percent, other expenses are 6 million, and the first-day return is 15 percent. A direct listing costs 18 million in advisory, legal and listing fees and raises capital by selling shares in the opening auction (assume a primary-capable listing is available) at an opening price assumed equal to fair value, then adjusted in a sensitivity.

IPO route:

```
Gross proceeds        = 15M × 20            = 300.0
Gross spread          = 6% × 300            =  18.0
Other expenses                              =   6.0
Money left on table   = 15% × 300           =  45.0   (shares sold at 20, worth 23)
Total economic cost   = 18.0 + 6.0 + 45.0   =  69.0   (23.0% of the 300 raised)
```

Direct listing route:

```
Fees                                         =  18.0
Pricing error (if opening price is 10% below fair value): 0.10 × 300 = 30.0
Total, base (no pricing error)               =  18.0  (6.0% of 300)
Total, 10% below fair value                  =  48.0  (16.0% of 300)
Total, 10% above fair value                  = −12.0  (a gain)
```

In the base case the direct listing saves 51.0 million (69.0 − 18.0). The direct listing wins as long as the opening price is not more than about 17 percent below fair value: 18 + x × 300 = 69, x = 17.0 percent. The comparison depends on two things: whether an expected 15 percent first-day return is representative (the IPO's underpricing is a *systematic* expected cost), and whether the direct listing brings pricing *risk* both ways. The main benefit of the IPO is not captured in the numbers: certainty of capital raised and a book of long-term investors. A risk-averse founder who needs the money may rationally pay for it.

### 20.5.4 Dual-track and other alternatives

Many companies run a **dual-track** process, preparing for an IPO while soliciting private buyers; the credible IPO alternative disciplines bidders, and the sale alternative provides a floor. Other routes include a growth-equity or crossover round, a sale to a private equity sponsor (Volume 1, Chapter 23), listing on a smaller exchange or tier, reduced-disclosure regimes for small offerings (whose thresholds vary by country), and the SPAC merger, to which we now turn.

## 20.6 Special Purpose Acquisition Companies

### 20.6.1 How a SPAC works

A **special purpose acquisition company (SPAC)** is a shell company that raises cash in an IPO with the purpose of merging with a private operating company, which thereby becomes public. The standard structure, which dominated the market in 2020 and 2021 and has since evolved, has these features:

- **IPO of units.** Investors buy units, typically at 10.00, each consisting of one share and a fraction of a warrant to buy a share at a strike such as 11.50.
- **Trust account.** The proceeds are placed in a trust account invested in short-term government securities. They can be released only to fund the merger, to redeem public shareholders, or on liquidation.
- **Sponsor and founder shares.** The sponsor pays a nominal amount (such as 25,000) for *founder shares*, typically equal to 20 percent of the post-IPO shares (25 percent of the public shares). This is the **promote**. The sponsor often also buys *private placement warrants* to fund the IPO costs and working capital.
- **Deadline.** The SPAC usually has 18 to 24 months to complete a merger, after which it must liquidate and return the trust to public holders (the sponsor's founder shares and warrants then expire worthless).
- **Vote and redemption right.** Public shareholders can vote on the merger and, *whether they vote for or against it*, can redeem their shares for a pro rata share of the trust (typically about 10.00 plus interest). They keep their warrants.
- **PIPE and other financing.** To fill the gap left by redemptions and to demonstrate demand, SPACs often arrange a private investment in public equity (PIPE) at 10.00 per share.
- **Underwriting fees.** A part of the fee (often about 2 percent of the IPO) is paid at the IPO, and a deferred portion (often 3.5 percent) is paid at the merger.

In the merger the target's holders typically receive shares valued at 10.00, and the combined company lists in the SPAC's place. Seen from the target, the SPAC is a way to obtain a *negotiated price*, a faster timeline, and the ability to market a forward-looking story (historically, projections in merger materials faced a different liability regime than in an IPO prospectus, which regulators have since sought to narrow), and possibly more cash via the trust and the PIPE. From the SPAC investor's perspective, the structure offers a free put at the trust value plus a free option (the warrant) on the merged firm.

### 20.6.2 The dilution and the cost of capital

The headline price of 10.00 per share is misleading as a measure of value delivered, because several claims share in the combined company without contributing cash equal to their stake: the sponsor's founder shares, the warrants (public and private), the deferred underwriting fee, and transaction expenses. Moreover, redemptions reduce the trust, so the *cash delivered per share* falls while the *dilution is fixed*. The result is that the effective cost of the cash raised can be very high, especially in a high-redemption deal.

A convenient way to think about it is the **net cash per share**:

```
Net cash delivered = Trust remaining after redemptions + PIPE − deferred fee − transaction costs
Shares outstanding after merger = Public non-redeeming + PIPE shares + Founder shares + Target holders' shares
Net cash per share = Net cash delivered / Shares outstanding after merger
Value per share ≈ (Target standalone equity value + Net cash − Value of warrants) / Shares outstanding
```

The warrant value should come from an option model (Volume 1, Chapter 18), with the caveat that standard Black-Scholes ignores the redemption feature, early-exercise and redemption provisions of warrants, and the dilution effect of exercise.

**Example 20.6 – SPAC dilution under different redemption levels.** A SPAC raised 200 million with 20 million public shares at 10.00, founder shares of 5 million (20 percent of the post-IPO total of 25 million shares), 6.67 million public warrants (one-third of a warrant per unit) and 6 million private placement warrants (12.67 million warrants in all, strike 11.50, five-year life). The target's agreed pre-money equity value is 800 million (80 million shares at 10.00). A PIPE of 50 million (5 million shares at 10.00) is arranged. The deferred underwriting fee is 7 million and other transaction costs are 15 million (assume the initial IPO costs were paid from the private placement). The risk-free rate is 4 percent and volatility 40 percent for the warrant valuation.

Net cash and share count by redemption level:

| Redemptions | Public shares left | Trust cash | Net cash (trust + 50 PIPE − 22) | Total shares after merger | Net cash per share |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 20.0M | 200.0 | 228.0 | 110.0M | 2.07 |
| 50% | 10.0M | 100.0 | 128.0 | 100.0M | 1.28 |
| 90% | 2.0M | 20.0 | 48.0 | 92.0M | 0.52 |

Total shares = public left + 5 PIPE + 5 founder + 80 target. For 50 percent: 10 + 5 + 5 + 80 = 100 million. Net cash is 100 + 50 − 7 − 15 = 128.

The headline price is 10.00, but only 1.28 of cash per share arrives when half the trust is redeemed. Now estimate value per share. Let the post-merger share value be S, solved with the warrant value w(S) from a Black-Scholes call (K = 11.50, T = 5, r = 4%, σ = 40%):

```
S = (800 + Net cash − 12.67 × w(S)) / Shares
```

Iterating, with w(S) from the call formula (for example, w(10) = 3.65, w(9) = 3.0), the solution at 50% redemption is S ≈ 8.91, with w ≈ 2.92 and warrant dilution of 12.67 × 2.92 = 36.9 million. Results:

| Redemptions | Share value S | Warrant value w | Value of warrants (12.67M) | Shortfall vs 10.00 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | 9.00 | 2.98 | 37.7 | 10.0% |
| 50% | 8.91 | 2.92 | 36.9 | 10.9% |
| 90% | 8.82 | 2.86 | 36.2 | 11.8% |

The true value per share is about 11 percent below the 10.00 reference price because the target is valued at 800 (its actual worth, assumed) while shares are issued at 10.00 to the sponsor (5 million shares for nearly nothing), and to warrant holders. The *cost of capital* perspective is clearer still. With 50 percent redemptions, the target receives 128 million of net cash from 150 million of gross cash (100 trust plus 50 PIPE), and gives to the sponsor 5 million founder shares worth 5 × 8.91 = 44.6 million and to warrant holders 36.9 million: total non-cash and cash costs of 22 + 44.6 + 36.9 = 103.5 million, or 69 percent of the 150 million of gross cash raised and 81 percent of the 128 million of net cash received. (Part of the cost of the warrants is compensation for the redemption put that public holders were granted.) By comparison, the IPO in Example 20.5 cost 23 percent of proceeds. High redemptions make the SPAC an expensive source of cash, which is why targets increasingly demand minimum cash conditions, and why sponsors forfeit or restructure part of their promote in difficult deals.

The result is not an indictment of the SPAC route in all conditions: for a target that does not need the cash and primarily values a negotiated price, speed or the ability to market its projections, the *promote and warrants* are a price paid for a service. The point of the analysis is that the 10.00 headline and the 800 million valuation are not comparable to an IPO valuation until the dilution is stated.

### 20.6.3 Incentives: sponsor versus public shareholder

The founder shares are valuable even if the merged company performs poorly, because the sponsor paid almost nothing for them. This creates an incentive to complete *any* deal before the deadline, including deals that destroy value for public shareholders who do not redeem. The public shareholder can protect himself by redeeming, but then he forgoes the upside, so those who remain are exposed to the weakest incentives. This is a textbook agency problem, discussed in Volume 1, Chapter 24.

**Example 20.7 – Sponsor versus public shareholder payoffs.** Using the data of Example 20.6 (5 million founder shares, 6 million private warrants bought for 1.00 each, so the sponsor invests 6.025 million including the 25,000 for founder shares), compute payoffs at different post-merger share prices P. Warrant values w come from the Black-Scholes call (K = 11.50, T = 5, r = 4%, σ = 40%).

| Post-merger price P | Warrant value w | Sponsor value 5P + 6w | Sponsor profit (value − 6.025) | Public non-redeemer profit per unit (P + w/3 − 10) | Public return |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | 0.47 | 22.8 | +16.8 | −5.84 | −58% |
| 6 | 1.26 | 37.5 | +31.5 | −3.58 | −36% |
| 8 | 2.35 | 54.1 | +48.0 | −1.22 | −12% |
| 10 | 3.65 | 71.9 | +65.9 | +1.22 | +12% |
| 12 | 5.12 | 90.7 | +84.7 | +3.71 | +37% |
| 15 | 7.52 | 120.1 | +114.1 | +7.51 | +75% |

For example at P = 8: sponsor value = 5 × 8 + 6 × 2.35 = 40 + 14.1 = 54.1 million; profit = 54.1 − 6.025 = 48.0 million; the public holder who held a unit (one share plus one-third of a warrant) has 8 + 2.35/3 − 10 = −1.22.

The sponsor profits at every price shown, even at 4.00, a 60 percent decline from the reference price. The sponsor's founder shares alone break even at a price of 6.025 / 5 = 1.21. The public shareholder breaks even only at a price a little below 10.00 (about 9.0, where the warrant's contribution of w/3 makes up for the shortfall). The asymmetry shows why sponsors are accused of favoring completion over quality, and why changes to the structure (partial forfeiture of the promote, performance-based vesting of founder shares at higher prices, and tighter disclosure) have been proposed and adopted in various forms. It also explains why the public shareholder's redemption right, which converts him into a holder of a risk-free bond plus a free warrant, has been so heavily used.

### 20.6.4 The redemption option as a free put

Redemption gives each holder a put option on the stock at the trust value, and the warrants are kept. For a holder who buys a unit at 10.00 and redeems at about 10.00 plus interest, the return is the interest earned on the trust (at a short-term rate of about 4 percent, roughly 6 percent over 18 months) plus the value retained in the warrants, a nearly risk-free profit. Large investors specializing in this strategy were prominent in the SPAC market, and their selling of the shares or redemption depresses the cash delivered. The sponsor's *response* has been to compensate the remaining holders through PIPE inducements, forward purchase agreements or extension fees, each of which adds to dilution. This is why *the stated redemption-free case is the exception, not the rule*, and the planning assumption for an operating company evaluating a SPAC should be a high redemption rate with committed cash (PIPE or forward purchase) to cover the minimum cash it requires.

### 20.6.5 Regulation and the post-boom market

After a surge in 2020 and 2021, the SPAC market contracted sharply, with many mergers completed at prices that later fell well below 10.00 and many SPACs liquidating without a deal. Regulators in the United States responded with an accounting statement in 2021 that led many SPACs to treat their warrants as liabilities measured at fair value, and with rules adopted later that sought to align disclosure and liability for SPAC mergers more closely with those of IPOs, including provisions on projections and on the sponsor's compensation and conflicts. The market continues to evolve, and specific provisions should be checked against current rules. The managerial lesson is general: any structure that gives a party a valuable option at negligible cost produces an investor base that exercises the option, and the cost falls on whoever supplies the cash.

## 20.7 Dual-Class Share Structures

### 20.7.1 The structure and the wedge

A **dual-class** (or multi-class) structure issues shares with unequal voting rights. The usual form gives founders or insiders a class with several votes per share (often ten) while public investors hold a class with one vote, or in some cases none. Cash-flow rights (dividends, liquidation) are typically equal per share. The consequence is a **wedge** between control and ownership: insiders can hold a majority of votes while owning a minority of the economic claims.

If a fraction f of the total shares carries v votes per share and is held by the insider, and the remainder carries one vote per share, the insider's voting share is:

```
Voting share = v·f / (v·f + (1 − f))
Economic share = f
Wedge = Voting share − Economic share
```

The economic ownership needed for 50 percent of votes is f = 1 / (v + 1): about 9.09 percent when v = 10.

**Example 20.8 – Voting power and the private-benefits threshold.** After going public, the founder's company has 100 million shares: 20 million Class B (10 votes each, all held by the founder) and 80 million Class A (1 vote each, public).

```
Total votes   = 20 × 10 + 80 × 1 = 280 million
Voting share  = 200 / 280        = 71.4%
Economic share = 20 / 100        = 20.0%
Wedge         = 71.4% − 20.0%    = 51.4 percentage points
```

If the founder sells Class B shares until she holds 10 million (converting the rest to Class A on transfer, a typical feature), then f = 0.10: votes = 10 × 10 + 90 = 190; voting share = 100 / 190 = 52.6 percent, still a majority from a 10 percent economic stake. She loses majority control when f < 9.09 percent: at f = 0.0909, voting share = 0.909 / (0.909 + 0.9091) = 50.0 percent.

*Incentive effect.* Suppose the founder can take an action that gives her private benefits of 1 million but costs the firm x million of value. She bears only her economic share f of the cost. She prefers the action if f × x < 1, that is, x < 1/f.

```
f = 20%:  x < 5.0 million   (she will destroy up to 5 of firm value to gain 1)
f = 10%:  x < 10.0 million
f = 100%: x < 1.0 million   (she internalizes everything)
```

At f = 15 percent (a 15 million Class B stake) her threshold is 1 / 0.15 = 6.67. The fall in her economic stake therefore *raises* the amount of value she is willing to destroy for a unit of private benefit, while her voting power (which makes her immune to a takeover or a shareholder vote) stays high. This is the agency-cost argument against large wedges; it implicitly assumes that private benefits are material and that she will take value-reducing actions, which is far from universal.

### 20.7.2 The debate

**Arguments for.** *Long-term vision:* founders insulated from quarterly pressure and activist campaigns can invest in projects with a long payoff and a story that the market does not yet appreciate (consistent with the theory of Stein on myopic investors and with the view that founders have private information about their vision). *Founder-specific human capital:* when a founder's skill is the firm's main asset, protecting control can protect value (a point associated with Aghion and Bolton's work on control rights). *Voluntary and priced:* investors know the structure at the IPO, so any entrenchment cost should be reflected in the IPO price, and many such firms have performed well. *Takeover bargaining:* a concentrated holder can bargain for a higher takeover price (but also can refuse a premium offer).

**Arguments against.** *Agency costs and entrenchment:* the wedge reduces accountability, as in Example 20.8. *Value effects:* research by Gompers, Ishii and Metrick on US dual-class firms found that firm value tended to fall as the wedge between votes and cash-flow rights widened, though they, like others, acknowledged issues of causality. *Governance fragility over time:* the founder who designed the structure may not be the one who runs the company decades later, and the structure may pass control to heirs or successors without the original justification (Bebchuk and Kastiel argued against *perpetual* dual-class structures on this ground). *Exclusion from certain indices or restrictions by some investors* have, at various times, made dual-class shares less attractive to some funds; index-provider policies on multi-class shares have changed over time.

**What the evidence says.** The evidence is mixed. Some studies find positive effects in the early years after the IPO, particularly for innovative firms, and negative effects as time passes and the founder's vision becomes less distinctive, a pattern that supports the design of *sunset provisions*. No honest summary can claim that dual-class structures are uniformly good or bad; the relevant questions are about *design*.

### 20.7.3 Design features that matter

- **Sunset clauses.** A *time-based* sunset converts high-vote shares to ordinary shares after a set number of years; an *event-based* sunset converts them when the founder leaves, dies, becomes incapacitated, or falls below a minimum economic stake; a *transfer-based* provision converts shares on sale or gift (other than to permitted family vehicles).
- **Minimum economic ownership.** High-vote shares convert automatically if the holder's economic stake falls below a threshold (say 10 percent), limiting the wedge.
- **Coattail provisions.** In a takeover, all classes must receive equal consideration, preventing a control premium for the high-vote class only.
- **Independent directors and approval rights.** Class A shareholders elect a minimum number of directors, or related-party transactions need a majority-of-the-minority vote.
- **Limits on voting rights.** Some exchanges and regulators permit weighted voting rights only subject to conditions on the ratio, on eligible holders and on sunset or independent governance rules. Several Asian exchanges began permitting weighted voting rights in the late 2010s, subject to such conditions, and rules in other markets have evolved as well, so conditions should be checked locally.
- **Non-voting shares.** A class with no vote at all raises the wedge even higher and is controversial among institutional investors.

### 20.7.4 Valuing the vote

If voting rights carried no value, both classes would trade at the same price. In practice the high-vote class often trades at a modest premium, which is a market-based estimate of the value of control. Care is needed: the premium also reflects differences in liquidity and conversion rights. Where the vote premium is large, it indicates that controlling shareholders can extract private benefits; where it is small, it suggests the market is not worried (Zingales; Nenova). For an acquirer, the structure means the deal can be approved only with the controller's consent, so the control holder's willingness to sell, and the price relative to public holders, are central to the negotiation.

## 20.8 Choosing the Route: A Managerial Framework

Table 20.1 summarizes how the alternatives compare on the dimensions most often decisive.

| Route | Cash raised | Cost profile | Control retained | Key risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset sale | Yes (after tax) | Tax on gain; transaction costs | None over the business | Tax leakage; buyer certainty |
| Spin-off | No (except via pre-spin debt) | One-time separation costs; dis-synergies | Shareholders own both | Flowback pressure; stranded costs |
| Equity carve-out | Yes (minority) | IPO discount and spread | Parent keeps control | Governance conflicts |
| IPO | Yes | Spread plus underpricing (about 20% or more of proceeds in Example 20.5) | Founder control with dual-class | Market window; price risk |
| Direct listing | Limited or none | Low fees; pricing risk | High | No committed capital |
| SPAC | Depends on redemptions | Promote and warrants (about 70% of gross cash in Example 20.6 at 50% redemptions) | Negotiated | High redemptions; sponsor incentives |

A practical sequence for a CFO is: (1) establish the *objective* (raise cash, create a pure play, achieve control, gain speed); (2) estimate the *value* of each route net of tax and costs, as in Examples 20.1 to 20.7; (3) test *robustness* to market conditions, since many routes depend on windows of investor appetite; (4) design *governance* to deal with the conflicts each route creates (controlling shareholders, sponsors, spun-off companies' takeover defences); and (5) plan *communication and execution*, including investor days, transition services and separation of IT and finance functions. Boards should resist the temptation to select a route merely because it is fashionable in a given year.

## Case Study: Halvorsen Mobility

## Key Takeaways

- Separations create value only if the business is worth more to someone else, or on its own, than inside the parent after dis-synergies, stranded costs and one-time costs; a headline conglomerate discount is a gross, not a net, figure.
- In Calder's sum-of-the-parts, a 600 million gross discount shrinks to a 306 million net gain, and the equity gain (12.75 percent) is larger than the enterprise gain (7.8 percent) because of leverage.
- Tax basis drives the sale-versus-spin choice: a taxable sale must clear a price hurdle equal to the tax cost, whereas a qualifying spin can avoid corporate tax but restricts later transactions.
- The logic of tax-free spin-offs is that they are genuine reorganizations rather than disguised sales or dividends; the conditions (control, active business, business purpose, continuity, limits on subsequent acquisitions) differ by jurisdiction and must be verified with tax counsel.
- Spin-offs require deliberate allocation of debt, basis, pensions and contracts, and often suffer temporary flowback selling.
- Equity carve-outs raise cash and create a market price while retaining control, but impose IPO costs and a controlled-company governance conflict.
- IPOs impose spread plus underpricing, which together can cost a fifth or more of proceeds; direct listings reduce explicit costs but offer no committed capital and carry pricing risk.
- SPAC economics are driven by the promote, warrants and redemptions; at high redemptions the effective cost of cash can far exceed that of an IPO, and sponsors profit even when public holders lose.
- Dual-class structures create a wedge between votes and cash-flow rights; the evidence is mixed, and design features (sunsets, coattails, minimum stakes, independent directors) determine whether they protect vision or entrench control.
- The choice among routes should follow from the objective, a value comparison net of tax and costs, robustness to market conditions and a governance design that addresses the conflicts each route creates.
