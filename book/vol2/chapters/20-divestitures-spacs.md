# Chapter 20: Divestitures, Spin-offs, SPACs and Dual-Class Structures

## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

- Compare the main forms of corporate separation (asset sale, spin-off, split-off, equity carve-out, tracking stock) and choose among them using value, tax, control and financing criteria.
- Quantify the value created or destroyed by a separation using a sum-of-the-parts analysis net of dis-synergies and one-time costs, and allocate debt and tax basis in a spin-off.
- Explain, in general terms, the logic of tax-free spin-offs, the conditions typically attached to them, and the restrictions on subsequent transactions.
- Analyze SPAC economics: the sponsor promote, warrants, redemption rights, and dilution, and compute net cash and value per share under different redemption scenarios.
- Evaluate dual-class share structures, measuring the wedge between voting and cash-flow rights and summarizing the governance arguments for and against them, including sunset provisions.
- Compare IPOs, direct listings, SPAC mergers and sales as routes to public markets, including the full cost of each.

## Opening Vignette

*(Illustrative scenario. Calder Industries and its subsidiaries are fictional.)*

Calder Industries is a diversified manufacturer with two divisions that share a corporate headquarters, a name and a balance sheet. Its Industrial division makes pumps and valves, a mature, cyclical business with EBITDA of 300 million. Its Consumer division makes premium kitchen appliances and has EBITDA of 150 million, growing faster, with higher margins and heavy marketing needs. Calder's consolidated enterprise value is 3.9 billion, about 8.7 times EBITDA, and net debt is 1.5 billion.

For three years the CEO, Priya Raman, has heard the same message from investors: the Consumer division deserves a multiple like that of branded-goods peers, and the Industrial division a multiple like that of industrial peers, yet the market blends them into a discount. An activist fund has bought 4 percent of the shares and published a letter proposing a separation. Raman's board has three alternatives: sell Consumer to a strategic buyer who has indicated an interest at a price that would trigger a large taxable gain; spin Consumer off to shareholders as a separate listed company; or sell a minority stake in Consumer in an IPO and keep control.

Meanwhile a founder-led company in a different industry, one Calder's venture arm has invested in, is deciding how to go public. Its founder wants to retain control and has been courted by a SPAC sponsor promising speed and certainty, while its bankers argue for a traditional IPO, and a third adviser suggests a direct listing. The founder wants a share structure in which her shares carry ten votes each.

Both situations raise the questions of this chapter. What does a separation do to value, and how much of the apparent gain is real? What are the tax logic and the economics of each route? What does a SPAC actually cost the company relative to an IPO? When does a dual-class structure create value and when does it entrench? We will use Calder throughout the first half of the chapter and the founder's company in the second half.

## 20.1 The Map of Corporate Separations

### 20.1.1 Why firms separate

Volume 1, Chapter 22 treated acquisitions as a way to create value through synergy. Separations are the mirror image. They are justified when a business is worth more to someone else, or on its own, than inside the current parent. The standard motivations are:

- **Focus.** Management attention, capital allocation and incentive pay can be tailored to a single business. Diversification discount research, associated with Lang and Stulz and with Berger and Ofek, found that diversified firms tended on average to trade at a discount to the sum of their stand-alone parts, though later work has debated how much of this is a measurement artifact or reflects the selection of firms that diversify when their opportunities are poor.
- **Investor clienteles and transparency.** A pure-play firm is easier to value, to cover by analysts and to compare with peers, and can attract investors who would not hold the conglomerate.
- **Capital allocation.** Internal capital markets may cross-subsidize weak divisions (the "socialism" critique of Scharfstein and Stein). Separation forces each business to compete for capital at its own cost.
- **Incentives and currency.** A separately listed business can grant equity compensation tied to its own performance and can use its own shares for acquisitions.
- **Strategic fit and regulation.** The business may be a drag on the parent's credit rating, face antitrust constraints, or need a different capital structure (a high-growth unit needs equity, a cash-generating unit supports debt).
- **Monetization.** The parent may simply need cash, to repay debt or fund a better opportunity.

Against these must be set the costs: lost synergies (shared procurement, shared overhead, cross-selling), **dis-synergies** from duplicated corporate functions, one-time separation costs (IT systems, legal entities, stranded costs), loss of diversification of cash flows (which may raise the cost of debt of each part), and, in a taxable transaction, tax.

### 20.1.2 The menu

| Form | What happens | Cash to parent? | Control after | Typical tax outcome (general) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Asset or subsidiary sale (divestiture) | Sold to a strategic or financial buyer for cash or stock | Yes | Buyer | Taxable to the seller on gain over tax basis |
| Spin-off | Subsidiary shares distributed pro rata to parent shareholders | No (except via debt raised by subsidiary) | Separate public company, same shareholders | Can be tax-free if conditions are met |
| Split-off | Parent shareholders offered subsidiary shares in exchange for parent shares | No | Subset of holders | Can be tax-free if conditions are met |
| Equity carve-out | Minority stake in subsidiary sold to public investors in an IPO | Yes (subsidiary or parent receives proceeds) | Parent keeps control | Often taxable only to the extent of gain on the shares sold |
| Tracking stock | Parent issues a class of shares whose dividends track a division | No | Parent retains legal ownership | Typically no tax event on issuance |
| Sale via reverse merger (e.g., Reverse Morris Trust) | Spin-off followed by merger of the spun entity with a third party | Possibly (via debt) | Third party plus old holders | Can be tax-free if ownership tests are met |

The tax column is a generalization. Tax-free treatment depends on detailed statutory conditions that vary by country and change over time; readers must consult tax counsel before relying on any of it.

### 20.1.3 Evidence in brief

Empirical studies of spin-offs, associated with Schipper and Smith, Hite and Owers, Cusatis, Miles and Woolridge, and others, have generally reported positive announcement-period returns on average, and some have found positive subsequent performance of both parent and spun-off firm, particularly where the separation increases focus. Studies of asset sales have also tended to find positive average announcement returns for sellers, in line with the view that sales redeploy assets to higher-valued uses. Later research has documented that some of the post-spin gains are associated with the spun-off company or the parent being acquired. These are statements about averages across samples; individual transactions vary widely, and no manager should assume that a separation creates value merely because it is a separation. The remainder of this chapter shows how to test whether a specific one does.

## 20.2 Divestitures: Selling a Business

### 20.2.1 The hold-sell decision

A divestiture is a capital budgeting decision in reverse. The parent should sell if the after-tax proceeds exceed the value of retaining the business, where value of retention is the present value of the after-tax cash flows the business will generate *to this owner*, including synergies. Since the buyer's value includes their own synergies, a sale happens when the buyer can generate more than the seller (Volume 1, Chapter 22). The parent's analysis should therefore compare:

```
Sell if:  Net after-tax proceeds  >  PV of retained cash flows (to this owner)
Net after-tax proceeds = Price − Tax on gain − Transaction costs − Stranded costs (PV)
Tax on gain = t × (Price − Tax basis)
```

Two points deserve emphasis. First, **tax basis** matters. A business bought long ago, or built organically, usually has a low tax basis, so a sale triggers tax on nearly the whole price. The same business separated in a qualifying tax-free spin-off, by contrast, may trigger no corporate-level tax. That is one reason spin-offs are popular. Second, **stranded costs**, the shared overhead formerly absorbed by the divested business, remain with the parent unless eliminated. Ignoring them overstates the benefit.

### 20.2.2 Consideration and structure

A buyer may pay in cash, stock, or both. Cash gives the seller certainty but crystallizes the tax; stock can sometimes defer tax but exposes the seller to the buyer's performance. Common contract elements include earn-outs (deferred contingent consideration tied to performance, discussed in Volume 1, Chapter 22 as a real option), transition services agreements (the seller supplies IT, payroll or logistics for a limited period), indemnities and escrows, and non-compete clauses. In a **carve-out sale** to a private equity buyer (Volume 1, Chapter 23), financing is usually largely debt, so the parent's price depends on credit market conditions.

### 20.2.3 Worked comparison: sell or spin

**Example 20.1 – Sale versus tax-free spin-off of Calder's Consumer division.** A strategic buyer offers 1,800 million in cash for the Consumer division. Consumer's tax basis is 600 million, the corporate tax rate on the gain is 25 percent, and transaction costs are ignored. The alternative is a spin-off that would be tax-free to Calder and to its shareholders if conditions are met, in which case Consumer would be worth 1,704 million as a stand-alone company (derived in Example 20.2).

Sale:

```
Gain             = 1,800 − 600 = 1,200
Tax on gain      = 25% × 1,200 = 300
After-tax cash   = 1,800 − 300 = 1,500
```

Spin-off: shareholders receive Consumer's stand-alone value, 1,704, with no corporate tax and no current shareholder tax in the stylized case, but Calder receives no cash.

At first sight the spin-off (1,704) beats the sale (1,500) by 204. The two are comparable on an enterprise-value basis; in practice the parent would also have Consumer borrow and pay a dividend to Calder before the spin, so that Calder can reduce its own debt, which shifts value between the two equity stubs without changing the total.

The taxable-sale alternative would be preferable only if the buyer's price exceeds the spin value by at least the tax: to match 1,704 after tax, the sale price P must solve P − 0.25(P − 600) = 1,704, so 0.75P + 150 = 1,704, P = 2,072. The buyer would have to pay about 2,072 million, 15 percent above its 1,800 offer, to leave Calder's shareholders as well off as the spin. Equivalently, the tax cost of 300 is a hurdle the sale price must clear. If the buyer's synergies are large enough to justify 2,072, the sale is better; if not, the spin is. Note that this ignores the possibility that a spin's value would be lower or higher than 1,704 in the market, which is uncertain, whereas a cash price is certain: certainty has value, as does a competitive auction.

## 20.3 Spin-offs

### 20.3.1 Mechanics

In a spin-off, the parent distributes shares of a subsidiary pro rata to its own shareholders. Each parent shareholder ends up with shares of both companies in proportion to the original holding, and no cash changes hands. The steps are: the parent establishes the subsidiary as a separate legal entity (the "separation"), contributes assets, negotiates a separation agreement (assets, employees, contracts, intellectual property, and the allocation of liabilities), allocates debt, arranges a tax ruling or opinion where appropriate, files a registration or information statement with the securities regulator, obtains stock exchange listing, declares a distribution with a record date, and settles the distribution. A "when-issued" market typically emerges before the distribution date, allowing price discovery.

Several decisions deserve care:

- **Capital structure of each entity.** Parents frequently have the spun entity borrow and pay a *cash dividend to the parent* just before the spin. This lets the parent reduce its own leverage while delivering a stand-alone capital structure to the new firm. Each entity's leverage should be set for its own business risk, which is where the Volume 1, Chapter 15 trade-off theory applies.
- **Governance.** Each firm needs its own board, management, and incentive plans. Antitakeover provisions of the spun-off firm are a common subject of criticism.
- **Commercial arrangements.** Transition services, supply agreements and shared intellectual property agreements govern the relationship after the spin. A poorly designed agreement can destroy value through conflict.
- **Employee and pension allocation.** Pension liabilities need to be allocated with an actuarial view.
- **Index and shareholder base effects.** After a spin, the new company may be too small, or in the wrong sector, for the funds that hold the parent, and these funds may sell. This *flowback* selling often creates temporary downward price pressure on the new shares, a source of the short-term underperformance and of the opportunities that spin-off specialists look for.

### 20.3.2 Tax-free logic, described generally

In many tax systems, a distribution of shares of a subsidiary can be tax-free to both the distributing corporation and its shareholders if it meets conditions designed to ensure that the transaction is a genuine reorganization and not a disguised cash distribution or a way to sell a business without tax. In the United States, the relevant provisions are in Section 355 of the Internal Revenue Code. Broadly, and without attempting to state every requirement, the typical conditions include:

- the parent controls the subsidiary and distributes all or substantially all of its stock;
- both businesses are engaged in an active trade or business that has been conducted for a minimum period;
- the transaction has a genuine corporate business purpose other than tax avoidance, and is not a "device" for distributing earnings;
- the historic shareholders maintain a continuing interest in both firms; and
- there are limits on acquisitions of either company, or of sizeable stakes, shortly after the distribution, since a spin followed by a taxable sale would otherwise let the parent sell the subsidiary without paying tax (the so-called Morris Trust issue, and the reason for the Reverse Morris Trust ownership thresholds).

Other countries have different rules, and some tax distributions of shares as dividends in kind unless relief applies. The managerial points are these. First, qualification is valuable (in Example 20.1, it avoids 300 million of tax), so the company will plan the transaction to satisfy the conditions and will often obtain an external tax opinion or a ruling. Second, qualification restricts what the parent and the spun-off company can do for some period afterward, notably a sale to a third party, and these restrictions are normally enforced through a tax matters agreement that allocates liability if a later action causes the spin to be taxable. Third, for the *shareholder* the typical consequence of a qualifying spin is deferral: no tax at distribution, with the original tax basis allocated between the two holdings, usually in proportion to their relative market values immediately after the spin, and the holding period carried over.

### 20.3.3 Valuing the separation: sum of the parts

The central analysis is a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuation, in which each division is valued separately using appropriate multiples or DCF (Volume 1, Chapter 21), the values are added, and the total is compared with the current consolidated value. The *gross* gain is the difference. The *net* gain subtracts dis-synergies and one-time costs. The analysis is only as good as its inputs: the multiples applied to each division must be those of truly comparable stand-alone peers, and the dis-synergies must be realistic.

**Example 20.2 – Calder's sum of the parts.** Calder's consolidated enterprise value is 3,900 million, net debt 1,500 million and shares 100 million. Industrial EBITDA is 300 and Consumer EBITDA is 150, so the blended multiple is 3,900 / 450 = 8.67 times. Pure-play peers trade at 9 times EBITDA for industrial businesses and 12 times for branded consumer businesses. Separation would create stand-alone dis-synergies of 20 million of annual EBITDA (12 in Industrial, 8 in Consumer) and one-time costs of 90 million.

Step 1 – Gross SOTP:

```
Industrial: 300 × 9  = 2,700
Consumer:   150 × 12 = 1,800
SOTP                 = 4,500
Conglomerate discount = 4,500 − 3,900 = 600  (13.3% of 4,500)
```

Step 2 – Capitalize the dis-synergies at each division's multiple:

```
Industrial: 12 × 9  = 108
Consumer:    8 × 12 =  96
Total                = 204
```

Step 3 – Net enterprise value after separation:

```
Post-separation EV = 4,500 − 204 = 4,296
Less one-time costs = 4,296 − 90 = 4,206
Net gain vs. current 3,900 = 306  (7.8% of 3,900)
```

Step 4 – Equity value per share. Net debt of 1,500 is allocated as 1,000 to Industrial and 500 to Consumer (3.5 times each division's post-dis-synergy EBITDA: 1,000 / 288 = 3.47 and 500 / 142 = 3.52). Equity values are:

```
Industrial equity: (2,700 − 108) − 1,000 = 1,592  → 15.92 per share
Consumer equity:   (1,800 −  96) −   500 = 1,204  → 12.04 per share
Combined                                    = 2,796 → 27.96 per share
Less one-time costs (90 / 100 shares)       = 0.90
Net value per original share                = 27.06
```

Current equity value is 3,900 − 1,500 = 2,400, or 24.00 per share. The spin therefore adds 3.06 per share, a 12.75 percent equity gain, from an enterprise value gain of 7.8 percent. This illustrates how leverage amplifies the equity effect: the same 306 million of enterprise value gain is spread over an equity base of 2,400.

Several cautions apply. The 13.3 percent conglomerate discount is the *gross* figure; 204 + 90 = 294 of it, or nearly half, is consumed by dis-synergies and costs, leaving a net gain of 306. A 1-turn lower multiple for Consumer (11 instead of 12) would reduce the Consumer value by 150, partly offset by 8 lower capitalized dis-synergies, cutting the net gain to about 164, so the conclusion is sensitive to the peer multiple. And, importantly, the *market* may already reflect the expected separation (activist pressure raises the share price), in which case the announcement return will be smaller. Finally, the figures above are values the market *could* assign; there is no guarantee. Separation changes the information environment and the investor base, but it does not by itself create cash flows.

### 20.3.4 Basis and per-share allocation

**Example 20.3 – Allocating tax basis and checking leverage in the spin.** Calder distributes one Consumer share for each Calder share held (a 1-for-1 distribution). Immediately after the spin the shares trade at 15.92 (Industrial, the continuing "RemainCo") and 12.04 (Consumer), per Example 20.2's values before the one-time cost, which we ignore for allocation. A shareholder who owned one Calder share with a tax basis of 18.00 allocates basis in proportion to market values:

```
Total post-spin value per original share = 15.92 + 12.04 = 27.96
Industrial share of value = 15.92 / 27.96 = 56.94%
Consumer share of value   = 12.04 / 27.96 = 43.06%
Basis allocated to Industrial = 18.00 × 0.5694 = 10.25
Basis allocated to Consumer   = 18.00 × 0.4306 =  7.75
```

If the shareholder later sells the Consumer share at 15.00, the gain is 15.00 − 7.75 = 7.25 (rather than the 15.00 − 18.00 = −3.00 loss that would result if she mistakenly assigned the whole basis to Consumer). The allocation matters for later tax. Leverage check: Industrial's net debt / post-dis-synergy EBITDA is 1,000 / 288 = 3.47 times and Consumer's 500 / 142 = 3.52 times, similar at the design stage, though Consumer's higher growth and need for marketing investment would argue for lower leverage, and Industrial's cyclicality argues for lower leverage too. A careful design would run each entity through a coverage stress test (Volume 1, Chapter 14) and might shift debt to the more stable cash generator. Credit ratings of both entities, and the cost of refinancing the parent's existing bonds (which may have change-of-control or asset-sale covenants), must be checked as well.

### 20.3.5 What goes wrong

The common failures are: separating businesses that are interdependent (shared plants, customers or technology) so that dis-synergies exceed the multiple arbitrage; loading the spun-off company with too much debt, leaving it unable to invest (a form of "dumping"); weak governance of the spun company; underestimating stranded costs at the parent, leaving RemainCo with an overhead too large for its size; and *pricing pressure from flowback selling* in the first months, mistaken by management for a verdict on the strategy. Creditors may also object: a spin that transfers value away from the borrowing entity can raise fraudulent transfer concerns if the parent is or becomes insolvent (Chapter 19), and bondholders sometimes seek protection through covenants on asset sales and distributions.
