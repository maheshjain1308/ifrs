## Learning Objectives

After studying this chapter you should be able to:

- Explain why sponsors use a special-purpose vehicle (SPV) and non-recourse debt, and identify the project characteristics that make project finance preferable to corporate finance.
- Map the contractual web around a project (EPC, O&M, offtake, supply, financing and security documents) and explain how each contract moves a specific risk away from lenders.
- Apply the principle of risk allocation to construction, resource, price, operating, financial and political risks, and evaluate a risk matrix critically.

## Opening Vignette

*Illustrative scenario; the company and all figures are fictional.*

Marlow Power Partners is a mid-sized developer that has spent three years assembling a 100 MW solar farm in a sunny, grid-connected region. It holds the land lease, the grid connection agreement and a 20-year power purchase agreement (PPA) with a regional utility at a fixed price per megawatt-hour. Construction will cost about 120 million, including financing fees and interest during construction. Marlow's own balance sheet is modest: total equity of about 60 million, and its existing corporate lenders will not lend another 90 million against a company this size.

Marlow's chief financial officer, Imani Okafor, proposes a different route. Rather than borrowing at the corporate level, Marlow will create a new company, Marlow Solar One Ltd, that owns only this farm. Banks will lend to that company against the project's contracted cash flows, with no claim on Marlow's other assets. Marlow and a pension-fund co-investor will provide the equity.

The board is sceptical. "You are telling me lenders will put in three-quarters of the money and we can walk away if it fails?" asks the chair. Imani's reply frames the chapter: the lenders are not betting on Marlow, they are betting on a contract web. The engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contractor guarantees the price and completion date; the PPA fixes the revenue; an operator guarantees availability; and the debt is sized so that, even in a bad solar year, cash flow covers debt service with a margin. The lenders will charge for the risks they keep, and they will police the structure with covenants.

Over the next hour the board asks the questions this chapter answers. Why a separate company? What exactly does "non-recourse" protect? How is the debt sized, and what does a debt service coverage ratio of 1.30 mean? What happens if the sun is weaker than forecast, or if construction runs six months late? And, ultimately: does a project that clears all of the lenders' tests still earn the sponsors an acceptable return?

## 15.1 Why Project Finance Exists

### 15.1.1 Corporate finance versus project finance

In the corporate finance of Volume 1 (Chapters 6, 11 and 15), a firm raises capital against the whole of its balance sheet. Lenders look at the firm's diversified earnings, assets and management, and they have recourse to everything the firm owns. The investment decision (the project's NPV) is made inside the firm, and financing is largely a secondary decision governed by capital-structure trade-offs.

**Project finance** inverts this. The project is financed as a stand-alone economic unit: a legally separate company is created to build and operate one asset (or a closely related group of assets), and lenders look to that company's cash flows, and to the assets it owns, as the primary and sometimes sole source of repayment. The financing is therefore inseparable from the project's contracts, risk allocation and cash-flow forecast. In effect, the lenders underwrite a contract structure rather than a corporate credit.

The distinction is one of degree, not kind. Few project loans are *completely* non-recourse; during construction, sponsors often provide completion guarantees, equity commitments or standby facilities. The term "limited recourse" is more accurate for most real transactions. What matters is that recourse to the sponsor is bounded, defined by contract, and usually falls away once the asset is built and proven.

### 15.1.2 Which projects suit project finance

Project finance is not suited to every investment. It works best when several conditions hold:

1. **Large, discrete, long-lived assets** with high up-front capital cost and low operating cost: power plants, wind and solar farms, transmission lines, toll roads, pipelines, ports, LNG terminals, hospitals, water treatment plants, data-center campuses.
2. **Predictable, contractable revenue.** The more of the revenue stream is fixed by a long-term contract with a creditworthy counterparty (take-or-pay offtake, availability payment, regulated tariff), the more debt the project can support.
3. **Transparent, ring-fenceable cash flows.** The asset must be separable from the sponsor's other businesses. Shared assets, cross-subsidies and intra-group pricing make ring-fencing difficult.
4. **Controllable risks.** Construction, operating and technology risks need to be understood and allocatable by contract. A first-of-a-kind technology with no performance guarantee is generally unfinanceable on a non-recourse basis.
5. **A finite life.** Lenders prefer an asset whose cash flows will pay off the debt well before the end of the contract or asset life, leaving a tail.

### 15.1.3 Why sponsors choose it

Sponsors accept the cost and complexity for four reasons. **Higher leverage:** because debt is sized to project cash flows rather than the sponsor's credit, a project may carry 70 to 85 percent debt even if the sponsor's own gearing target is far lower. **Limited balance-sheet impact:** depending on accounting standards, ownership and control, the debt may be off balance sheet or at least labelled non-recourse, though rating agencies often look through to guarantees. **Risk isolation:** a project failure is confined to the equity invested and contractual guarantees. **Partnership and discipline:** a joint-venture SPV lets sponsors, offtakers and infrastructure funds each hold a stake that suits their risk appetite, and lenders' advisers scrutinise the project more thoroughly than many boards would.

### 15.1.4 The costs of project finance

Documentation is extensive, with transaction costs commonly a few percent of project cost; lenders charge margins above corporate debt and impose covenants and reserve accounts that delay equity distributions; and negotiation takes many months. As the Modigliani-Miller logic of Volume 1, Chapter 15 reminds us, leverage does not create value by itself; value comes from tax shields, risk-sharing, reduced agency costs, or access to capital that would otherwise be unavailable.

> **Common mistake.** Treating project finance as "free leverage." The risk the sponsor does not bear through recourse is borne by lenders, who price it through higher margins, tighter covenants and lower leverage. Cheaper-looking debt is paid for elsewhere in the structure.

## 15.2 The SPV and the Contractual Web

### 15.2.1 The special-purpose vehicle

The **special-purpose vehicle** (SPV, sometimes "project company" or "ProjectCo") is a newly formed company whose only business is the project. Its constitution typically prohibits other activities, other debt and other subsidiaries. The SPV signs all the project contracts, holds the permits and the assets, borrows from the lenders, and pays dividends to its shareholders (the sponsors) only after the lenders' cash waterfall has been satisfied.

The SPV's purpose is to make a defined set of cash flows legally and economically separate. This protects lenders against a sponsor's insolvency (the project's assets are not part of the sponsor's estate) and protects the sponsor against the project's insolvency (the project's creditors cannot claim against the sponsor except under agreed guarantees). It also ensures that project cash flow cannot be diverted to the sponsor's other needs.

### 15.2.2 The parties

The sponsors (developers, utilities, infrastructure and pension funds) contribute equity. Senior lenders (commercial banks, export-credit agencies, development banks, institutional bond investors) hold first-ranking security, with mezzanine lenders behind them if any. Around the SPV sit the EPC contractor, the operator, the offtaker, input suppliers, insurers, public authorities and a team of advisers (independent engineer, market and insurance consultants, model auditor, lawyers).

### 15.2.3 The contract map

The project's value is carried by its contracts. Table 15.1 shows the core contracts and the principal risk each is designed to address.

**Table 15.1 – Core project contracts and the risks they address**

| Contract | Counterparty | Risk addressed | Typical lender protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| EPC contract | Contractor (often a consortium) | Cost overrun, delay, defects, performance shortfall | Fixed price, fixed date, liquidated damages (LDs), performance bond, parent guarantee |
| Offtake / PPA | Utility, government agency, corporate buyer | Price and volume of revenue | Long-term fixed or indexed price, take-or-pay or availability payment, creditworthy counterparty |
| O&M agreement | Operator | Availability, maintenance cost | Fixed fee, availability guarantee, bonus-malus |
| Supply agreement | Fuel, feedstock or equipment supplier | Input price and availability | Pass-through or fixed price, supply-or-pay |
| Concession / licence | Public authority | Right to operate, regulation, change in law | Compensation for termination, change-in-law protection |
| Insurance | Insurers | Physical damage, delay in start-up, business interruption | Lenders named as loss payees, minimum cover |
| Loan agreement and security | Lenders | Repayment | Security over assets, accounts, contracts, shares; covenants; step-in rights |
| Direct agreements | Each key counterparty and the lenders | Termination, counterparty default | Lender right to cure and step in before contract termination |

Two features of this map deserve emphasis. First, the **direct agreements** (also called consents or tripartite agreements) are crucial but easy to overlook: they give the lenders the right to be notified of defaults and to cure them, or to step in and replace the SPV, before the offtaker or contractor terminates. Without them, a contract that looks strong on paper can be terminated at the moment the lenders most need it. Second, every contract has a *counterparty credit risk* of its own: an LD of 10 percent of the EPC price is only as good as the contractor's balance sheet or parent guarantee.

### 15.2.4 The security package and cash waterfall

Lenders take security over everything the SPV owns: the assets, the bank accounts, the receivables, the rights under the project contracts and the sponsors' shares in the SPV. Practically, the lenders' strongest tool is **control over the cash**. All project revenues are paid into controlled accounts, and a contractual waterfall determines the order in which cash is released:

1. Operating costs, taxes and statutory payments.
2. Senior debt fees and interest.
3. Scheduled senior principal.
4. Top-up of the **debt service reserve account** (DSRA), commonly sized at six months of the next debt service payments.
5. Top-up of the **major maintenance reserve account**, where applicable.
6. Mezzanine or subordinated debt service.
7. Any mandatory prepayments (cash sweep).
8. Distributions to equity, only if the lock-up and default tests are passed (Section 15.4.4).

The waterfall explains why project equity is the residual claim in the strictest sense: its return arrives last, and only if the project is healthy. It also explains why equity IRR in project finance is sensitive to relatively small changes in revenue, as leveraged cash flows magnify operating variation (Example 15.5).

## 15.3 Risk Allocation

### 15.3.1 The governing principle

The central rule of project finance risk allocation is simple to state and difficult to apply: **each risk should be borne by the party best able to control it, or, if it cannot be controlled, best able to price and diversify it, at least cost.** An EPC contractor controls construction methods and procurement, so it should bear cost-overrun and delay risk (and be paid a premium for doing so). A government controls regulation and tariffs, so it should bear change-in-law risk. Nobody controls weather, so resource risk is shared between insurance, sponsor equity and lenders' debt cushion, and priced through the DSCR.

When the parties allocate risk to the wrong party, the project becomes more expensive, not cheaper. A contractor asked to accept an unpriceable risk will either add a large contingency or walk away. A government that retains a risk it cannot manage will bear the consequences later, often at greater cost.

### 15.3.2 The risk matrix

Table 15.2 shows a stylised risk matrix for a contracted renewable project. Actual matrices are far more detailed, but the pattern is representative.

**Table 15.2 – Stylised risk allocation matrix for a contracted solar project**

| Risk | Description | Primary holder | Mitigants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Construction cost | Capex exceeds budget | EPC contractor | Fixed-price, date-certain turnkey contract; contingency; parent guarantee |
| Completion / delay | COD (commercial operations date) later than planned | EPC contractor, then sponsor | Delay LDs sized to cover interest and fixed costs; delay-in-start-up insurance; sponsor completion support |
| Technology / performance | Panels or inverters underperform | EPC contractor, equipment supplier | Performance tests, warranties, performance LDs |
| Resource (irradiation) | Solar yield below forecast | Equity and lenders | Independent yield assessment (P50/P90), debt sized on P90 or with DSCR margin, DSRA |
| Offtake / price | Counterparty fails to pay; price is lower | Offtaker (price), lenders (credit) | Long-term PPA, creditworthy offtaker, termination payments |
| Operating cost | O&M costs rise | O&M contractor, then SPV | Fixed-price O&M with indexation caps |
| Interest rate | Floating rates rise | SPV | Fixed-rate debt or swaps covering 75-100 percent |
| Foreign exchange | Revenue and debt in different currencies | SPV or offtaker | Currency-matched debt, indexed tariffs |
| Political / regulatory | Expropriation, retroactive tariff change, change in law | Government, political-risk insurers | Concession compensation, MIGA or ECA cover, international arbitration |
| Force majeure | Events beyond all parties' control | Shared | Insurance, termination compensation formulas |
| Merchant / residual | Revenue after PPA expires | Equity | Debt fully repaid before PPA end; no reliance on merchant revenue |

### 15.3.3 Construction and completion risk

Construction is usually the riskiest phase: cash is committed, nothing is yet earning, and the collateral is a partly built asset worth far less than its cost. Lenders therefore insist on **fixed-price, date-certain, turnkey** contracts, **performance bonds** or parent guarantees, and **delay and performance LDs**. A well-structured delay LD covers at least the extra interest during construction and the SPV's fixed costs, so lenders' ratios are not impaired; but LDs are usually capped (often 10 to 20 percent of contract price). Lenders also require budget contingency and sponsor equity undertakings, sometimes backed by letters of credit. Example 15.1 quantifies the gap between delay LDs and the true cost of delay.

### 15.3.4 Operating, market and political risk

During operations, technical risk shrinks and volume, price and counterparty risks dominate. For contracted assets the offtaker's credit quality is critical: a PPA with a weak utility is worth much less than one with a strong buyer, and lenders reflect this in margin, DSCR and tenor. Where revenue is partly merchant, lenders demand higher DSCRs, shorter tenors, cash sweeps and sometimes hedges. Political risk includes expropriation and inconvertibility but, most commonly in practice, retroactive changes to tariffs, subsidies or taxes; mitigants include political-risk insurance, multilateral participation, treaty protection and stabilisation clauses, though none is perfect.

### 15.3.5 Quantifying risk: a delay example

**Example 15.1 – Delay liquidated damages versus the actual cost of delay.** The Marlow solar project (Section 15.6) has 90 million of senior debt at 5.5 percent, fully drawn at the completion date. The EPC contract provides delay LDs of 12,000 per day (that is, 0.012 million). Construction finishes 180 days late. The SPV continues to incur 0.30 million of fixed costs over the delay period (insurance, site and administration costs not covered by the EPC price).

*Step 1: Extra interest during construction.* Interest over 180 days (taken as half a year) on 90 million at 5.5 percent: 90 × 0.055 × 0.5 = 2.475 million.

*Step 2: Additional fixed costs.* 0.30 million.

*Step 3: Total delay cost to the SPV.* 2.475 + 0.30 = 2.775 million.

*Step 4: LD recovery.* 0.012 million per day × 180 days = 2.160 million.

*Step 5: Shortfall.* 2.775 − 2.160 = 0.615 million. This is a direct cost to equity (or must be funded by a contingency or standby equity).

*Step 6: Broader consequence.* The PPA term and tariff typically start at COD, so the entire cash-flow profile shifts six months later; the debt grace period and the equity cash flows both move. The equity's loss of time value is additional to the shortfall and, because equity bears the residual, can easily exceed it.

*Interpretation.* LDs rarely make the SPV whole. They are sized to protect lenders' coverage ratios, not to compensate sponsors for lost time value. Sponsors should negotiate LD rates on the basis of the project's real daily cost of delay, not a standard percentage of contract value.

## 15.4 Non-Recourse Debt: Sizing, Coverage Ratios and Covenants

### 15.4.1 Cash flow available for debt service

All project coverage ratios start from **cash flow available for debt service (CFADS)**: the cash the project generates in a period that is available to pay interest and scheduled principal. It is defined contractually in the loan agreement, but in essence:

```
CFADS = Revenue − Operating costs − Taxes paid ± Working-capital movements
        (± Capex or maintenance reserve movements, as defined in the loan agreement)
```

Debt service itself is not deducted, and neither is any distribution to equity. Defining CFADS carefully is itself a negotiation: sponsors prefer definitions that include insurance proceeds, reserve releases and interest income; lenders prefer narrow ones. Two models with the same cash flows but different CFADS definitions can produce different headline ratios, so always read the definition before comparing ratios across deals.

### 15.4.2 The coverage ratios

**Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR).** For each period t:

```
DSCR_t = CFADS_t / (Interest_t + Scheduled principal_t)
```

It measures how many times the period's debt service is covered by the cash flow. A DSCR of 1.30 means CFADS can fall by about 23 percent (1 − 1/1.30) before debt service is no longer fully covered. Lenders focus on the **minimum** DSCR over the loan life, and the **average** DSCR, which indicates the typical cushion.

**Interest cover ratio (ICR).** CFADS divided by interest alone; used mainly during early operations or for bullet loans.

**Loan life coverage ratio (LLCR).** At any date, LLCR compares the present value of CFADS over the remaining loan life to the outstanding debt:

```
LLCR = [ Σ (t=1..N) CFADS_t / (1 + r)^t  +  DSRA balance ] / Debt outstanding
```

where r is the weighted average cost of the senior debt (the discount rate for the loan) and N is the remaining loan life. LLCR is a "solvency" style measure: it asks whether the cash flows over the loan's remaining life are sufficient, in present-value terms, to repay the debt. A project whose DSCR is dipping in one bad year might still have a comfortable LLCR if later years are strong.

**Project life coverage ratio (PLCR).** The same calculation, but with CFADS over the whole project (or contract) life rather than the loan life. The difference between PLCR and LLCR measures the "tail": the cash flow after the loan is repaid, which acts as an additional cushion. A tail of two years on a 20-year project is typical for contracted renewables.

**Gearing (debt-to-total-capital).** Debt / (Debt + Equity) at financial close; often capped (for example, at 75 percent) regardless of what DSCR sizing would allow.

Table 15.3 compares the ratios.

**Table 15.3 – Coverage ratios compared**

| Ratio | Formula | Horizon | What it tells lenders | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DSCR | CFADS / debt service | One period | Liquidity cushion each year | Sizing, distribution lock-up, default trigger |
| ICR | CFADS / interest | One period | Ability to pay interest | Early operations, bullet debt |
| LLCR | PV(CFADS over loan life) / debt | Loan life | Solvency over remaining loan | Refinancing tests, default triggers |
| PLCR | PV(CFADS over project life) / debt | Project life | Tail cushion beyond loan | Tenor decisions, refinancing capacity |
| Gearing | Debt / (Debt + Equity) | At close | Equity skin in the game | Debt-capacity cap, equity commitment |

Typical DSCR targets vary by revenue quality: contracted infrastructure with strong offtakers may carry minimum DSCRs of around 1.20 to 1.35 on a P50 case; assets with merchant exposure often require 1.7 or higher or conservative P90-based sizing. These ranges are illustrative; actual terms depend on the market, jurisdiction and offtaker rating.

**Example 15.2 – Computing DSCR, average DSCR and LLCR.** A small project has the following annual CFADS and scheduled debt service over a six-year loan (million). The loan's cost is 6 percent.

| Year | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CFADS | 14 | 16 | 18 | 18 | 16 | 14 |
| Debt service | 10 | 11 | 12 | 12 | 11 | 9 |

*Step 1: Annual DSCRs.* 14/10 = 1.400; 16/11 = 1.455; 18/12 = 1.500; 18/12 = 1.500; 16/11 = 1.455; 14/9 = 1.556.

*Step 2: Minimum and average.* The minimum DSCR is 1.400 (year 1); the average of the six ratios is (1.400 + 1.455 + 1.500 + 1.500 + 1.455 + 1.556)/6 = 8.865/6 = 1.477.

*Step 3: Debt outstanding at the start.* Since debt service repays the loan, the debt outstanding equals the PV of debt service at 6 percent:
10/1.06 + 11/1.06² + 12/1.06³ + 12/1.06⁴ + 11/1.06⁵ + 9/1.06⁶
= 9.434 + 9.790 + 10.075 + 9.505 + 8.220 + 6.345 = 53.369 million.

*Step 4: PV of CFADS over the loan life at 6 percent.*
14/1.06 + 16/1.06² + 18/1.06³ + 18/1.06⁴ + 16/1.06⁵ + 14/1.06⁶
= 13.208 + 14.240 + 15.113 + 14.258 + 11.956 + 9.869 = 78.644 million.

*Step 5: LLCR.* 78.644 / 53.369 = 1.474 (ignoring any DSRA balance).

*Interpretation.* The LLCR is close to the average DSCR because debt service is roughly proportional to CFADS. Where debt service is front-loaded relative to CFADS (as with a level annuity on a declining cash-flow profile), the minimum DSCR falls well below the LLCR, which is why lenders watch both.

### 15.4.3 Sizing debt: sculpting to a target DSCR

Rather than offering a loan and testing the ratios afterwards, lenders usually **size the debt from the cash flows**. The sponsor and lenders agree a target DSCR, a tenor and an interest rate; the model then computes the maximum debt the project can service. Two repayment profiles are common.

**Annuity or level repayment.** Debt service is constant (or a standard mortgage-style profile). The binding DSCR is the lowest, which occurs in the year when CFADS is lowest. If CFADS declines over time (as with degrading solar output), the minimum DSCR occurs late in the loan, and debt capacity is limited by that year.

**Sculpted repayment.** Debt service in each period is set equal to CFADS divided by the target DSCR. Repayments thereby track the cash-flow profile, and the DSCR is constant over the loan life. Debt capacity is the present value of the sculpted debt service at the loan's interest rate:

```
Debt service_t = CFADS_t / target DSCR
Debt capacity  = Σ Debt service_t / (1 + r)^t
```

Sculpting maximises debt for a given minimum DSCR, and with a constant DSCR the LLCR at financial close equals the target DSCR as well. The practical complication is **circularity**: interest depends on the opening balance, which depends on earlier repayments, which depend on debt service sizing, which depends on CFADS, which (through taxes and interest income) can depend on interest. Models solve this with iteration, macros or closed-form formulas.

In addition to the DSCR test, lenders apply a **gearing cap**, so debt capacity is the lesser of the DSCR-sized amount and the maximum percentage of total project cost.

**Example 15.3 – Sculpted debt sizing for the Marlow solar project, with a binding DSCR.** The project has the following assumptions (pre-tax, for clarity; real models include tax):

- Capacity 100 MW at a 25 percent capacity factor: P50 energy = 100 × 0.25 × 8,760 h = 219,000 MWh = 219 GWh in year 1.
- Degradation 0.5 percent per year.
- PPA price: 62 per MWh, flat nominal for 20 years.
- Operating costs: 2.6 million in year 1, escalating at 2 percent per year.
- Total project cost: 120 million.
- Debt: 18-year tenor, 5.5 percent fixed, target DSCR 1.30 (sculpted), gearing cap 75 percent.

*Step 1: Revenue and CFADS, year 1.* Revenue = 219,000 MWh × 62 = 13.578 million. CFADS = 13.578 − 2.600 = 10.978 million.

*Step 2: CFADS, later years.* Energy in year t is 219 × 0.995^(t−1) GWh; costs rise 2 percent a year. Year 2: revenue 13.510, costs 2.652, CFADS 10.858. Year 18: revenue 12.469 (219 × 0.995¹⁷ × 62/1,000), costs 3.641, CFADS 8.828. Year 20: CFADS 8.557 million. The profile declines steadily, because both degradation and cost inflation erode CFADS while the price is flat.

*Step 3: Sculpted debt service.* Year 1: 10.978 / 1.30 = 8.445 million. Year 2: 10.858 / 1.30 = 8.352. Year 18: 8.828 / 1.30 = 6.791.

*Step 4: Debt capacity.* The PV of these 18 amounts at 5.5 percent is 87.392 million.

*Step 5: Gearing test.* 87.392 / 120 = 72.8 percent, below the 75 percent cap (90 million). **The DSCR constraint is binding**; debt is 87.4 million.

*Step 6: Repayment schedule.* Year 1 interest = 87.392 × 5.5% = 4.807; principal = 8.445 − 4.807 = 3.638; closing balance 83.754. Year 2 interest = 4.606; principal = 3.746; balance 80.008. The final payment in year 18 has interest of 0.354 and principal of 6.437, leaving a zero balance.

*Step 7: LLCR and PLCR at close.* The LLCR equals 1.30 by construction. The PLCR, which includes years 19 and 20, is 1.370; the extra 0.07 is the tail.

*Step 8: Compare with a level annuity.* A level 18-year annuity at 5.5 percent for the same 87.392 million has annual debt service of 87.392 × 0.055 / (1 − 1.055⁻¹⁸) = 7.771 million. The DSCR is then 10.978/7.771 = 1.413 in year 1, 1.287 in year 9 and 8.828/7.771 = **1.136 in year 18**. A lender with a 1.30 minimum DSCR would have to cut the loan substantially under an annuity profile. Sculpting therefore supports considerably more debt for the same risk metric.

*Interpretation.* The sculpted profile front-loads less principal than an annuity, because CFADS falls over time. The project's equity gets cash earlier too, which matters for IRR.

### 15.4.4 Covenants: lock-up, default and reserves

Coverage ratios become operational through covenants, usually two thresholds:

- **Distribution lock-up** (for example, historic and projected DSCR below 1.15): equity distributions are blocked and cash is trapped in the project account. If the ratio recovers within a period, the trapped cash is released; if not, it may be applied to prepay debt.
- **Event of default** (for example, DSCR below 1.05): lenders may accelerate, enforce security or step in.

The gap between the base case and the lock-up ratio is the cushion for operating variation; the gap between lock-up and default is the cure period, which gives the sponsor time to react. Lock-ups are a gentle but effective form of control: they protect lenders, but also cost sponsors, because trapped cash defers equity returns and the IRR penalty is immediate.

**Reserve accounts** provide liquidity. A **DSRA** of six months' debt service allows the project to meet payments through a temporary shortfall. A **major maintenance reserve account** accumulates cash for large future repairs (for example, inverter replacement or turbine overhaul). Sponsors often replace cash-funded reserves with letters of credit, which free cash but add a contingent liability.

Other covenants restrict additional debt, change of control and amendments to project contracts, and require hedging, insurance and reporting.

### 15.4.5 Debt instruments and tenor

**Bank term loans** dominate, with miniperm structures (shorter tenor, balloon, cash sweep to incentivise refinancing) common where bank appetite is limited to seven or ten years. **Project bonds** attract institutional investors seeking long-dated stable cash flows. **Export-credit and development-finance** loans bring long tenors and political-risk comfort. **Mezzanine** debt fills the gap between senior debt and equity at higher cost. Longer tenor raises debt capacity but shrinks the tail and raises refinancing and merchant risk, so loans are usually repaid a year or two before the PPA or concession ends.

## 15.5 The Structure of a Project Finance Model

### 15.5.1 Why the model is the centrepiece

In a project financing the model is a contractual document: lenders size debt from it, test covenants against it, and attach it to the loan agreement as the base case, usually after an independent model audit.

### 15.5.2 Standard architecture

A good model separates inputs, calculations and outputs and flows information one way: inputs and scenarios; a timing module with period flags; construction (capex, IDC, sources and uses); operations (volumes, degradation, prices, costs); tax and accounting; debt (drawdown, sculpting, reserves, hedging); ratios and covenant tests; the cash waterfall; returns (project IRR, equity IRR, break-even price); and a checks and sensitivities module.

### 15.5.3 Good modelling practice

Several principles distinguish reliable models from fragile ones.

**Consistent timing.** Use one time axis (monthly, quarterly or semi-annual) with flags to control which formulas operate in which period. Mismatched periods between revenue and debt service lead to errors, particularly with semi-annual repayment and annual PPAs.

**One calculation per row, no hard-coded numbers in formulas.** Every assumption lives in the inputs sheet and is traceable. A single constant embedded in a formula is a classic source of undiscovered error.

**Integrity checks.** The balance sheet balances in every period; sources equal uses; debt closes to zero at maturity; cash waterfall outflows equal inflows. A master check cell aggregates all of them.

**Circularity control.** IDC, fees capitalised in the loan, sculpting and tax can all create circular references. Sound models either resolve them algebraically, use a macro with convergence checks, or break the loop with a copy-paste approach; circularity left uncontrolled produces unstable results.

**Nominal and real consistency.** Indexation, cost inflation and the discount rate must all be in the same terms; mixing real cash flows with nominal discount rates is a persistent infrastructure-model error (see Volume 1, Chapter 6).

### 15.5.4 Sensitivity and scenario analysis

The standard sensitivity set covers energy yield (P50, P90, P99), price or volume, operating costs, capex, unhedged interest rates, inflation, tax and COD delay. Lenders run combined downside cases in which the minimum DSCR must stay above 1.0, and often above the default covenant. **Break-even analysis** turns "cushion" into a number: the fall in yield that takes the minimum DSCR to the lock-up level, the default level and 1.00 (Example 15.5). Monte Carlo analysis complements deterministic cases for resource and merchant price risk, but its output is only as good as the assumed correlations and tails.

## 15.6 Renewable Project Economics

### 15.6.1 From resource to revenue

A utility-scale solar farm is, economically, a long-dated annuity with an unusually transparent structure: almost all cost is incurred up front, operating cost is small, fuel is free, and revenue depends on two numbers, the quantity of electricity produced and the price at which it is sold.

**Energy yield.** The first-year energy is capacity times capacity factor times hours in a year:

```
Energy (MWh) = Capacity (MW) × Capacity factor × 8,760
```

For 100 MW at a 25 percent capacity factor this is 219,000 MWh. Capacity factors differ widely by technology and location, and a point of capacity factor matters: in Section 15.6.5 a fall from 25 to 22 percent adds more than 9 per MWh to the cost of energy.

**Degradation.** Panels lose a small fraction of output each year; we use 0.5 percent a year, so output in year t is the first-year output times 0.995^(t−1).

**P50 and P90.** An independent engineer estimates the long-term average annual yield (the **P50**, the level exceeded with 50 percent probability) and its uncertainty. The **P90** is the yield level that is exceeded 90 percent of the time; the **P99** is exceeded 99 percent of the time. If annual yield is approximately normal with standard deviation σ, then P90 ≈ P50 − 1.28σ and P99 ≈ P50 − 2.33σ. These statistics are reported for a one-year horizon and a ten-year horizon; because inter-annual variation partly averages out, the ten-year P90 lies closer to the P50 than the one-year P90. Lenders look at the P90 (and sometimes P99) to test debt service in a bad year, and sponsors use the P50 for expected returns. When the P90 is, say, 8 percent below the P50, the difference is the "resource uncertainty haircut."

**Price.** A fixed-price PPA gives certainty; an indexed PPA transfers inflation risk to the offtaker; a merchant tail exposes the project to wholesale prices. For merchant exposure the key concept is the **capture price**, the average price the asset actually receives, which for solar is usually below the baseload average because solar plants all produce at the same hours and depress midday prices as penetration rises.

**Costs.** Operating costs (O&M, insurance, land lease, grid and asset-management fees, property taxes) are often fixed in real terms and indexed; major maintenance such as inverter replacement can appear as a lumpy mid-life cost.

### 15.6.2 The cost of electricity: LCOE

The **levelised cost of energy (LCOE)** is the constant price per MWh at which the discounted revenues equal the discounted costs over the asset's life:

```
LCOE = [ Capex + Σ Opex_t / (1 + r)^t ]  /  [ Σ Energy_t / (1 + r)^t ]
```

The discount rate r is the project's cost of capital. LCOE is the average cost, and the price at which the project exactly earns r. It is useful for comparing technologies and for judging whether a PPA price is plausible, but it has limits: it ignores timing and location of generation (a MWh at noon is not worth a MWh at 7 p.m.), integration and grid costs, the effect of taxes and subsidies unless explicitly modelled, and the financing structure beyond the single discount rate.

**Example 15.6 – LCOE and its sensitivity.** Using the Marlow project's data (capex 120, opex 2.6 growing 2 percent, energy 219 GWh falling 0.5 percent a year, 20 years), compute the LCOE at a 7 percent discount rate.

*Step 1: PV of operating costs.* Σ 2.6 × 1.02^(t−1) / 1.07^t for t = 1 to 20 = 32.032 million.

*Step 2: PV of capital and operating cost.* 120 + 32.032 = 152.032 million.

*Step 3: PV of energy.* Σ 219,000 × 0.995^(t−1) / 1.07^t = 2,237,396 MWh (discounted).

*Step 4: LCOE.* 152.032 million / 2,237,396 MWh = **67.95 per MWh**.

*Step 5: Sensitivity.* At a 6.375 percent discount rate (a 75/25 blend of 5.5 percent debt and 9 percent equity, ignoring tax) the LCOE falls to 65.48. At 5 percent it is 60.26; at 9 percent, 76.19. Changing capex to 100 or 140 (at 7 percent) gives 59.01 or 76.89. Reducing the capacity factor to 22 percent raises the LCOE to 77.22.

*Interpretation.* The LCOE moves by roughly 8 to 9 per MWh for each 20 million of capex, and by about 16 per MWh between 5 and 9 percent cost of capital. Renewable economics are capital-cost and financing-cost economics: a solar farm is a bet on the cost of capital as much as on the sun. This is why the sponsors' ability to access cheap, long-dated, non-recourse debt is a competitive advantage in renewables.

### 15.6.3 Equity returns and the leverage trap

Lenders' tests tell the sponsors how much debt is available. They do not tell them whether the project is worth doing. For that, sponsors evaluate **project IRR** (unlevered cash flow against total cost) and **equity IRR** (cash distributions against equity invested), and compare equity IRR to the required return on equity for the risk class (Volume 1, Chapters 10 and 11).

Leverage raises equity IRR only when the project's return exceeds the cost of debt. If project IRR is below the debt cost, leverage reduces equity IRR; if it is equal, leverage is neutral. This is the simple algebra behind positive and negative leverage.

**Example 15.4 – Equity IRR, the gearing cap and the break-even PPA price.** Continue with the Marlow data, but first compare two PPA prices.

*Case A: PPA price 62 per MWh.* From Example 15.3, debt is 87.392 million, equity is 120 − 87.392 = 32.608 million, and the sculpted DSCR is 1.30 each year. Equity cash flow each year is CFADS minus debt service. Year 1: 10.978 − 8.445 = 2.533; year 2: 10.858 − 8.352 = 2.506; years 19 and 20, after the loan is repaid, receive full CFADS of about 8.69 and 8.56. The equity IRR is **5.40 percent**. The unlevered project IRR (cash flows of −120 followed by 20 CFADS values) is 5.47 percent, essentially equal to the 5.5 percent cost of debt, so leverage adds nothing. The project does not clear a 9 percent equity hurdle.

*Case B: PPA price 66 per MWh.* Revenue in year 1 is 219,000 × 66 = 14.454 million; CFADS is 14.454 − 2.600 = 11.854; year 2: 11.730; year 3: 11.605; year 18: 9.633; year 20: 9.353.

*Step 1: DSCR-based capacity.* Sculpting at 1.30 gives debt capacity of 94.708 million.

*Step 2: Gearing cap.* 75 percent of 120 is 90.0 million; the cap binds. Debt is 90.0 million, equity 30.0 million.

*Step 3: Scale the debt service.* The sculpted debt service is multiplied by 90 / 94.708 = 0.95029. Year 1 debt service is (11.854 / 1.30) × 0.95029 = 8.665 million; year 2: 8.574; year 3: 8.483; year 18: 7.041. The DSCR is now 1.30 / 0.95029 = **1.368** every year (and LLCR is 1.368).

*Step 4: Equity cash flows.* Year 1: 11.854 − 8.665 = 3.189; year 2: 3.155; year 18: 2.591; year 19: 9.494; year 20: 9.353. Total distributions over 20 years are 70.96 million on 30.0 invested.

*Step 5: Equity IRR.* Solving −30.0 + Σ CF_t/(1+IRR)^t = 0 gives **8.81 percent**. The unlevered project IRR is 6.51 percent versus a 5.5 percent debt cost, so leverage is positive.

*Step 6: Break-even price.* Searching over price, the equity IRR reaches 9.00 percent at a PPA price of about **66.23 per MWh**. At that price the minimum DSCR is 1.374.

*Interpretation.* Between 62 and 66, the binding constraint switches from DSCR (cash flow limits debt) to gearing (the lender caps equity thinness). Above 66 additional price is a pure equity windfall: each additional 1 per MWh of price above 66 lifts equity IRR by roughly 0.8 percentage points (about 8.8 percent at 66, 10.5 percent at 68, 12.1 percent at 70). That is the operating leverage of a capital-heavy asset magnified by financial leverage.

> **Common mistake.** Reading a high equity IRR as evidence of a good project. Equity IRR conflates asset quality with financing. Always check the project IRR against the cost of debt, and the equity IRR against the risk-appropriate cost of equity. A 75 percent geared project with a 9 percent equity IRR is a thinly profitable asset with thick leverage.

### 15.6.4 Resource risk, covenants and the cushion

The sculpted debt of Example 15.4 (Case B) fixes debt service in nominal terms. If energy output falls short of the P50, CFADS falls proportionally more than revenue because operating costs are largely fixed, and the DSCR falls in an amplified way. This is the operating leverage that makes resource risk the main driver of equity risk.

**Example 15.5 – Stress test: P90 production, lock-up and default thresholds.** Keep the 66 per MWh case with debt of 90.0 million and the debt service schedule of Example 15.4. Now test lower production, expressed as a fraction of P50 in every year, with other inputs unchanged.

*Step 1: Production at 95 percent of P50.* Year 1 revenue is 14.454 × 0.95 = 13.731; CFADS = 13.731 − 2.600 = 11.131; DSCR = 11.131 / 8.665 = 1.285. In year 18 revenue at full P50 is 13.272, so at 95 percent it is 12.608; CFADS = 12.608 − 3.641 = 8.967 and DSCR = 8.967 / 7.041 = **1.274**, the minimum. Equity IRR falls to 6.00 percent.

*Step 2: Production at 92 percent of P50 (a plausible illustration of a one-year-style P90 haircut).* Year 1 DSCR = 1.235; the minimum is 1.217 in year 18. Equity IRR is **4.25 percent**.

*Step 3: Production at 90 percent of P50.* Minimum DSCR = **1.180**; equity IRR = **3.05 percent**.

*Step 4: Break-even production levels.* The production factor at which the minimum DSCR falls to the 1.15 lock-up level is **88.4 percent of P50**; to the 1.05 default level, **83.1 percent**; to 1.00, **80.5 percent**.

*Interpretation.* A drop of 8 percent in production (a P90-like year if applied permanently) lowers equity IRR from 8.8 to 4.25 percent, a fall of more than half, while the lenders' minimum DSCR remains well above the lock-up. The structure protects lenders far more than it protects equity. Lenders would face distress only after production falls roughly 17 to 20 percent below P50 permanently, an event far outside the P90 range for a single well-studied solar site, so a project with a 1.30 sculpted DSCR is safe from a creditor's perspective, while the equity can lose most of its return in a poor but not extreme year.

### 15.6.5 Merchant tail and hedging

A 20-year PPA may be shorter than the asset's 30-year life. Lenders give the merchant tail little or no value in sizing; sponsors should separate "contracted equity value" from "merchant tail value", since a large share of valuation resting on uncertain tail prices is a warning sign. Floating-rate debt is normally swapped to fixed for 75 to 100 percent of the exposure. For the Example 15.3 loan of 87.4 million, a 100 bp rise on the same principal schedule would lift year-1 debt service by about 0.87 million and cut the minimum DSCR from 1.30 to about 1.18.

## 15.7 Infrastructure, PPPs and Value for Money

### 15.7.1 Public-private partnerships

A **public-private partnership (PPP)** is a long-term contract under which a private consortium designs, builds, finances, operates and maintains an asset (a DBFOM structure) and is paid over 20 to 30 years. The SPV is the contracting entity, the structure of this chapter applies, and the public authority is the offtaker.

The central choice is the **payment mechanism**:

- **Availability payment.** The authority pays a fixed periodic amount as long as the asset is available at the specified quality (a hospital with working wards, a road open to traffic), with deductions for unavailability or poor performance. Demand risk stays with the public sector. This produces stable, bond-like cash flows and high leverage.
- **Demand-based (user-pay) revenue.** Tolls or user charges depend on traffic or usage. The private party bears demand risk, so debt sizing is more conservative, coverage ratios are higher, and equity returns are volatile. Projects with demand risk historically have a higher incidence of renegotiation and distress, because traffic forecasts are prone to optimism.
- **Hybrids.** The authority pays per user, or a base payment plus a share of upside, with caps and collars.

### 15.7.2 Value for money

Public authorities justify PPPs through **value for money (VfM)**: the claim that delivery through the private consortium is cheaper, on a risk-adjusted whole-life basis, than conventional public procurement. The standard tool is the **public sector comparator (PSC)**, a hypothetical cost of public delivery including a quantified value of the risks the public sector would retain.

The core difficulty is financing cost. Governments borrow more cheaply than private consortia, so the private financing premium must be earned back through risk transfer (construction cost and delay risk, whole-life maintenance risk) and efficiency. Whether the premium is justified depends on the discount rate used to compare the options, a long-running debate: discounting at the government's low borrowing rate makes PPPs look expensive; discounting at a risk-adjusted rate makes public delivery look cheaper than it really is because the risks that public delivery retains are not priced. A balanced approach values the transferred risks explicitly.

**Example 15.7 – The financing premium in an availability PPP.** A hospital costs 200 million to build. The consortium finances it with 180 million of senior debt (90 percent gearing) at 5.5 percent over 25 years and 20 million of equity. The annual operating and lifecycle cost is 6.0 million. Lenders require a minimum DSCR of 1.20. The government can borrow at 3.5 percent.

*Step 1: Annual debt service.* A 25-year annuity on 180 million at 5.5 percent: 180 × 0.055 / (1 − 1.055⁻²⁵) = 13.419 million.

*Step 2: CFADS needed.* 1.20 × 13.419 = 16.103 million.

*Step 3: Availability payment.* CFADS plus operating cost: 16.103 + 6.000 = **22.103 million a year**.

*Step 4: Equity return.* Equity receives CFADS minus debt service = 16.103 − 13.419 = 2.684 million a year for 25 years on 20 million invested: IRR = **12.75 percent** (before tax and before any risk events).

*Step 5: Public comparator financing.* If the government borrows 200 million at 3.5 percent over 25 years: 200 × 0.035 / (1 − 1.035⁻²⁵) = 12.135 million, so with operating cost the annual cost is 18.135 million.

*Step 6: The premium.* 22.103 − 18.135 = 3.968 million a year. Its present value at 3.5 percent over 25 years is 65.4 million, which is 21.9 percent of the PV of the public comparator's cost (298.9 million versus 364.3 million).

*Interpretation.* For the PPP to represent value for money, the transferred risks (construction overruns, delay, lifecycle failure, poor-quality operations) must be worth more than about 65 million in PV on a 200 million project, or the consortium must deliver measurable cost savings of that order. Both are plausible on a project with a history of public-sector overruns, but not automatic; the calculation shows why VfM claims require evidence rather than assertion. It also illustrates why the project's debt financing is only part of the story: the equity margin (12.75 percent on a 90 percent geared structure) is a significant component of the premium.

## 15.8 Implications for Corporate Leaders

For corporate managers, whether as sponsors, offtakers, contractors or lenders, four lessons stand out.

**Treat financing as part of the investment case.** The same asset has different equity returns under different structures. Compare the project's unlevered return with the corporate WACC (Volume 1, Chapter 11) to see whether the asset creates value, and use the financing to optimise, not to rescue a weak investment.

**Treat guarantees and equity-contribution agreements as contingent debt.** A parent guarantee of 30 million affects the sponsor's rating and covenant headroom even if the project debt is non-recourse on paper.

**Value the tail and the options separately.** Contracted cash flows deserve a low discount rate; merchant tails and expansion or repowering options deserve a higher rate or an option-pricing treatment (Volume 1, Chapter 18).

**Mind the rate environment.** Infrastructure returns are sensitive to financing costs, as the LCOE sensitivity showed. Projects approved at low rates may become uneconomic before financial close, so consider pre-close rate hedges and indexation in offtake pricing. Infrastructure financings that fail typically do so through optimistic demand forecasts, weak counterparties, short-tenor debt assumed to be refinanced, or thin coverage ratios with little room for error; lender-driven downside cases and independent audits exist to catch exactly these.

## Case Study

### Harbourline Toll Road: When the Traffic Forecast Meets the Lenders

*Illustrative case; the firm, the project and all figures are fictional.*

Aurelia Infrastructure Partners, a private infrastructure fund, has won a 30-year concession to build and operate Harbourline, a 42-kilometre toll road linking a port to an industrial region. The construction cost is 800 million, to be funded with 560 million of senior bank and bond debt (70 percent gearing) and 240 million of equity from Aurelia and two pension-fund co-investors. Unlike a typical renewable project, Harbourline has no availability payment: revenue is tolls, which depend on traffic.

The traffic forecast, prepared by the sponsors' consultant, projects steady growth as the industrial region develops. The lenders' own traffic adviser is more cautious: its base case is about 12 percent lower in the first five years, and its downside case, which assumes a competing rail freight link opens in year 8, is about 25 percent below the sponsors' forecast by year 10. The concession agreement allows toll increases in line with inflation but caps real increases, and gives the authority a right to terminate for default with compensation limited to senior debt outstanding after year 5.

On the construction side, the road is built under a fixed-price, date-certain EPC contract with a consortium of two contractors, each jointly and severally liable, backed by performance bonds of 10 percent of contract price. Delay LDs are set at a daily rate sized to cover interest and fixed costs, capped at 15 percent of the contract price. Geotechnical risk is excluded from the fixed price for a 3-kilometre section crossing marshland, with a provisional sum and a shared-overrun mechanism.

The debt is sized by the lenders on their traffic adviser's base case with a minimum DSCR of 1.60, an average DSCR of 1.80, and a sculpted repayment over 24 years. A DSRA covers six months of debt service. Lock-up is set at a DSCR of 1.30 and default at 1.10. The interest rate is hedged for 80 percent of the debt with a swap whose counterparty is a highly rated bank. The remaining 20 percent floats.

Under the sponsors' own traffic forecast, equity IRR is about 11 percent. Under the lenders' base case it is about 8 percent. Under the lenders' downside case, the minimum DSCR falls to 1.18 in year 9, below the lock-up threshold for three consecutive years, with equity distributions deferred until traffic recovers. The equity IRR in that case falls to about 3 percent.

Aurelia's investment committee is divided. One partner argues that the sponsors' forecast reflects the best available information about regional growth and that the lenders' downside is overly conservative. Another points out that the equity's 240 million is exposed to a risk, traffic, that no counterparty will take: the concession authority will not guarantee volumes, and the EPC and O&M contracts cannot hedge demand. A third argues for a revenue-sharing mechanism with the authority that would raise the DSCR in bad outcomes in exchange for giving up some upside, and for bringing in a third pension co-investor to reduce Aurelia's own exposure.

The committee must decide whether to proceed at the proposed capital structure, renegotiate the concession terms, restructure the debt (for instance with a longer tenor and lower gearing), or walk away.

### Discussion Questions

1. Using the principle that risks belong with the party best able to control or diversify them, identify which Harbourline risks are well allocated and which are not. Which risk is retained by equity, and why can it not be transferred by contract?
2. Explain how the lenders arrived at a minimum DSCR of 1.60 when the Marlow solar project (Example 15.3) was sized at 1.30. What features of revenue risk justify the difference, and how would the debt capacity change if the lenders had sized at 1.30?
3. The sponsors' forecast gives an equity IRR of about 11 percent, the lenders' base case about 8 percent. If the equity's required return is 10 percent, what should the committee conclude, and what additional analysis (for example, a probability weighting of cases) would you request?
4. Evaluate the proposal for revenue sharing with the authority, and the proposal to add a third co-investor. How does each affect the risk, return and control of Aurelia's equity, and what would you negotiate?

## Key Takeaways

- Project finance lends against a stand-alone SPV's contracted cash flows rather than the sponsor's balance sheet; "non-recourse" is in practice limited recourse, bounded by guarantees, equity undertakings and completion support.
- The value of the structure sits in the contract web (EPC, offtake, O&M, direct agreements) and in the lenders' control over cash through the waterfall, reserves and covenants.
- Allocate each risk to the party best able to control or price it; mis-allocation raises cost rather than lowering it.
- DSCR measures period liquidity cushion, LLCR and PLCR measure solvency over loan and project life; the lender's minimum DSCR and the gearing cap jointly determine debt capacity.
- Sculpting debt service to CFADS maximises debt for a given DSCR and equalises the cushion over time; a level annuity on a declining cash-flow profile concentrates risk in late years.
- Lock-up and default covenants convert coverage ratios into action; the gap between base case and lock-up is the operating cushion, and trapped cash costs equity IRR immediately.
- Project models are structural documents: consistent timing, no hard-codes, integrity checks, scenario switches, controlled circularity and consistent real/nominal treatment.
- Renewable economics are capital-cost economics: LCOE is highly sensitive to capex, capacity factor and the cost of capital; resource risk is amplified by operating leverage.
- Leverage raises equity IRR only when project IRR exceeds the cost of debt; high equity IRR can reflect financing rather than asset quality, and the lenders are protected far better than equity by a 1.30 DSCR.
- PPP value for money depends on whether transferred risk and efficiency exceed the private financing premium over public borrowing, which requires evidence, not assertion.
