# Chapter 3 — The Architecture of a DCF

Before computing anything, a DCF requires a decision about *whose* cash flow is being valued. This choice determines the discount rate, the treatment of debt, and the final bridge to per-share value. Get it wrong and the numbers, however carefully calculated, are meaningless.

## 3.1 Claims on a business

A business's operating cash flows are claimed by several parties.

| Claimant | Receives | Typical form |
|----------|----------|--------------|
| Lenders | Interest and principal | Contractual; senior |
| Preference shareholders | Fixed dividend | Contractual-ish; senior to equity |
| Equity shareholders | Residual cash flow | Dividends, buybacks; unlimited upside |
| Minority shareholders in subsidiaries | Share of subsidiary profits | Claim on part of consolidated cash flows |
| Government | Taxes | Paid before distributions to equity |

Valuation can focus on *all* claimants together (the "firm" or "enterprise") or only on equity holders. Both are legitimate; they must not be mixed.

## 3.2 Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF)

**FCFF** is the cash flow generated by operations available to *all* providers of capital — debt and equity — after paying taxes on operating profit and after reinvestment in the business. It is independent of capital structure (apart from the effect of taxes, handled in the discount rate).

**FCFF = EBIT × (1 − t) + Depreciation & amortisation − Capital expenditure − Increase in net working capital**

Equivalently: **FCFF = NOPAT − Net reinvestment**, where net reinvestment = capex − depreciation + ΔNWC.

FCFF is discounted at the **weighted average cost of capital (WACC)**. The result is the **enterprise value (EV)** — the value of operating assets. To reach equity value, subtract debt and other non-equity claims and add non-operating assets (Chapter 13).

## 3.3 Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)

**FCFE** is the cash flow left for equity holders after the firm has paid taxes, met reinvestment needs and serviced debt (interest and net principal).

**FCFE = Net income + D&A − Capex − ΔNWC + Net borrowing**

(where net borrowing = new debt issued − debt repaid).

Equivalently, **FCFE = FCFF − Interest × (1 − t) + Net borrowing**.

FCFE is discounted at the **cost of equity**, and the result is directly the **equity value**. No bridge for debt is needed, because debt service has already been deducted from the cash flows.

## 3.4 Dividend discount model (DDM)

The DDM discounts *dividends* actually paid at the cost of equity:

**Equity value = Σ Dividendₜ ÷ (1 + Ke)ᵗ**

The DDM is a special case of FCFE valuation: when the firm pays out all FCFE as dividends, they are identical. When dividends are less than FCFE (cash accumulates) the DDM understates value unless the retained cash is treated carefully; when dividends exceed FCFE (the firm borrows or issues shares to pay) it overstates. The DDM works well for stable, high-payout firms and for financial institutions where FCFE is hard to define.

## 3.5 Which to use? FCFF versus FCFE

| Situation | Preferred model | Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|--------|
| Stable leverage; clear debt ratio | Either | Should give consistent answers |
| Leverage changing materially | FCFF (with changing WACC) or APV | FCFE requires continuous modelling of debt flows |
| Highly leveraged or distressed | FCFF/APV | Equity is a residual of a residual; noisy |
| Financial institutions | FCFE / DDM | Debt is operating raw material; FCFF undefined |
| Firm with negative FCFE but positive FCFF | FCFF | FCFE negative due to debt paydown misleads |
| Multiple business units | FCFF by unit | Different risks, different WACCs |

FCFF is the workhorse of professional practice for non-financial firms because it separates the *operating* valuation from the *financing* decision, and because it does not require forecasting the debt schedule in detail.

## 3.6 The consistency rules

A DCF has internal consistency requirements that are easy to state, easy to violate and often decisive for the result.

1. **Cash flow ↔ discount rate.** FCFF → WACC. FCFE → Ke. Dividends → Ke.
2. **Cash flow ↔ claim.** FCFF → EV (then subtract debt etc.). FCFE → equity value (do *not* subtract debt).
3. **Cash flow ↔ currency and inflation.** Nominal cash flows with nominal rate in the same currency.
4. **Growth ↔ reinvestment.** Growth in operating income requires reinvestment (Chapter 7). A terminal growth rate must carry matching terminal reinvestment.
5. **Discount rate ↔ risk of cash flows.** Use a rate that reflects the *business risk* of the cash flows, with leverage consistent with the capital structure assumed.
6. **Tax treatment ↔ tax rate.** Use operating tax (on EBIT) for FCFF; the tax shield on interest enters via after-tax cost of debt in WACC. Don't double-count by also reducing FCFF for interest.
7. **Cash and non-operating assets ↔ income.** If interest income on cash is excluded from FCFF (as it should be), add the *value* of cash and investments separately.
8. **Timing ↔ convention.** Mid-year cash flows with mid-year discounting; the valuation date matches the balance-sheet date used for the bridge.

> **Pitfall.** The classic violation: discounting FCFE at WACC "to be conservative". The WACC is lower than the cost of equity, so this *overstates* value. FCFE at WACC is wrong in both directions of intuition.

## 3.7 The structure of a DCF model

A professional DCF workbook typically has these modules:

1. **Inputs and assumptions sheet** — growth, margins, tax rate, working-capital days, capex ratio, discount-rate inputs, scenario switches.
2. **Historical analysis** — three to five years of financial statements reformulated into operating and financing components; ratios (ROIC, margins, turnover).
3. **Forecast** — revenue, operating costs, EBIT, tax, NOPAT; reinvestment (capex, depreciation, working capital).
4. **Free cash flow** — FCFF for each year.
5. **Discount-rate module** — cost of equity (CAPM), cost of debt, weights, WACC (by year if leverage changes).
6. **Terminal value** — Gordon and (as cross-check) exit multiple.
7. **Valuation and bridge** — EV, adjustments, equity value, per-share value.
8. **Sensitivity and scenarios** — data tables on WACC × g and key operating drivers.
9. **Checks** — balance sheet balances, cash reconciles, implied multiples and returns are reasonable.

## 3.8 Introducing Meridian Foods Ltd

Our running case is a packaged-foods manufacturer with the following history and attributes. All figures are ₹ crore.

**Business.** Meridian makes branded biscuits, ready-to-cook mixes and snacks, sold through distributors and modern trade across India. It operates five plants and has strong brands in several regional categories.

**Latest financials (base year, FY2026).**

| Item | ₹ crore |
|------|--------:|
| Revenue | 2,000 |
| EBITDA | 340 |
| Depreciation & amortisation | 100 |
| EBIT | 240 (margin 12.0%) |
| Interest expense | 54 |
| Profit before tax | 186 |
| Tax @ 25% | 46.5 |
| Net income | 139.5 |

**Balance sheet (31 March 2026).**

| Assets | ₹ cr | Liabilities & equity | ₹ cr |
|--------|-----:|----------------------|-----:|
| Net fixed assets | 900 | Equity (share capital + reserves) | 1,100 |
| Net working capital (operating) | 200 | Borrowings (all debt) | 600 |
| Goodwill & intangibles | 100 | | |
| Long-term investments (non-operating) | 100 | | |
| Cash & equivalents | 150 | | |
| Other operating assets | 250 | | |
| **Total** | **1,700** | **Total** | **1,700** |

Operating invested capital = net fixed assets 900 + operating NWC 200 + intangibles 100 + other operating assets 250 = **₹1,450 crore**. As a cross-check, equity 1,100 + debt 600 − cash 150 − non-operating investments 100 = 1,450. Base-year NOPAT is 240 × 0.75 = 180, so the base-year return on invested capital is about 12.4%.

Shares outstanding: **50 crore**. Face value ₹10.

**Strategy.** Management plans to grow revenue by 14%, 12%, 10%, 9% and 8% over the next five years (premiumisation, distribution expansion, new categories), with EBIT margin rising from 12.0% to 14.5% on operating leverage and mix. Every ₹1 of additional revenue requires ₹0.50 of additional invested capital (a sales-to-capital ratio of 2.0). The corporate tax rate is 25%.

**Market inputs.** Risk-free rate (10-year government bond) 7.0%; equity risk premium 6.0%; industry unlevered beta 0.75; target debt-to-equity 25% (20% debt-to-capital); pre-tax cost of debt 9%.

These inputs will be justified, challenged and refined in Chapters 4–13; the final valuation appears in Chapter 14.

## Chapter 3 summary

- FCFF belongs to all capital providers → discount at WACC → enterprise value → bridge to equity.
- FCFE belongs to equity → discount at Ke → equity value directly.
- DDM is a special case of FCFE when payout equals FCFE.
- Consistency rules (cash flow ↔ discount rate ↔ claim ↔ currency ↔ growth ↔ reinvestment) keep a model honest.
- Meridian Foods Ltd is the running example for Parts II–IV.
