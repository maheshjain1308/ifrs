# Preface {.unnumbered}

Every investment decision, every acquisition, every capital-budgeting exercise and every listing rests on a single question: *what is this worth?* Discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation is the most complete answer finance has to that question. Its logic is a single sentence: **an asset is worth the cash it will generate for its owners, adjusted for the timing and the risk of that cash.** Everything else in this book — free cash flows, betas, terminal values, bridges from enterprise to equity — is a way of making that sentence precise and usable.

DCF has a curious reputation. To its admirers it is the only rigorous valuation method, the one that forces an analyst to state explicitly what she believes about growth, profitability, reinvestment and risk. To its critics it is "garbage in, garbage out", a spreadsheet that can be tuned to deliver any number its author desires. Both views contain truth. DCF is rigorous in structure and fragile in inputs. The craft of valuation lies in disciplining the inputs: tying growth to reinvestment, tying margins to competitive advantage, tying discount rates to the risk of the cash flows actually being valued, and tying the whole story to evidence.

This book is written for three kinds of reader:

- **Students** preparing for professional examinations (CA, CFA, CMA, MBA finance) who need a clear, structured treatment with worked examples and practice problems.
- **Analysts and finance professionals** who build DCF models and want a reference for the many judgement calls that textbooks gloss over.
- **Business owners, founders and managers** who must understand what a valuation report is saying, and what it is *not* saying.

## How the book is organised

**Part I – Foundations** (Chapters 1–3) explains what valuation is, reviews the mathematics of discounting, and sets out the architecture of a DCF: enterprise versus equity, FCFF versus FCFE, and the consistency rules that keep a model honest.

**Part II – Cash Flows** (Chapters 4–8) shows how to turn financial statements into free cash flows, how to forecast, and how to normalise accounting data so that it reflects economic reality.

**Part III – Discount Rates and Terminal Value** (Chapters 9–12) covers the cost of equity, beta, the cost of debt, WACC and the terminal value — which, in most valuations, carries more than half of the answer.

**Part IV – Building and Testing the Valuation** (Chapters 13–16) takes the model from enterprise value to a value per share, works a complete case study end to end, and teaches sensitivity analysis, scenarios, simulation and cross-checks.

**Part V – Special Situations** (Chapters 17–24) adapts DCF to leveraged capital structures (APV), financial institutions, cyclical firms, start-ups, distressed companies, emerging-market and Indian contexts, real options, ESG, and finally to the question of professional practice and common errors.

**Part VI – Applications and Extensions** (Chapters 25–33) puts the tools to work: mergers and acquisitions and synergy valuation, private companies and disputes, a second full case study (a listed pharmaceutical company), a library of six compact valuations, a fully integrated three-statement model that reconciles FCFF with FCFE, taxes and tax losses, sector primers for fourteen industries, a chapter on growth, competitive advantage and the value of ROIC, and a closing chapter on the limits of DCF.

**Appendices** provide formula sheets, two sets of practice problems with full solutions, a glossary, discount-factor tables, a spreadsheet blueprint and answers to the numerical review questions.

## The running case

Throughout the book we follow a fictional company, **Meridian Foods Ltd**, a packaged-foods manufacturer. Its numbers are introduced in Chapter 3 and developed chapter by chapter until the complete valuation in Chapter 14, and returns in Chapters 25 and 29. All figures are in ₹ crore unless stated. Meridian is entirely invented; any resemblance to a real company is coincidental, and the figures are for teaching, not investment advice.

## A note on numbers

Worked examples are rounded for readability, so a spreadsheet rebuild may differ in the last decimal. Every market input (risk-free rate, equity risk premium, tax rate) is an *illustration*, not a forecast; source current inputs when you value a real business.

# How to Use This Book {.unnumbered}

**If you are new to valuation**, read Part I carefully, then Chapters 4, 5, 9, 10, 11 and 12, then the case study in Chapter 14. Work the practice problems in Appendix B before reading further.

**If you build models for a living**, read the "Pitfalls" boxes in each chapter, Chapters 8, 12, 13 and 15, and Chapter 24. They are where most real-world valuation errors occur.

**If you are revising for an examination**, use the chapter summaries and key-formula boxes, the formula sheet in Appendix A, and the practice problems. Examination questions typically test (i) computing free cash flow from a set of data, (ii) computing WACC and un-levering/re-levering beta, (iii) terminal value, and (iv) going from enterprise value to equity value.

**Conventions.** Formulas are set in bold within the text. *Key idea* boxes summarise the central point of a section. *Pitfall* boxes flag common errors. *Worked example* boxes give fully-solved numerical illustrations. At the end of each chapter, a short list of *review questions* tests recall.
