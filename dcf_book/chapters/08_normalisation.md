# Chapter 8 — Normalising Earnings and Cleaning the Base Year

The base year is the platform on which every forecast stands. If it is distorted — by one-offs, acquisitions, accounting changes or cyclical peaks — every number above it inherits the distortion. This chapter covers the normalisation adjustments that professional valuers routinely make.

## 8.1 Why normalise?

Reported operating profit is a good starting point but rarely a *maintainable* level. Normalisation seeks the level of earnings that the business can sustain under normal conditions, free of unusual influences. The result is "maintainable EBIT" (or EBITDA), a central concept in income-approach valuation under Indian valuation practice as well as globally.

## 8.2 Categories of adjustments

### 8.2.1 Non-recurring items
Gains and losses from sale of property, one-time legal settlements, restructuring, impairments, insurance recoveries. Remove them from the base year; consider whether similar items occur regularly.

### 8.2.2 Non-operating items
Rental income from surplus property, dividend or interest income from investments, profits of associates. Remove from EBIT; value the underlying asset separately.

### 8.2.3 Accounting policy differences and changes
If the target uses a different depreciation policy or revenue-recognition approach from peers, adjust to a common basis. Where a standard changed during the historical window (e.g., adoption of Ind AS 116 in FY2020), restate history to be comparable.

### 8.2.4 Related-party and owner-related adjustments
In private and closely held companies, owner-managers may take salary above or below market, charge personal expenses to the company, or transact with related parties on non-arm's-length terms. Replace with market-rate equivalents. In a valuation of an owner-managed business, a "replacement cost of management" is usually deducted.

### 8.2.5 Acquisitions and disposals
Pro-forma the base year to include a full year of acquired businesses (and exclude divested ones), so that growth is organic and margin is representative.

### 8.2.6 Inflation, currency and commodity effects
If the base year benefited from unusually favourable commodity prices or exchange rates, adjust to mid-cycle levels (see Section 8.5).

## 8.3 A worked example: normalising Meridian

Suppose Meridian's reported FY2026 profit statement included the following, in ₹ crore:

| Item | Amount | Treatment |
|------|-------:|-----------|
| Reported EBIT | 252.0 | starting point |
| Gain on sale of a surplus warehouse | (14.0) | non-recurring, non-operating — remove |
| Restructuring cost: closure of an old plant | +6.0 | one-off — add back |
| Insurance recovery for a fire | (3.0) | one-off — remove |
| Rental income on idle land (included in revenue) | (2.0) | non-operating — remove; value the land separately |
| Promoter-family salary above market | +4.0 | related-party — normalise |
| **Maintainable EBIT** | **243.0** | |

The result, 243, is close to but not identical with the 240 used in earlier chapters; our teaching case uses 240 for simplicity (as if the adjustments had already been made). In a real engagement the normalisation schedule is itself an exhibit in the valuation report, with evidence for each line.

## 8.4 Working-capital normalisation

If year-end working capital is unusually low (because of aggressive collection at year-end) or high (inventory build before a festival season), the base-year NWC percentage is unrepresentative. Use the average of monthly balances if available, or the three-year average ratio. The *change* in NWC matters in the first forecast year; a distorted base distorts year 1 cash flow.

## 8.5 Cyclical businesses: mid-cycle margins

For cyclical industries (metals, cement, shipping, capital goods, commodities) the base year may be at the peak or trough. Valuation based on a peak year overstates; a trough year understates. Normalise by:

1. **Averaging margins** over a full cycle (typically 7–10 years).
2. **Using mid-cycle commodity prices** (a price deck from forward curves or long-run cost curves).
3. **Modelling the cycle explicitly** — rising and falling for some years — and reverting to the mid-cycle in the terminal year.

*Example.* A steel producer earned EBIT margins of 6%, 14%, 22%, 17%, 4%, −3%, 8%, 18% over eight years. The average is 10.75%; use roughly 10–11% for the terminal year and normalise the base year accordingly. The terminal value should *never* be based on peak margins.

## 8.6 Capex normalisation

If a company underspent on maintenance capex in recent years, reported FCFF is flattering. Compare capex to depreciation and to peer capex intensity over the cycle; adjust to a sustainable level. Likewise, a one-off expansion year inflates capex; its growth benefit appears only in later revenue, so treat it as growth capex tied to specific revenue additions.

## 8.7 Normalising the tax rate

Reported effective tax rates include one-offs: deferred tax reversals, prior-period items, MAT credits, tax holidays. Normalise to a marginal rate in the terminal period; in the forecast, model known incentives with their expiry dates.

## 8.8 Stock-based compensation and employee costs

Cash salary is clearly an operating cost; stock compensation is also a cost, even though it involves no cash outlay. Do not add it back to EBIT. Failing to do so inflates profit and FCFF, particularly in technology firms where SBC may be 10–20% of revenue.

## 8.9 Adjustments for debt-like items

Normalisation also affects the bridge. Items that behave like debt and should be deducted from enterprise value include:

- Unfunded pension and gratuity obligations (post-tax).
- Lease liabilities (when treated as debt).
- Contingent liabilities that are probable and measurable.
- Deferred consideration for acquisitions.
- Overdue statutory dues, legal settlements payable.
- Redeemable preference shares and convertible instruments (see Chapter 13).

The principle: if the business will have to pay it with cash that is *not* in FCFF, it belongs in the bridge.

## 8.10 Documentation and sign-off

Each adjustment needs: (i) a source (annual report note, management certificate, ledger), (ii) a rationale, (iii) a quantification, (iv) a tax effect. Maintain a normalisation schedule in the workpapers. Under Indian valuation standards (ICAI Valuation Standards, Registered Valuers rules), the valuer must document the basis of each adjustment and its source.

## 8.11 Common mistakes

1. **Over-adjusting.** Adding back every unfavourable item and ignoring favourable ones.
2. **Under-adjusting.** Extrapolating a peak-year profit.
3. **Forgetting the tax effect** of each adjustment.
4. **Adjusting EBIT but not working capital or capex.**
5. **Not applying the same adjustments to peer data** used in multiples.

## Chapter 8 summary

- Maintainable earnings, not reported earnings, form the base.
- Remove non-recurring and non-operating items; normalise owner-related and related-party items.
- For cyclical firms use mid-cycle margins.
- Normalise capex, working capital and tax.
- Capture debt-like items in the bridge; document each adjustment.
