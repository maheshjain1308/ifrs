# Chapter 7 — Forecasting: Growth, Margins and Reinvestment

A DCF is only as good as its forecast. Yet forecasting is where valuations most often go wrong — not through arithmetic, but through stories that are internally inconsistent. This chapter builds a disciplined forecasting framework in which growth, profitability and reinvestment are tied together.

## 7.1 The forecasting philosophy

Three principles guide good forecasts.

1. **Start from the outside in.** Begin with the market: size, growth, competitors, regulation. Then position the company within it. A forecast that has a company growing faster than its market indefinitely must explain whose share it is taking.
2. **Tie the three pillars together.** Growth in operating income comes from revenue growth, margin change, or both. Revenue growth requires reinvestment. Margin and capital efficiency determine how much value the growth creates. Never forecast these independently.
3. **Let assumptions fade toward the sustainable.** Competitive forces pull growth toward the economy's rate, margins toward industry norms and returns toward the cost of capital — except where durable advantages justify otherwise. The forecast should show *how and when* the convergence occurs.

## 7.2 The explicit forecast period

How many years should the explicit forecast cover? The answer depends on how long the company's current growth and margins will differ from steady state.

| Company type | Typical explicit period |
|--------------|-------------------------|
| Mature, stable (utilities, staples) | 5 years |
| Moderate growth | 5–7 years |
| High-growth or margin-expansion story | 10 years or more |
| Start-ups, turnarounds | 10–15 years, with a fade |

A short explicit period forces the terminal value to carry heavy weight and to embed non-steady-state assumptions; a very long period invites spurious precision. Many practitioners use five years of detailed forecast plus a *fade* period (years 6–10) in which growth and margins interpolate to terminal values.

## 7.3 Forecasting revenue

### 7.3.1 Top-down
Revenue = Market size × Market share. For Meridian: the Indian packaged-foods categories in which it operates are expected to grow roughly 9–10% in nominal terms; Meridian's growth above that (14% in year 1) reflects share gains and category expansion, which should *decline* toward the market rate. That is why the plan has growth fading from 14% to 8%.

### 7.3.2 Bottom-up
Revenue = Volume × Price, by segment, channel or geography. E.g., number of outlets × average throughput × price per unit. Bottom-up forecasts expose capacity constraints and price/volume trade-offs.

### 7.3.3 Cross-checks
- Compare forecast revenue growth with historical growth, industry growth and peer growth.
- Compute implied market share and check plausibility.
- Check absolute levels: ₹3,300 crore in five years — does the distribution reach and plant capacity support it?
- Check that revenue growth in the fade years converges toward nominal GDP.

## 7.4 Forecasting margins

Operating margin = Gross margin − (Selling, general & administrative ÷ Revenue) etc. Forecast margin by cost line, or directly as EBIT margin with justification.

**Sources of margin change:**

- **Operating leverage.** Fixed costs spread over a larger revenue base raise margin as sales grow. Estimate the fixed/variable split.
- **Mix.** Shift toward higher-margin products or channels.
- **Pricing power and input costs.** For FMCG: commodity costs (wheat, palm oil, packaging), pass-through ability and lag.
- **Scale and efficiency programmes.** Procurement, automation.
- **Competitive intensity.** Margin gains are typically competed away over time unless there is a moat.

For Meridian the plan has EBIT margin rising from 12.0% (FY2026) to 14.5% (FY2031), a gain of 2.5 percentage points over five years. A sanity check: leading packaged-food peers earn EBIT margins of 15–20%; Meridian at 14.5% remains below them, so the target is plausible, though not guaranteed.

> **Pitfall.** *Hockey-stick margins.* Forecasts often show margins flat for two years and then jumping. Unless a specific event justifies a step change, margin paths should be smooth and attributable to identified drivers.

## 7.5 Forecasting reinvestment

Reinvestment is the cash the business must put back to create the forecast growth. There are three ways to forecast it, each of which should lead to similar answers.

### 7.5.1 Line-by-line
Forecast capex, depreciation and working capital separately. Capex comes from plant capacity needs; depreciation from the asset schedule; working capital from days or percentage of revenue. Rigorous but requires detailed data.

### 7.5.2 Sales-to-capital ratio
**Reinvestment = ΔRevenue ÷ (Sales-to-capital ratio)**

The sales-to-capital ratio measures incremental revenue per rupee of incremental invested capital. It is the marginal counterpart of capital turnover. If Meridian generates ₹2.00 of additional revenue for each ₹1.00 of net new investment (net capex plus ΔNWC), the sales-to-capital ratio is 2.0.

Historical check: average capital turnover is 1.38× (2,000 ÷ 1,450), lower than the marginal 2.0, because the *marginal* investment is expected to be more efficient than the existing stock of capital (which includes goodwill and older, costlier assets). Is 2.0 plausible? Compare history: over the last three years revenue rose from about 1,416 to 2,000 (+584), while invested capital rose from 1,100 to 1,450 (+350), a marginal ratio of 1.67. Meridian's plan assumes a *better* marginal ratio (2.0) than it has achieved recently. This is an aggressive assumption we will test in sensitivity analysis. For now, we adopt it as management's plan; a more cautious analyst would use 1.6–1.8.

### 7.5.3 Reinvestment rate and the growth equation
**Growth in NOPAT = Reinvestment rate × ROIC**

where the reinvestment rate = Net investment ÷ NOPAT. If a firm reinvests 40% of NOPAT at an ROIC of 15%, NOPAT grows 6% a year. Conversely, to grow at 6% with ROIC 15% the firm needs a 40% reinvestment rate. This identity is the single most useful consistency check in forecasting.

> **Key idea.** *Growth is not free.* For given ROIC, more growth requires more reinvestment, leaving less FCFF. A valuation that raises growth without raising reinvestment is simply creating value from nothing.

## 7.6 The expected growth identity applied to Meridian

Year 1: NOPAT growth = (213.75 ÷ 180) − 1 = 18.75%. This exceeds revenue growth (14%) because the margin also expands. The reinvestment rate is 140 ÷ 213.75 = 65.5%. Growth fed by margin expansion is not the same as growth fed by reinvestment, and the growth identity (g = reinvestment rate × ROIC) applies to growth that comes from *new capital*; margin gains are a one-off lift that must eventually stop. In the terminal state, a reinvestment rate of 33.3% at an ROIC of 15% gives g = 5% (Chapter 12). ✔

## 7.7 Scenario-based forecasting

Rather than one forecast, build several scenarios, each internally coherent, and attach probabilities.

| Scenario | Revenue CAGR (5 yrs) | FY2031 EBIT margin | Sales-to-capital | Probability |
|----------|---------------------:|-------------------:|-----------------:|------------:|
| Bear | 7% | 12.0% | 1.5 | 25% |
| Base | 10.6% | 14.5% | 2.0 | 50% |
| Bull | 13% | 16.0% | 2.2 | 25% |

A probability-weighted value is the expected value; the spread shows the uncertainty. Chapter 15 returns to this.

## 7.8 Building the Meridian forecast

Using the plan's assumptions:

| ₹ crore | FY26A | FY27E | FY28E | FY29E | FY30E | FY31E |
|---------|------:|------:|------:|------:|------:|------:|
| Revenue growth | 13.1% | 14.0% | 12.0% | 10.0% | 9.0% | 8.0% |
| Revenue | 2,000.0 | 2,280.0 | 2,553.6 | 2,809.0 | 3,061.8 | 3,306.7 |
| EBIT margin | 12.0% | 12.5% | 13.0% | 13.5% | 14.0% | 14.5% |
| EBIT | 240.0 | 285.0 | 332.0 | 379.2 | 428.6 | 479.5 |
| NOPAT (25% tax) | 180.0 | 213.8 | 249.0 | 284.4 | 321.5 | 359.6 |
| Net reinvestment | — | 140.0 | 136.8 | 127.7 | 126.4 | 122.5 |
| **FCFF** | — | **73.7** | **112.2** | **156.7** | **195.1** | **237.1** |
| Reinvestment rate | — | 65.5% | 54.9% | 44.9% | 39.3% | 34.1% |

Reinvestment is large early (66% of NOPAT) and falls as growth slows — typical for a company growing out of a heavy investment phase. FCFF rises more than threefold over five years even though NOPAT rises 1.7×, because the *reinvestment burden falls as growth fades*.

*Cross-check of ROIC.* Invested capital grows: 1,450 + 140 = 1,590 (FY27), 1,727 (FY28), 1,855 (FY29), 1,981 (FY30), 2,104 (FY31). ROIC on opening capital: FY27: 213.8/1,450 = 14.7%; FY28: 249.0/1,590 = 15.7%; FY29: 284.4/1,727 = 16.5%; FY30: 321.5/1,855 = 17.3%; FY31: 359.6/1,981 = 18.2%.

ROIC climbs to about 18%, above the 15% assumed in the terminal period, and that is a warning worth heeding. ROIC rises because margin expansion and a high sales-to-capital ratio coexist. In the terminal period we assume ROIC *fades* to 15% (still well above WACC 11.2%), a conservative convergence. If we believed Meridian could sustain 18%, terminal value would be higher. Document the decision either way.

## 7.9 The fade period

For many companies, a sharp drop from 8% growth to 5% growth at year 6 is unrealistic; a fade period smooths it. A simple 5-year fade would take growth from 8% to 5% in equal steps (7.4%, 6.8%, 6.2%, 5.6%, 5.0%) and margins from 14.5% toward a terminal level, and ROIC from 18% to 15%. The valuation effect is usually modest but the forecast is more defensible. In the Meridian base case we keep the five-year explicit period for clarity and encode the fade in the terminal assumptions; Chapter 14 shows the fade variant as a sensitivity.

## 7.10 Forecasting with competitive-advantage logic

The length of time a company can earn ROIC above its cost of capital is its *competitive-advantage period*. Sources: brands, switching costs, network effects, scale, regulation, patents. Evidence: persistent high ROIC across a business cycle, pricing power, stable share. A DCF should explicitly link the assumed advantage period to these sources. For commoditised businesses with no moat, ROIC should converge to WACC after a short period; for strong brands it may stay elevated for decades.

## 7.11 Checklist for a defensible forecast

1. Revenue growth consistent with market growth and share.
2. Margin changes attributed to identified drivers.
3. Reinvestment consistent with growth (via sales-to-capital or line items).
4. ROIC trajectory stated and plausible.
5. Working capital days and capex intensity compared with history and peers.
6. Tax rate path stated.
7. Growth and ROIC fade toward steady state.
8. Scenarios and sensitivities identified.

## Chapter 7 summary

- Forecast from the outside in; tie growth, margins and reinvestment together.
- Reinvestment = ΔRevenue ÷ sales-to-capital; growth = reinvestment rate × ROIC.
- Use fade periods and scenarios; avoid hockey-sticks.
- Check ROIC path against competitive-advantage logic.
