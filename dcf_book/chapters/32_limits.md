# Chapter 32 — The Limits of DCF: Critiques, Behaviour and Judgement

A book on DCF should end with an honest account of what DCF cannot do. The method is powerful, but it is not an oracle. This chapter reviews the standard criticisms, the behavioural traps that afflict even careful practitioners, the contexts where DCF is the wrong primary tool, and the habits that distinguish good valuers from merely competent ones.

## 32.1 The standard criticisms

### 32.1.1 "Garbage in, garbage out"
The criticism: a DCF can be tuned to produce any answer. Response: true — which is why a DCF must be *disciplined* (growth tied to reinvestment, ROIC fades, TV share disclosed, scenarios and reverse-DCF cross-checks). A DCF that is documented, consistent and tested produces a *defensible range*, not an arbitrary number. The remedy for tunable models is transparency, not abandoning the method.

### 32.1.2 "Terminal value dominates"
True: for most going concerns, 60–85% of value is in the TV. The remedy is to treat TV as a hypothesis to be tested (Chapter 12) and to examine implied multiples, ROIC and growth. If a valuer cannot defend the terminal state, they cannot defend the valuation.

### 32.1.3 "The discount rate is unknowable"
Estimates of the ERP and beta carry wide error bands. But a precise but wrong discount rate is worse than an approximate right one; show sensitivity to the rate and avoid false precision. Where the discount rate is deeply uncertain, express value as a function of it (as in the WACC × g tables).

### 32.1.4 "The future is not predictable"
Forecasts are uncertain; valuation is a statement about expectations. The method does not require perfect forecasts, only *unbiased expected values* and honest ranges. Scenario analysis and simulation represent the uncertainty rather than ignoring it.

### 32.1.5 "Markets know better"
Market prices aggregate many opinions; a DCF represents one. Where market prices are the only observable signal (liquid, widely followed stocks), the burden is on the DCF to explain differences (reverse DCF). But markets also display bubbles and panics, so an estimated intrinsic value is a useful anchor and warning.

### 32.1.6 "DCF ignores flexibility and strategy"
A static DCF misses managerial options (expand, abandon). Real-options thinking (Chapter 23) addresses that, with care.

### 32.1.7 "DCF is too slow and complicated"
For quick decisions a multiple is fine. The point of DCF is *understanding*: forcing explicit assumptions. Even a simple one-stage DCF using the value-driver formula (EV = NOPAT × (1 − g/ROIC)/(WACC − g)) reveals more about a business than a P/E.

## 32.2 Behavioural traps for the valuer

| Bias | How it appears | Counter-measure |
|------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Anchoring** | The first number (the asking price, the market price, last year's value) pulls the estimate toward it | Build the valuation independently before looking at the price; note the first estimate and the reasons for changes |
| **Confirmation** | Seek evidence supporting a favoured conclusion | Seek disconfirming evidence; assign a colleague to argue the other side |
| **Overconfidence** | Ranges too narrow; point estimates presented as precise | Widen ranges; backtest past valuations |
| **Optimism / planning fallacy** | Management plans, and valuers who adopt them, are too rosy | Compare plans with history; apply base rates |
| **Narrative bias** | A compelling story overrides numerical discipline | Convert the story to numbers and test each; identify what must be true |
| **Recency** | Extrapolating the last few years | Use full-cycle data; study base rates |
| **Herding** | Adopting the consensus discount rate or multiple without thought | Derive independently; explain differences |
| **Sunk-cost / commitment** | Defending a model because of effort invested | Review fresh; invite challenge |
| **Client pressure** | Drifting toward a desired answer | Pre-commit to assumptions and methods; disclose conflicts |

### 32.2.1 Base rates
Few companies sustain high growth for long. A forecast of 20% growth for ten years should be compared with how often companies of that size have achieved it. Historical studies of growth persistence consistently show strong *mean reversion*: high growth fades, and above-normal margins and returns attract competition. A DCF that assumes persistence must argue *why this firm is exceptional*, and should say so explicitly.

### 32.2.2 The outside view
Ask: "Among companies that look like this one, what happened?" Combine the inside view (the specific story) with the outside view (reference class). If the inside view says 25% margin but 90% of the reference class never exceeded 18%, require strong evidence.

## 32.3 When DCF is not the right primary tool

| Situation | Better primary tool | Why |
|-----------|---------------------|-----|
| Liquidation or run-off | Liquidation/asset value | No going concern |
| Holding companies and funds | NAV/SOTP | Value is in the underlying assets |
| Highly speculative early-stage with no model | Comparable rounds, VC method, scenario analysis | Cash-flow forecast arbitrary |
| Financial institutions | Excess-return/DDM | Enterprise cash flow undefined |
| Short-horizon trading or merger arbitrage | Market prices, deal terms | Intrinsic value less relevant |
| Real estate | Cap-rate and comparable sales | Market-evidence strong |
| Illiquid pooled assets | NAV with adjustments | |

In many of these, DCF still appears as a cross-check; the point is that it should not be the *only* anchor.

## 32.4 Where DCF is evolving

- **Data and automation.** Spreadsheet models are increasingly generated or supported by software and data providers; the risk is uncritical acceptance of mechanically produced numbers. The analyst's value lies in judgement: choosing the story, challenging assumptions.
- **Scenario and probabilistic thinking.** Wider use of simulations and probability-weighted outcomes, especially for climate and regulatory risks.
- **Intangibles.** Better ways of capitalising R&D, brands and data in invested capital and ROIC.
- **ESG and climate integration.** More explicit scenario modelling of carbon pricing, physical risk and transition capex (Chapter 23).
- **Standardisation of professional practice.** Valuation standards, registered-valuer regimes and reporting requirements push toward documented, reviewable models.

None of these change the principle: value is the present value of expected cash flows, adjusted for risk.

## 32.5 Habits of excellent valuers

1. **They ask "what must be true?"** for any value, and check whether it is plausible.
2. **They look at the base rate** before the story.
3. **They triangulate** — DCF, multiples, transactions, reverse DCF — and reconcile.
4. **They own their assumptions.** Every key input has a source, an owner and a date.
5. **They present ranges** and say which assumptions drive them.
6. **They are comfortable saying "I don't know"** for inputs that cannot be estimated, and show the sensitivity instead.
7. **They keep a record** of past valuations and review them against what happened.
8. **They separate the analysis from advocacy**: advisory roles may involve advocacy; valuation does not.
9. **They keep it simple where possible** — a model that nobody can follow is not a better model.
10. **They update** — risk-free rate, ERP and peers change; the same company is worth different amounts in different markets.

## 32.6 Closing summary of the book's argument

1. **Value is the present value of the cash a business will generate for its owners, discounted for time and risk.**
2. **Cash flow is operating profit after tax less reinvestment.** Growth must be paid for.
3. **Return on invested capital relative to the cost of capital determines whether growth creates value.**
4. **Consistency is the main discipline:** cash flow, discount rate, currency, claim and financing policy must match.
5. **Terminal value is where most of the value and most of the error live.**
6. **The bridge from enterprise to equity must be complete and non-duplicative.**
7. **Uncertainty should be shown, not hidden:** sensitivities, scenarios, reverse DCF.
8. **Different businesses need different adaptations,** but the same principles.
9. **Professional practice** requires documentation, independence and humility.

## Chapter 32 summary

- The standard criticisms of DCF are partly valid; the response is discipline and transparency, not abandonment.
- Behavioural biases (anchoring, optimism, narrative) are the practitioner's main enemies; counter them with base rates and independent challenge.
- DCF is not always the right primary tool; know when to lead with another method.
- Excellent valuation is judgement supported by consistent arithmetic.
