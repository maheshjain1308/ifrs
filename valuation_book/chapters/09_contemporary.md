# Chapter 9 — Contemporary Approaches: PER, Goodwill and Leveraged Buy-Outs

## 9.1 Why "other approaches" exist

As business became technology-driven, and management teams began to position themselves as "value creators" through inorganic growth and conglomerate-style structures, the concept of value and valuation underwent a paradigm shift. The "MNC" tag and inorganic growth gave rise to complex structures and tiers of management styles. Yet the game-plan stays the same: *separate the seed from the chaff* using the time-honoured asset-based or income-based approaches, or the more mature cash-flow approach, or a combination of all three. But in bull markets analysts liberally use terms like "PEs", "exit multiples", "LBOs" and "brand value".

Some of these concepts arose from the peculiarities of certain industries. An **internet company** has virtually zero fixed assets but a robust online presence and huge brand-recall value. That gave rise to metrics such as **price per page visited** or, for an online play store, **price per subscriber**. Yet discerning analysts still ask for the cash-to-sales ratio and apply a DCF before they "put the money in the pot".

## 9.2 Goodwill-based approach

Another contemporary approach is the **goodwill-based** valuation. A retail giant desperate to acquire a traditional mom-and-pop store in a particular hotspot (so as to secure the location) can reasonably adopt this method:

1. First take an **asset-based valuation** (net assets).
2. Then value the **goodwill separately** by linking a multiple to the store's **annual sales** or its **footfall**.

*Worked example 9.1.* A store with net assets of ₹2 crore, annual sales ₹6 crore, in a high-footfall location. The acquirer pays goodwill at 0.5× annual sales = ₹3 crore. Total price = 2 + 3 = **₹5 crore**. The goodwill represents the value of location and customer base, which the asset approach cannot capture. The key judgement is the multiple, which should be supported by comparable transactions or by the profit the location earns above a normal return.

## 9.3 Price Earning Ratio (PER)

The price-earning ratio **equates the EPS to the price prevailing on the stock market** — the logic being that the market prices the stock on its fundamentals, so you need not look beyond it to value the stock.

*Example from the source chapter:* EPS ₹40; average share price over the last quarter ₹50; PER = 50 ÷ 40 = **1.25**.

Note the source's observation that PER is a **relative figure**; comparison *across industries in the same sector* can give a more **median PER** that may be acceptable for valuation. (Conventionally P/E is price ÷ EPS and is typically a larger number, e.g. 10–30; the example is simply to illustrate the arithmetic.)

Where PER is used: to estimate value from earnings (price = EPS × PER), to compare companies, and to infer the market's growth expectations.

## 9.4 Leveraged Buy-Outs (LBOs)

The increasingly complex nature of commerce gave rise to a new class of "strategic investors": **Private Equity (PE) firms**. They scout for enterprises "in the rough", acquire them using a clever mix of **debt and equity** (typically **70:30 debt to equity**), and then target to **sell within a medium-term period, say 3 to 5 years**. In the process they leverage the debt and create value (both perceived and real) and then either spin off the management control to another entity for a price or go for an outright sale.

### 9.4.1 The mechanics
1. **Sources and uses.** Purchase price (+ fees) funded by debt (senior and subordinated) and sponsor equity.
2. **Operating forecast** — revenue, EBITDA, capex, working capital.
3. **Debt schedule** — interest and principal; cash flow after debt service used to repay debt.
4. **Exit.** Exit EV = EBITDA (or similar) × exit multiple; equity = EV − remaining debt.
5. **Returns** — sponsor's IRR and multiple of money.

### 9.4.2 The source chapter's LBO example

*X is a small software company providing a niche data-control and testing service, with 60 employees and some steady contracts, generating an EBITDA of ₹100 lakh per year.*

- A **venture capitalist (VC)** convinces the managing director to sell the **majority stake** to him, valued at a premium of **100% per share over the book value**, plus a **one-time goodwill payout of ₹50 lakh**, using an income-based valuation approach. Total consideration: **₹250 lakh**.
- The VC then ropes in a **banker to pump in ₹200 lakh** for the acquisition-cum-expansion as well as for brand marketing. The remaining **₹50 lakh** gap is the VC's contribution as promoter equity towards securities premium.
- Since the core operations team is not dismantled, the company easily achieves approximately **20% average growth** in each of the next 3 years.
- At the end of the third year, the VC puts the company on the "sale block" and attracts a leading MNC. The exit multiple is **7× EBDAT**.

**The hypothetical entity value (₹ lakh):**

| | Y₀ | Y₁ | Y₂ | Y₃ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| EBIDTA | 100.00 | 120.00 | 144.00 | 178.00 |
| Less: interest (on debt ₹200 lakh at 18%, repaid linearly over 6 years) | 36.00 | 30.00 | 24.00 | 18.00 |
| EBDTA | 64.00 | 90.00 | 120.00 | 160.00 |
| Less: taxes @ 30% | 19.20 | 27.00 | 36.00 | 48.00 |
| **EBDAT** | **44.80** | **63.00** | **84.00** | **112.00** |
| Multiple | | | | 7 |
| **Capitalised value at end of Y₃** | | | | **784** |
| Less: debt | | | | (100) |
| **Equity value** | | | | **684** |

*Check.* Debt outstanding at the end of year 3 is 200 − 3 × 33.33 = 100; interest falls as debt is repaid (18% of 200, 166.7, 133.3, 100 = 36, 30, 24, 18) ✔. *Note on Y₃ EBITDA:* 20% growth on 144 would give 172.8; the source table shows 178 and the downstream numbers (160, 48, 112, 784, 684) are built on 178. With 172.8, EBDAT would be (172.8 − 18) × 0.7 = 108.4 and equity value 7 × 108.4 − 100 = **₹658.5 lakh**. The conclusion is unchanged.

**Return to the VC.** The VC put in ₹50 lakh of equity (if we take the 250 consideration funded by ₹200 lakh bank and ₹50 lakh VC equity) and receives an equity value of ₹684 lakh in 3 years — a multiple of 13.7× and an IRR of about 139% a year (13.68^(1/3) − 1). Even allowing for sharing with management, the leverage effect is dramatic. (The book does not compute the IRR; the figures above are the natural implication.)

### 9.4.3 Where the value comes from
1. **Growth** in EBITDA (20% a year).
2. **Debt pay-down** from operating cash flow.
3. **Multiple**: here exit at 7× — what the entry multiple was matters (entry EV/EBITDA = 250/100 = 2.5×!). A jump from 2.5× to 7× is an enormous multiple expansion, the biggest part of the return; this is why the example should be read as an illustration of mechanics, not as an expected outcome.

### 9.4.4 The risk of an LBO
"One of the prime casualties in an LBO model is that the **future cannot be predicted with exactitude**. Thus, if at the end of the third year the industry is caught in a **cyclical slowdown**, the VC will find itself saddled with a huge loan and burgeoning interest costs that are difficult to recycle." High leverage magnifies both gains and losses.

**Risk factors:** cyclical downturn; interest-rate rise; covenant breach; failure to deliver growth; exit-market closure; key-person departure.

## 9.5 LBO as a valuation tool (ceiling price)

By reversing the model, a valuer can compute the **maximum price a financial buyer could pay** for a target IRR (say 20–25%). This gives a ceiling for a financial buyer; strategic buyers with synergies may pay more.

*Worked example 9.2.* Target EBITDA 200; sponsor wants 25% IRR over 5 years; exit at 7× EBITDA; debt capacity 4× EBITDA = 800 at 10%, with 60% of free cash flow after interest swept to debt reduction. If year-5 EBITDA is 300 and remaining debt 400, exit equity at 7× = 2,100 − 400 = 1,700. Required entry equity = 1,700 ÷ 1.25⁵ = 1,700 ÷ 3.0518 = **557**. Maximum entry EV = equity 557 + debt 800 = **1,357** → 6.8× EBITDA. Paying more than ~6.8× reduces the sponsor's IRR below target.

## 9.6 Choosing among approaches

| Situation | Contemporary tool | Caveat |
|-----------|-------------------|--------|
| Internet/e-commerce | Price per user/page/subscriber | Check cash-to-sales and DCF |
| Retail hotspot | Asset + goodwill by sales/footfall | Support the multiple |
| Listed comparables | PER | Relative, not absolute |
| PE acquisition | LBO | Sensitive to leverage and exit |

## Chapter 9 summary

- Contemporary approaches arise from industry peculiarities; they supplement, not replace, asset/income/cash-flow methods.
- Goodwill approach: asset value plus goodwill by sales/footfall multiple.
- PER equates EPS to price; relative figure.
- LBO: ~70:30 debt-equity, 3–5 year horizon; returns from growth, debt pay-down and exit multiple; major risk is cyclical downturn.
