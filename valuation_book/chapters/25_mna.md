# Chapter 25 — Valuation in Mergers and Acquisitions: Synergies, Price and Exchange Ratios

The source chapter introduces valuation primarily for **mergers and acquisitions**, "the next topic for discussion". This chapter shows how the methods of the book are used in a deal: the standalone value of the target, the value of synergies, the maximum price a buyer should pay, the effect on the buyer's earnings per share, and the exchange ratio in a share-swap.

## 25.1 Three values in every deal

1. **Standalone value** of the target — what it is worth under its current owners and strategy (asset, income, DCF).
2. **Value to the acquirer** = Standalone value + PV of synergies − integration and transaction costs.
3. **Price** — the negotiated amount, somewhere between the seller's minimum and the buyer's maximum (Chapter 13).

**Value created for the buyer = Value to the acquirer − Price paid.**
**Value gained by the seller = Price paid − Standalone value.**

If the buyer pays the entire synergy value to the seller, it earns just its cost of capital on the deal.

## 25.2 Sources of synergy

| Type | Examples | Reliability |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Cost synergies** | Overhead consolidation, procurement scale, plant rationalisation, logistics | High |
| **Revenue synergies** | Cross-selling, wider distribution, new products | Low |
| **Financial synergies** | Lower cost of debt, unused tax losses, higher debt capacity | Medium |
| **Operating improvements** | Better working-capital and capex management | Medium |
| **Dis-synergies** | Customer loss, talent attrition, culture clash | Often underestimated |

In the source chapter's wording, "if any additional earning is there due to acquisition, the same should also be considered in valuation" (Chapter 4) — this is the synergy.

## 25.3 Valuing synergies

Treat synergy as a project: forecast the incremental after-tax cash flows, subtract costs to achieve them, and discount at a rate that reflects their risk (usually the target's WACC plus a premium for execution risk).

### 25.3.1 Worked example 25.1
*Pioneer Ltd* considers acquiring *Target Ltd*, whose **standalone equity value is ₹800 crore** (10 crore shares, ₹80 per share).

- Cost synergies: **₹40 crore a year pre-tax at run-rate**, reached 50% in year 1 and 100% from year 2.
- One-off integration cost ₹30 crore in year 1.
- Tax 25%; discount rate 12.5%; after year 5 the synergy grows 3% a year.

| ₹ crore | Y1 | Y2 | Y3 | Y4 | Y5 |
|---------|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Pre-tax synergy (40 × ramp) | 20 | 40 | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| Integration cost | (30) | – | – | – | – |
| Net pre-tax | (10) | 40 | 40 | 40 | 40 |
| After tax (× 0.75) | (7.5) | 30 | 30 | 30 | 30 |

PV of years 1–5 at 12.5% = **73.5**. Terminal value at year 5 = 30 × 1.03 ÷ (0.125 − 0.03) = **325.3**; PV = **180.5**. **Total PV of synergies = ₹254.0 crore, or ₹25.4 per Target share.**

**Maximum price** for Pioneer = standalone ₹80 + synergies ₹25.4 = **₹105.4 per share**. Paying more than that destroys Pioneer's value.

**Negotiation.** Suppose Pioneer offers **₹95 per share** (₹950 crore): the premium over standalone value is 18.75%; Target's shareholders gain ₹15 per share (₹150 crore) over standalone value; Pioneer gains 105.4 − 95 = ₹10.4 per share (₹104 crore) — about 41% of the synergy.

## 25.4 Valuing the target by the book's methods

| Method | Use in an acquisition |
|--------|-----------------------|
| Net realisable value | Seller's floor; relevant if assets will be sold |
| Replaceable value | Buyer's ceiling — rebuilding is the alternative |
| Earnings capitalisation/P/E | Benchmark for acquisition multiple |
| DCF | Standalone and with synergies |
| EVA/MVA | Whether the target creates value for its capital |
| Relative (EV/EBITDA, precedent transactions) | Market check; includes control premia paid in other deals |
| Chop-shop | If the buyer will separate businesses |

## 25.5 Earnings per share: accretion and dilution

Boards also look at the effect on **EPS**. It is a market-optics test, not a value test: a deal can be EPS-accretive and value-destroying, or EPS-dilutive and value-creating.

### 25.5.1 Worked example 25.2
Pioneer: net income ₹600 crore; 100 crore shares; EPS ₹6.00; P/E 20× → price ₹120. Target: net income ₹70 crore. Offer: ₹950 crore (₹95 per share; P/E paid = 950 ÷ 70 = 13.6×).

| Financing | Combined net income | Shares | EPS | Change |
|-----------|--------------------:|-------:|----:|-------:|
| **All cash** (debt at 9%; after-tax interest 950 × 0.09 × 0.75 = ₹64.1 crore) | 600 + 70 − 64.1 = 605.9 | 100 | ₹6.06 | **+1.0%** |
| **All stock** (950 ÷ 120 = 7.92 crore new shares) | 670 | 107.92 | ₹6.21 | **+3.5%** |
| All cash, with ₹30 crore post-tax synergy | 635.9 | 100 | ₹6.36 | **+6.0%** |

Both are accretive because Pioneer pays a lower P/E (13.6×) than its own (20×). The stock deal is more accretive because the "cost" of equity issued at a P/E of 20 (earnings yield 5%) is lower than the after-tax cost of debt (6.75%). Accretion says nothing about whether the price is justified by value (₹95 < ₹105.4: yes in this example).

## 25.6 The exchange ratio in a share-swap merger

In a merger where the acquirer pays in its own shares, the **exchange ratio** is the number of acquirer shares given for each target share.

**Exchange ratio = Value per share of target ÷ Value per share of acquirer**

Both values must be measured **on the same basis** (the same methods and weights). The syllabus questions (Chapter 21, Q4 and Q5) show the method: compute each company's value per share by net assets and by earnings capitalisation, weight them (1:3), and divide.

### 25.6.1 Worked example 25.3
Using Chapter 22's problem P8: A: NAV ₹120, earnings ₹200; B: NAV ₹45, earnings ₹70; weights 1 : 2 → A = ₹173.33; B = ₹61.67; **ratio 0.3558**. A holder of 1,000 B shares receives 355.8 A shares.

### 25.6.2 Fairness of the ratio
The ratio is fair if each group's shareholders receive value equal to what they give up, measured by consistent valuation. Tests: (i) compare the ratio with the market-price ratio; (ii) compare relative contributions to combined earnings, assets and cash flow; (iii) check the pro-forma ownership of the merged entity against each side's contribution.

**Pro-forma ownership.** If A has 100 crore shares and B 10 crore, at 0.3558 B's holders receive 3.558 crore A shares: **3.4%** of the merged company (3.558 ÷ 103.558). If B contributes 5% of combined earnings, the ratio looks low for B's holders — a prompt to re-examine the weights.

## 25.7 Cash, stock or mixed consideration

| Consideration | Seller's risk | Buyer's position |
|---------------|---------------|------------------|
| **Cash** | None after closing | Keeps all synergy upside; needs financing; leverage rises |
| **Stock** | Shares post-merger risk and synergy | Conserves cash; shares gains and risks; dilution |
| **Mixed / earn-out** | Partial | Contingent payments align incentives; need valuation of contingent claim |

## 25.8 Other deal valuation issues

1. **Control premium:** the extra paid over the unaffected price for control; typically comes from synergies and better management; observed premiums range widely.
2. **Contingent liabilities** (as in H Ltd. — Q4): deducted in net assets; assume full materialisation unless stated.
3. **Partly paid shares** (as in XY Ltd. — Q5): allocate consideration on fully-paid equivalents.
4. **Valuation date and subsequent events.**
5. **Tax and legal structure:** share vs asset purchase; merger relief; stamp duty.
6. **Purchase price allocation** after the deal (Ind AS 103): price allocated to identifiable assets and goodwill (Chapter 27).

## 25.9 Checklist for the buyer

1. Standalone value by two or three methods.
2. Synergies itemised, owned, costed, risk-weighted.
3. Walk-away price = standalone + a share of risk-weighted synergies.
4. Sensitivity of value to synergy delivery and WACC.
5. Funding, leverage, covenants.
6. EPS effect as a secondary test.
7. Integration plan with accountability.

## Chapter 25 summary

- Deal analysis separates standalone value, synergies and price.
- Synergies are valued as a project; example: ₹254 crore (₹25.4 per share); maximum price ₹105.4.
- EPS accretion is an optical test; accretive deals can destroy value.
- Exchange ratio = target value per share ÷ acquirer value per share on a consistent basis.

## Check your understanding

1. Define standalone value and value to the acquirer.
2. Why are revenue synergies riskier than cost synergies?
3. Compute the exchange ratio: target ₹90, acquirer ₹360.
4. If a buyer pays the full synergy value to the seller, what return does it earn?
5. Why can an EPS-accretive deal still destroy value?

**Answers.** (1) What the target is worth under present ownership; standalone plus PV of synergies less costs. (2) They depend on customers and competitors; cost savings are within management's control. (3) 90 ÷ 360 = **0.25**. (4) Exactly its cost of capital (zero NPV). (5) Accretion can result from cheap financing or a high acquirer P/E regardless of whether the price exceeds value.
