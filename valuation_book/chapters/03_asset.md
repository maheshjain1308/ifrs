# Chapter 3 — The Asset-Based Approach

## 3.1 The idea

The simplest way to value a business is to ask what its **assets** are worth and subtract what it **owes**. Under the asset-based approach, the value of the shares of a target company is computed in terms of the **net assets acquired**. The method is not based on income generation; it is based on income-*generating assets*.

Its natural users are holding companies, real-estate and investment firms, asset-heavy manufacturers, companies in liquidation, and acquirers who intend to break up and sell parts of a business. It is **least important for IT and knowledge-based companies**, where "hard" assets matter little because the real assets are intellectual property rights and human resources.

The approach has three variants: **net asset value** (book value), **net realisable value** (liquidation value, or adjusted book value) and **replaceable value**.

## 3.2 Net asset value (book value) method

**Value of the firm (as per the source chapter) = Fixed assets + Net current assets − Long-term debt**

(The source writes "Net Fixed Asset = Fixed Assets + Net Current Assets − Long Term Debt"; this is the **net assets / net worth** attributable to shareholders.)

**Advantage.** It is the simplest method, using historical costs that are easily available.

**Limitations.**
1. A **balance sheet is not a valuation device**. Historical cost less depreciation has little relevance to current worth.
2. It ignores current values of assets, **even for intangibles** such as brand and intellectual property.
3. Therefore it offers only a **lower limit** to the value of shares of the target.

*Worked example 3.1.* Fixed assets ₹600, net current assets ₹250, long-term debt ₹300 (all in crore). Net asset value = 600 + 250 − 300 = **₹550 crore**. With 10 crore shares: ₹55 per share.

### Adjusted book value
Often the starting point is improved by (i) removing fictitious assets (preliminary expenses, accumulated losses), (ii) adding unrecorded assets and (iii) deducting unrecorded liabilities. This produces an *adjusted* NAV that still uses book values for the ordinary items.

## 3.3 Net realisable value (NRV)

Also called **liquidation value** or **adjusted book value**, NRV is the realisable value of all assets after deducting liquidation expenses and paying off liabilities. In some cases, where the target is a going concern, liquidation expenses can be ignored.

Although it looks simple, computing NRV can be harder than it seems: a buyer in an off-market (distress) purchase is likely to offer the lowest prices. The method is **not popular** because it involves a **total break-up** of the target. It is generally useful where the acquirer wants to **sell one part of the business and integrate the remaining part** with its existing operations.

### 3.3.1 The source chapter's NRV example

The realisable values differ from book values as follows:

| | Book values (₹) | Net realisable values (₹) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Long-term debt (term loan from ZB Bank) | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Current liabilities | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| **Total liabilities (A)** | **20,000** | **20,000** |
| **Non-current assets (B)** | | |
| PPE | 50,000 | 40,000 |
| Licences | 10,000 | 30,000 |
| | 60,000 | 70,000 |
| **Current assets (C)** | | |
| Sundry debtors | 50,000 | 45,000 |
| Cash | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| | 60,000 | 55,000 |
| **Net assets (B) + (C) − (A)** | **1,00,000** | **1,05,000** |

Thus the net realisable value of ₹1,05,000 exceeds the book net worth of ₹1,00,000 because the licences are worth far more than their cost, outweighing the shortfalls on plant and debtors. If there are **5,000 equity shares**, value per share = 1,05,000 ÷ 5,000 = **₹21** (versus ₹20 on book value).

*Lesson.* Realisable values can be higher or lower than book values; the direction depends on the assets.

### 3.3.2 Realisation percentages: a practical guide

| Asset | Typical recovery as a % of book value |
|-------|---------------------------------------|
| Cash and bank | 100% |
| Quoted investments | Market value (less tax and costs) |
| Debtors | 60–95% depending on age and quality |
| Inventory | 40–80% (raw materials higher; work-in-progress and specialised stock lower) |
| Land and buildings | At market value (may exceed book substantially) |
| Plant and machinery | 20–60% in a forced sale; higher in an orderly sale |
| Licences/IP | Depends on transferability; can exceed book if internally generated and unrecorded |
| Goodwill | Usually nil in liquidation |

## 3.4 Replaceable value

This method values the business by the **cost to acquire a group of equivalent assets and liabilities in the open market**. It has an advantage over book value because it takes into account **proper current valuation**, and it is generally **slightly higher than net realisable value**, because quick asset disposal is not encouraged. For this reason many authors believe it is the **maximum price an acquirer would pay** for an equivalent business — since it could rebuild or buy the equivalent assets instead.

Limitation: only hard assets are taken into consideration; the **loyalty of staff**, relationships and organisational know-how cannot be replaced by buying equipment.

### The three values compared

For a firm with net assets (book) 550:

| Basis | Net assets | Per share (10 crore shares) |
|-------|-----------:|---------------------------:|
| Net asset value (book) | 550 | 55 |
| Net realisable value (break-up) | 680 | 68 |
| Replaceable value (rebuild) | 790 | 79 |

The ordering *book ≤ realisable ≤ replacement* is typical for an asset-heavy firm with undervalued land, though it is not a law.

## 3.5 When is the asset approach appropriate?

| Situation | Reason |
|-----------|--------|
| Liquidation or break-up planned | Going concern value is irrelevant |
| Holding/investment company | Value is the underlying assets (add a holding-company discount for tax/overhead leakage) |
| Real-estate and land-rich firms | Market values of land are the main value |
| Loss-making firm with no earning power | Assets are worth more than earnings |
| Floor value in negotiations | No rational seller accepts less than liquidation value |
| Companies in the early years with no earnings | A cost-based indication |

## 3.6 Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths:** objective; based on observable values; straightforward; provides a floor; useful for cross-checks.

**Weaknesses:** (i) *ignores earning power* — two firms with the same net assets but different profitability get the same value; (ii) ignores **goodwill** and unrecorded intangibles; (iii) uses historical cost (in the book-value variant); (iv) liquidation value ignores the going-concern premium; (v) requires valuation of each asset (time-consuming).

**Conclusion (source chapter).** The asset-based approach can depict the enterprise's net worth fairly correctly using the fundamental principle of **going concern**. However, it suffers from a major drawback: **it fails to consider the ability of the enterprise to generate future revenues and how market dynamics will affect future operations and cash flows.** That is the gap the income and cash-flow approaches fill.

## 3.7 Worked example: break-up of a business

*Valmiki Engineering Ltd* has these book values (₹ lakh): land 400, building 250, plant 600, inventory 300, debtors 350, cash 50, total 1,950. Liabilities: bank loan 700, creditors 250, other 50 (total 1,000). Estimated realisable values: land 640, building 200, plant 330, inventory 195, debtors 280, cash 50; liquidation costs 60.

- Realisable assets: 640 + 200 + 330 + 195 + 280 + 50 = **1,695**
- Less liquidation costs: 60 → 1,635
- Less liabilities: 1,000 → **NRV to equity = ₹635 lakh**

Book equity = 1,950 − 1,000 = 950. The NRV is 33% lower because plant and stocks fetch much less than book, while land fetches more. An acquirer who values Valmiki as a *going concern* by earnings would compare that figure with this floor of 635.

## 3.8 Pitfalls

1. **Deducting liabilities twice or omitting contingent liabilities** (litigation, guarantees).
2. **Ignoring tax** on gains when assets are sold at above book value.
3. **Mixing going-concern and liquidation values** within one calculation.
4. **Double-counting** revaluation reserves.
5. **Ignoring unrecorded intangibles** (brands, licences) in the book-value variant — as the NRV example shows, licences can be worth three times book.

## Chapter 3 summary

- Net assets = assets − liabilities, valued on book (NAV), realisable (NRV) or replacement basis.
- Book value is a lower limit; replaceable value is often the upper limit an acquirer would pay.
- NRV matters for break-up; it is not popular because it implies total dismantling.
- The approach ignores future earning power — its main limitation.
