# Chapter 5 — The Cash-Flow-Based (DCF) Approach

## 5.1 From profit to cash

Profit is an accounting concept; owners are paid in **cash**. As opposed to the asset-based and income-based approaches, the cash-flow approach takes into account the **quantum of free cash** that is available in future periods and **discounts** it appropriately to match the **risk** of the flow. If the present value arrived at after applying the discount rate is more than the current cost of investment, the valuation of the enterprise is attractive to stakeholders as well as to externally interested parties such as stock analysts.

The DCF approach attempts to overcome the problem of **over-reliance on historical data** seen in the previous two methods. It is forward-looking and focuses on cash, not accruals.

## 5.2 The five steps

According to the source chapter there are essentially five steps in DCF-based valuation:

| Step | Task |
|------|------|
| (a) | Arrive at the **free cash flows** |
| (b) | **Forecast** future cash flows (projected free cash flows) |
| (c) | Determine the **discount rate** based on the cost of capital |
| (d) | Find the **terminal value (TV)** of the enterprise |
| (e) | Find the **present values** of both the free cash flows and the TV, and interpret the results |

## 5.3 Free cash flow

**Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF)** is the cash generated by operations available to all providers of capital (equity and debt):

**FCFF = EBIT × (1 − t) + Depreciation − Capital expenditure − Increase in working capital**

Alternatively, **FCFF = Profit after tax + Depreciation + One-off expenses − One-off incomes (+ after-tax interest if starting from net income) − Capex − Increase in working capital**.

**Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)** adds net borrowing and deducts interest:

**FCFE = Net income + Depreciation − Capex − Increase in working capital + Net borrowing**

FCFF is discounted at **WACC**; FCFE at the **cost of equity**.

### 5.3.1 Eliminating one-time items
In the source chapter's illustration the starting point is earnings after tax (EAT), from which **one-time incomes are eliminated** (they will not recur) and **one-time expenses are added back**, and **depreciation is added back** because it is a book entry (non-cash). A good practice, in line with Chapter 19 (normalisation), is to start from normalised operating profit.

## 5.4 The source chapter's DCF illustration, re-derived

**Step (a): free cash flow for the base year (₹ thousand)**

| Item | 2016-17 | Remark |
|------|--------:|--------|
| EAT | 600 | |

| Add: one-time expenses | 100 | one-time events eliminated |
| Add: depreciation | 100 | book entry (non-cash) |
| **Free cash flow** | **600** | |

**Step (b): projections.** Free cash flow estimated to grow at 5% a year; suitable assumptions are made for changes in working capital and investments in fixed assets:

| | 2017-18 | 2018-19 | 2019-20 |
|---|--------:|--------:|--------:|
| Free cash flow (5% growth) | 600.00 | 630.00 | 661.50 |
| Less: changes in working capital | (50.00) | (30.00) | 10.00 |
| Less: investment in fixed assets | (50.00) | (50.00) | (20.00) |
| **Adjusted free cash flow** | **500.00** | **550.00** | **651.50** |

**Step (c): discounting at WACC 8%.** Present-value factors 0.926, 0.857, 0.794:

| | 2017-18 | 2018-19 | 2019-20 |
|---|--------:|--------:|--------:|
| Adjusted FCF | 500.00 | 550.00 | 651.50 |
| PVF @ 8% | 0.926 | 0.857 | 0.794 |
| **PV** | **463.00** | **471.35** | **517.29** |

Sum of PVs = **₹1,451.64 thousand**.

**Step (d): terminal value.** Perpetual growth after year 3 is assumed at 3%. The source chapter writes TV = (CF at year 3 × growth) ÷ (WACC − growth) = (517.29 × 1.03) ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = **10,656.17**.

**Step (e): total.** The source chapter then adds this TV to the PV of cash flows: **Total DCF of enterprise = 12,107.81 thousand** — "the value of the enterprise for a potential acquisition is approximately 12,108 thousand".

### 5.4.1 A careful reading of the illustration

The line labelled "TV" in the source (10,656.17) deserves a careful reading. It is computed as (517.29 × 1.03) ÷ (0.08 − 0.03), where 517.29 is the **present value of the year-3 cash flow** (651.50 × 0.794). It is therefore *already* the **present value** of the terminal value, and adding it to the PV of the explicit cash flows is correct. The longer way:

- TV at the end of year 3 = 651.50 × 1.03 ÷ (0.08 − 0.03) = **13,420.9**
- PV of TV = 13,420.9 × 0.794 = **10,656.2** (the same figure as the source's "TV")
- Enterprise value = 1,451.64 + 10,656.2 = **12,107.8** ✔ (with unrounded factors: 12,105.6)

So the source's answer of ₹12,107.81 thousand is right. What a student must not do is the *mixed* version — apply the growth formula to the **un-discounted** cash flow (651.50 × 1.03 ÷ 0.05 = 13,420.9) and then **add it without discounting**; that would overstate value by about 23%. When in doubt, show the terminal value *at the end of year 3* and its *present value* on separate lines.

> **Key idea.** Whatever the presentation, the correct enterprise value equals PV of explicit flows + PV of TV, and TV-at-horizon must be discounted back by n years.

## 5.5 The weakness: WACC and TV

"The DCF is indeed a revolutionary model for valuation as FCFs truly represent the intrinsic value of an entity. However, the whole calculation gravitates heavily on the **WACC** and the **TV**. In fact in many cases the TV is found to be a significant portion in the final value arrived by DCF. This means that the growth rate and underlying assumptions need to be thoroughly validated to deny any room for margin of error of judgment."

In the illustration: PV of explicit flows = 1,451.64; PV of TV = 10,656.17; **TV share = 88%**.

### Sensitivity to the discount rate and growth (illustration)

Using the consistent formula on the same cash flows:

| WACC \ g | 2% | 3% | 4% |
|---------|---:|---:|---:|
| 7% | 12,329 | 15,174 | 19,916 |
| **8%** | 10,244 | **12,106** | 14,898 |
| 9% | 8,755 | 10,061 | 11,889 |

(₹ thousand, enterprise value; exact discounting at (1 + WACC)ⁿ, TV from year-3 cash flow of 651.5.) A one-point change in WACC moves value by roughly −17% / +25% (8% → 9% / 7%). This sensitivity is the central reason to present DCF values as ranges.

## 5.6 Deriving WACC

**WACC = Ke × E/(D + E) + Kd × (1 − t) × D/(D + E)**

where Ke is the cost of equity (Chapter 6), Kd the pre-tax cost of debt, t the tax rate, and E and D market values of equity and debt. In Chapter 7 we see how to compute Ke for a private firm or a new business using the un-levered (asset) beta.

## 5.7 Equity value and per-share value

DCF of FCFF gives **enterprise value**. To reach the value for shareholders:

**Equity value = Enterprise value − Debt (and debt-like items) − Preference capital + Cash and non-operating assets**

**Value per share = Equity value ÷ Number of equity shares**

*Worked example 5.1.* Enterprise value ₹1,478 crore; debt ₹300 crore; surplus cash and investments ₹40 crore; 10 crore shares. Equity = 1,478 − 300 + 40 = ₹1,218 crore → ₹121.8 per share.

## 5.8 Two-stage and multi-stage DCF

Companies rarely grow at one rate forever. A **two-stage model** uses a high growth rate for n years, then a stable rate. The source chapter's practical questions (the XYZ Ltd. and WXY Ltd. questions) use exactly this approach, with a different WACC in the high-growth and stable phases because the beta and capital structure change. Chapter 21 solves both.

### Skeleton
1. Forecast FCFF for each year in the high-growth period (revenue → EBIT → NOPAT → reinvestment).
2. Discount at the high-growth WACC.
3. Compute the terminal-year FCFF with **stable-growth assumptions** (capex offset by depreciation; working-capital growth at stable rate).
4. TV = FCFF(n+1) ÷ (WACC_stable − g).
5. Discount TV by the cumulative factor for n years.

## 5.9 Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths:** based on cash; forward-looking; explicit assumptions; theoretically sound intrinsic value; flexible (scenarios).

**Weaknesses:** sensitivity to WACC and TV; forecasting difficulty; false precision; inapplicable (without adaptation) to start-ups with no cash flow history, distressed firms and financial institutions.

## 5.10 Checklist for a defensible DCF

1. Cash flows normalised for one-offs, with working capital and capex tied to growth.
2. Discount rate consistent with risk, currency and capital structure.
3. Terminal growth ≤ long-run growth of the economy; terminal reinvestment consistent with growth.
4. TV share of value disclosed.
5. Bridge to equity complete (debt, cash, minorities, options).
6. Sensitivity table on WACC and g; cross-check against multiples.

## Chapter 5 summary

- DCF = PV of free cash flows + PV of terminal value.
- Five steps: FCF → forecast → discount rate → TV → PV and interpret.
- The source illustration gives ₹12,107.81 thousand; its "TV" line of 10,656.17 is already a present value (TV at year 3 is 13,420.9).
- Value hinges on WACC and TV; the TV share is often very large.
- Equity value requires the bridge from enterprise value.
