# Chapter 10 — The Chop-Shop (Break-Up Value) Method

## 10.1 The idea

The chop-shop method is named after the practice of buying a car and selling it in parts, because the parts together fetch more than the whole. In business: the approach attempts to identify **multi-industry companies that are undervalued** and that would have more value if **separated from each other**. In other words, an attempt is made to **buy assets below their replacement value**. It is also called the **break-up value** or **sum-of-the-parts** approach.

It suits conglomerates whose segments belong to different industries, where the market applies a blended multiple that fits none of the segments well — the **conglomerate discount**.

## 10.2 The three steps

| Step | Task |
|------|------|
| **1** | Identify the firm's various **business segments** and calculate the **average capitalisation ratios** for firms (pure-play) in those industries |
| **2** | Calculate a **"theoretical" market value** based upon each of the average capitalisation ratios |
| **3** | **Average** the "theoretical" market values to determine the **"chop-shop" value** of the firm |

*Capitalisation ratios* are ratios of market capitalisation (or value) to a financial metric: **capitalisation/sales**, **capitalisation/assets**, **capitalisation/operating income**. Each ratio is taken from *pure-play* firms in the segment's industry.

## 10.3 Illustration 4 — Cornett GmbH

*Using the chop-shop (or break-up value) approach, assign a value for Cornett GmbH, whose stock is currently trading at a total market price of **€4 million**. For Cornett, the accounting data are set out in three business segments: consumer wholesaling, specialty services and assorted centres.*

**Data for the firm's segments**

| Business segment | Segment sales | Segment assets | Segment income |
|------------------|--------------:|---------------:|---------------:|
| Consumer wholesaling | €1,500,000 | €750,000 | €100,000 |
| Specialty services | €800,000 | €700,000 | €150,000 |
| Assorted centres | €2,000,000 | €3,000,000 | €600,000 |

**Industry data for pure-play firms**

| Business segment | Capitalisation/sales | Capitalisation/assets | Capitalisation/operating income |
|------------------|---------------------:|----------------------:|-------------------------------:|
| Consumer wholesaling | 0.75 | 0.60 | 10.00 |
| Specialty services | 1.10 | 0.90 | 7.00 |
| Assorted centres | 1.00 | 0.60 | 6.00 |

### Solution

**(1) Using the capitalisation/sales ratio**

| Segment | Ratio | Segment sales | Theoretical value |
|---------|------:|--------------:|------------------:|
| Consumer wholesaling | 0.75 | €1,500,000 | €1,125,000 |
| Specialty services | 1.10 | €800,000 | €880,000 |
| Assorted centres | 1.00 | €2,000,000 | €2,000,000 |
| **Total** | | | **€4,005,000** |

**(2) Using the capitalisation/assets ratio**

| Segment | Ratio | Segment assets | Theoretical value |
|---------|------:|---------------:|------------------:|
| Consumer wholesaling | 0.60 | €750,000 | €450,000 |
| Specialty services | 0.90 | €700,000 | €630,000 |
| Assorted centres | 0.60 | €3,000,000 | €1,800,000 |
| **Total** | | | **€2,880,000** |

**(3) Using the capitalisation/operating income ratio**

| Segment | Ratio | Segment income | Theoretical value |
|---------|------:|---------------:|------------------:|
| Consumer wholesaling | 10.00 | €100,000 | €1,000,000 |
| Specialty services | 7.00 | €150,000 | €1,050,000 |
| Assorted centres | 6.00 | €600,000 | €3,600,000 |
| **Total** | | | **€5,650,000** |

**Average theoretical value** = (4,005,000 + 2,880,000 + 5,650,000) ÷ 3 = 12,535,000 ÷ 3 = **€4,178,333 ≈ €4,178,000**.

### Interpretation
The chop-shop value (€4.178 million) is slightly above the current market price (€4 million): the firm is **modestly undervalued** (by about 4.5%) on a break-up basis. The wide dispersion of the three estimates (€2.88 m on assets, €4.0 m on sales, €5.65 m on income) is itself informative: the **asset-based** figure is low because the assorted centres use a lot of assets; the **income-based** figure is high because they earn high income relative to sales. Averaging blends these views.

## 10.4 Variations and refinements

1. **Weighted average.** If one ratio is more reliable for a segment (e.g., income for stable businesses), weight it more.
2. **Use EV multiples** (EV/EBITDA) instead of equity-level ratios, then bridge to equity.
3. **Deduct unallocated corporate costs** (head-office) capitalised at an appropriate multiple.
4. **Adjust for taxes** on the sale of segments and for transaction costs.
5. **Adjust for intra-group transactions** and shared assets.
6. **Add non-core assets** (investments, surplus land) at market value, and **deduct net debt**.
7. **Use segment-specific DCFs** rather than ratios where cash flows are forecastable.

## 10.5 Worked example: sum-of-the-parts for a holding group

*Bhavani Industries* has three businesses (₹ crore):

| Segment | EBITDA | Peer EV/EBITDA | Segment EV |
|---------|-------:|---------------:|-----------:|
| Cement | 300 | 8.0× | 2,400 |
| Packaging | 120 | 10.0× | 1,200 |
| Hotels | 80 | 12.0× | 960 |
| **Total** | **500** | | **4,560** |

- Less: capitalised unallocated corporate costs (₹20 crore at 8×): (160)
- **Group EV (SOTP)** = 4,400
- Less: net debt (900) → **Equity value = 3,500**.

If the market capitalisation is ₹2,800 crore, the market is applying a **20% conglomerate discount** (1 − 2,800/3,500). A buyer who could unlock value by separating (or by improving capital allocation) sees the opportunity; but the discount may reflect governance and tax leakage costs that a break-up would not necessarily remove.

## 10.6 When does chop-shop make sense?

| Favourable | Unfavourable |
|------------|--------------|
| Diversified group with distinct segments | Integrated business with shared assets and synergies |
| Good segment disclosure (Ind AS 108) | Poor segment data |
| Market values the group at a discount | Group trades at a premium (market sees synergy) |
| Buyer plans to sell parts | Buyer plans to run as one |
| Clear pure-play comparables | Segments with no peers |

## 10.7 Link to asset-based valuation

The chop-shop approach complements the asset approach (Chapter 3): both consider the entity as separable parts. Where the asset approach values the *assets* separately, chop-shop values the *businesses* separately on market ratios. Replaceable value (Chapter 3) and chop-shop both aim at "buying assets below their replacement value".

## 10.8 Limitations

1. **Peers**: finding pure-play peers for each segment.
2. **Segment data**: allocation of common costs and assets is arbitrary.
3. **Synergies lost**: break-up may destroy synergy and incur separation costs, taxes and dis-synergies.
4. **Averaging** of ratios blends methods with different reliability.
5. **Not a prediction**: the break-up value says what the parts are worth *if* separated, not that separation will occur.

## Chapter 10 summary

- Chop-shop = break-up/sum-of-the-parts value: segment ratios from pure-play firms applied to segment metrics and then averaged.
- Cornett GmbH: values €4.005 m (sales), €2.88 m (assets), €5.65 m (income); average **€4.178 m** vs market €4 m.
- Useful for conglomerates trading at a discount; limited by comparability and separation costs.
