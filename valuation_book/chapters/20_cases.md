# Chapter 20 — Case Studies

Cases show how valuation behaves in the real world, where data are incomplete, methods disagree and the market reacts. This chapter analyses the two real-life case studies in the syllabus — the **Vodafone–Idea merger** and **Facebook's acquisition of WhatsApp** — and adds three constructed teaching cases.

## 20.1 Case study 1 — Vodafone and Idea Cellular: valuation in a merger

### 20.1.1 The facts (as in the source chapter)
Valuation was applied in the context of the **merger of Vodafone's Indian operations with Idea Cellular Ltd.** The valuation methods used by the appointed chartered-accountant firms were:

| # | Method | What was done |
|---|--------|---------------|
| (a) | **Market value method** | The share price observed on the NSE over a suitable time frame was considered |
| (b) | **Comparable companies' market multiple method** | Stock-market valuations of comparable companies on the BSE and NSE were considered |
| (c) | **NAV method** | The asset-based approach, to arrive at the net asset value of the merging entities as of **31 December 2016** |

**Surprisingly, the DCF method was not used.** The reason stated: the managements of both Vodafone and Idea had **not provided the projected (future) cash flows** and other parameters necessary for DCF. The final valuation using methods (a)–(c) provided a basis for a merger on a **share-exchange** basis. (Information from the valuation report filed by Idea Cellular with NSE.)

### 20.1.2 The market's reaction
A business newspaper reported that **Idea Cellular slumped 9.6%** as traders said the implied deal price in the planned merger **under-valued the company's shares**. Although traders had initially reacted positively, doubts about Idea's valuation after the merger sent shares downward. **Idea fell as much as 14.57%**, reversing earlier gains of **14.25%**, after announcing it would merge with Vodafone's Indian operations.

### 20.1.3 Lessons
1. **Valuation methods, though technically correct, may not elicit a positive reaction.** There is a **"perceived value"** that is not quantifiable and depends on analyst interpretation, majority opinion and other factors.
2. **Method choice is constrained by data.** Without management forecasts, DCF could not be done; the valuers relied on market, comparable and asset approaches. Listed targets with liquid shares lend themselves to market-based methods.
3. **For a share-exchange merger the ratio matters more than the absolute value.** What matters is each company's value *relative* to the other, measured consistently.
4. **Information asymmetry and signalling:** initial positive reaction followed by a drop shows that the market took time to digest the exchange terms.

### 20.1.4 How an exchange ratio is derived (illustration)
Suppose the valuers' conclusions (hypothetical numbers) per share are:

| Method | Company A (acquirer) | Company B (target) | Weight |
|--------|---------------------:|-------------------:|-------:|
| Market (volume-weighted price) | ₹ 100 | ₹ 40 | 50% |
| Comparable multiples | ₹ 110 | ₹ 46 | 25% |
| NAV | ₹ 60 | ₹ 30 | 25% |
| **Weighted value** | ₹ 92.5 | ₹ 39.0 | |

Exchange ratio = 39.0 ÷ 92.5 = **0.42** shares of A per share of B. Compare this with the market-only ratio (40 ÷ 100 = 0.40): the weighting toward multiples and NAV favours B's shareholders. If B's shareholders and the market believe B is worth more, the ratio will be challenged — as in the case.

## 20.2 Case study 2 — Facebook's acquisition of WhatsApp

### 20.2.1 The facts (as in the source chapter)
Facebook announced the takeover of **WhatsApp for a staggering USD 21.8 billion** (2015 in the source). The key characteristics of WhatsApp that influenced the deal were:

(a) A **free text-messaging service** with a **$1 per year service fee**; it had **450 million users** worldwide close to the valuation date.
(b) **70%** of the users were **active users**.
(c) An aggressive rate of user account increase of **1 million users a day** would lead to a pipeline of **1 billion users** within a year's range.

The **gross per-user value** would come to an average of **USD 55**, which included a **USD 4 billion payout as a sweetener for retaining WhatsApp employees** post takeover. The payback for Facebook will eventually be to **monetise this huge user base** with recalibrated charges in the **international messaging** arena. Facebook believes that the future lies in **international, cross-platform communications**. (Information from the official website of CNBC.)

### 20.2.2 Analysis of the numbers

| Measure | Calculation | Result |
|---------|-------------|-------:|
| Price per registered user | 21.8 bn ÷ 450 m | **$48.4** |
| Price per active user (70% active = 315 m) | 21.8 bn ÷ 315 m | **$69.2** |
| Price per user excluding the $4 bn retention payout | 17.8 bn ÷ 450 m | **$39.6** |
| Price per user on the "pipeline" of 1 bn users | 21.8 bn ÷ 1,000 m | **$21.8** |
| Revenue yield: $1 a year × 450 m users (if all paid) | ≈ $450 m a year | Price ÷ revenue ≈ **48×** |

The source's "$55 per user" is a rounded, deal-specific figure (the exact arithmetic depends on the user count assumed); what matters is the order of magnitude: **tens of dollars per user** for a business earning about a dollar per user.

### 20.2.3 Which valuation approach does this reflect?
- **Market (user-based) approach:** price per user/active user — comparables from other communications platforms.
- **Income approach with a long runway:** the value rests on monetising a very large user base in the future (Chapter 16: top-down TAM/SAM/SOM).
- **Strategic (investment) value** to Facebook: protecting its own communication franchise from a rival, plus synergy with its ad platform — the value to *this* buyer exceeds fair value to a financial buyer.
- **The $4 billion retention payout** is a *cost of retaining human capital*; it is not paid for assets; in accounting terms such payments (if contingent on continued service) may be treated as compensation rather than purchase consideration.

### 20.2.4 Lessons
1. For a platform, **users are the asset**; the multiple-of-users approach is the only *market* measure when revenue is tiny.
2. **Growth rate and runway** (1 million new users a day) drove the valuation.
3. **Strategic value** can exceed intrinsic standalone value.
4. **Sanity check** by backing out the implied revenue and margin required: to justify $21.8 bn at a 10% discount rate in perpetuity, WhatsApp would need annual free cash flow of about $2.2 bn (21.8 × 0.10) — i.e., roughly **$2–3 of free cash per user** on a billion users, compared with $1 of revenue per user then. The valuation embeds a belief in a 3–5× rise in monetisation per user plus massive user growth.

## 20.3 Case study 3 — A distressed hospital chain

*Lifeline Hospitals Ltd* (₹ crore): facing high leverage after a debt-funded expansion and a downturn in occupancy.

| Item | Value |
|------|------:|
| Going-concern enterprise value (if recovery plan succeeds) | 1,800 |
| Debt (face value) | 1,500 |
| Distress-sale enterprise value (if the plan fails) | 900 |
| Probability that the plan fails | 40% |

**Step 1 — Equity by state.**
- Recovery state: 1,800 − 1,500 = **300**.
- Distress state: 900 − 1,500 < 0 → equity worth **0** (limited liability).

**Step 2 — Value of equity** = 0.60 × 300 + 0.40 × 0 = **₹180 crore**.

**Step 3 — Value of debt** = 0.60 × 1,500 + 0.40 × 900 = **₹1,260 crore** (84% of face value).

*Check:* equity + debt = 180 + 1,260 = 1,440 = 0.6 × 1,800 + 0.4 × 900 ✔.

**Lesson.** A naive "blended EV minus debt" = 1,440 − 1,500 = −60 would suggest zero equity; the **state-by-state** approach recognises that equity has value in the recovery state and floors at zero in distress — equity is an option on the firm. Raise the failure probability to 60% and equity falls to 0.4 × 300 = 120.

## 20.4 Case study 4 — A fintech start-up

*PayNest Ltd*: a payments start-up with **12 lakh active users**, revenue ₹6 crore (growing 150%), losses ₹9 crore a year, 24 months of cash. An investor proposes to invest **₹15 crore**.

**Method 1 — comparable transactions.** A recent acquisition of a similar payments app was at **₹300 per active user**. PayNest value = 12 lakh × ₹300 = **₹36 crore**; adjust +10% for faster growth = ₹39.6 crore.

**Method 2 — scorecard.** Average pre-money of seed/Series-A payments start-ups ₹40 crore; factors: team 30% × 110%, opportunity 25% × 120%, product 15% × 100%, competition 10% × 70%, marketing 10% × 100%, funding need 5% × 80%, other 5% × 100% → 0.33 + 0.30 + 0.15 + 0.07 + 0.10 + 0.04 + 0.05 = **1.04** → ₹41.6 crore.

**Method 3 — venture capital method.** Expected exit value in 5 years ₹400 crore; investor wants **6×** → post-money = 400 ÷ 6 = **₹66.7 crore**; pre-money = 66.7 − 15 = **₹51.7 crore**; ownership = 15 ÷ 66.7 = **22.5%**.

**Method 4 — First Chicago.** Success (25%): exit ₹700 crore; moderate (40%): ₹200 crore; failure (35%): ₹10 crore; discount 30% for 5 years (factor 0.2693): 0.25 × 188.5 + 0.40 × 53.9 + 0.35 × 2.7 = 47.1 + 21.5 + 0.9 = **₹69.6 crore** post-money-equivalent → pre-money ₹54.6 crore.

**Summary of pre-money values (₹ crore):**

| Method | Pre-money |
|--------|----------:|
| Comparable transactions | 36–40 |
| Scorecard | 41.6 |
| VC method (6×) | 51.7 |
| First Chicago | 54.6 |

**Conclusion.** Pre-money of **₹40–₹52 crore**, with negotiation around ₹45 crore (investor gets 15 ÷ 60 = 25%). The *low-end* methods (market, scorecard) rely on precedent; the *high-end* (VC, First Chicago) on the exit story. The gap highlights that start-up valuation is a negotiation anchored by method.

## 20.5 Case study 5 — A cyclical manufacturer at the top of the cycle

*Rajat Steels Ltd*: EBITDA margins over the last eight years: 6%, 14%, 22%, 17%, 4%, −3%, 8%, 18% (average 10.75%). Revenue ₹2,000 crore; the latest year (18%) is near a peak.

- **Naive approach:** capitalise last year's EBITDA of ₹360 crore at 5× = ₹1,800 crore.
- **Normalised approach:** use the average margin 10.75% → EBITDA ₹215 crore × 5× = **₹1,075 crore**.
- **Difference:** the naive method overstates by **67%**.

*Lesson.* For cyclical businesses the base year must be normalised to a mid-cycle level (Chapter 19) before applying multiples or capitalising earnings. A DCF should model the cycle explicitly for some years and revert to mid-cycle in the terminal year.

## 20.6 What the five cases have in common

| Theme | Case(s) |
|-------|---------|
| Method follows the data available | Vodafone–Idea (no cash-flow forecasts → market, comparables, NAV) |
| Perceived value vs computed value | Vodafone–Idea (market reaction) |
| Users as the asset; strategic value | WhatsApp |
| Equity as an option; state-by-state valuation | Lifeline Hospitals |
| Multiple methods, wide range, negotiation | PayNest |
| Normalise before valuing | Rajat Steels |

## Chapter 20 summary

- Vodafone–Idea: market value, comparable multiples and NAV were used; DCF was not (no projections); the market still reacted negatively — "perceived value".
- WhatsApp: $21.8 bn for 450 m users (70% active), growth 1 m users a day, ≈ $55 per user including a $4 bn retention payout; valuation rests on user growth and future monetisation.
- Constructed cases reinforce distress, start-up and cyclical valuation.
