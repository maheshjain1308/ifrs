# Chapter 18 — Impact of ESG on Valuation

## 18.1 What is ESG?

**Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG)** is a framework designed to be embedded into an organisation's strategy that considers the **needs and ways in which to generate value for all organisational stakeholders** — employees, customers, suppliers and financiers. An illustrative list of what the three factors include:

| **Environmental** | **Social** | **Governance** |
|-------------------|-----------|----------------|
| Climate change | Employee development | Board independence |
| Water | Diversity and inclusion | Board diversity |
| Waste generation | Community development | Anti-corruption and bribery |
| Emissions | Health and safety | Tax transparency |
| Biodiversity | Customer | Ethical conduct |

ESG is on the radar of several investors today. Focusing on ESG issues can bring out **risks and opportunities** for the company's ability to create sustainable value. The key environmental aspects are **climate change and natural-resource scarcity**; social issues include **diversity and inclusivity, labour practices, health and safety, and cyber security**; the governance aspect emphasises **board diversity and independence, executive pay and tax transparency**.

## 18.2 The momentum

The source chapter lists the key developments:

- **Investment pace in ESG funds:** ESG funds tapped in excess of **$50 billion in 2020** and total assets with ESG focus crossed more than **$35 trillion** in the same period.
- **Green bonds:** the green-bonds market crossed a milestone of **$1 trillion in 2020**.
- **Sustainability taxonomy on the rise:** key regions (for example the **European Union**) have already defined sustainability taxonomies; others are in the process.
- **Convergence of ESG frameworks:** the IFRS Foundation launched work to develop a **single global reporting standard** on ESG.
- **SEBI:** in **February 2023**, the Securities Exchange Board of India proposed a **regulatory framework on ESG disclosures by listed entities**.

ESG performance and linked ratings have begun to play an influencing role for companies going to market to raise funds. The high ESG focus of investors, lenders and financial institutions has reached a *tipping point* and has started to impact financing options: companies with high ESG focus stand to benefit through **preferential/lower cost of debt** and access to specialised products like **Green, Social and Sustainability-linked Bonds**.

## 18.3 From "good to have" to "must have"

Traditionally ESG was viewed as "good to have" in the area of business ethics, sustainability, diversity and community. With heightened interest from stakeholder groups, directors realise it is moving into **"must-have" territory**. The business case for ESG generally begins with **operational efficiency and risk reduction** and extends to longer-term **operational and organisational resiliency and sustainability**. Boards recognise the strong and direct link between building a profitable business and focusing on environmental and social considerations; they also know that this requires **robust governance practices** that fortify the company's portfolio as a strong contender with investors and shareholders.

## 18.4 How do ESG factors enter valuation?

The most popular technique of valuing any business is **discounting future cash flows**. Accordingly, the impact of ESG risks can be incorporated in **either the discount rate or the expected cash flows**.

### 18.4.1 Option A — adjust the discount rate
Management and investors are generally more interested in adjusting the discount rate by including a **risk premium**. This is more *practical* but the impact of ESG factors may **not be explicit**: a reader of the valuation cannot see *what* risk the premium represents, *when* it hits, or *how large* it is.

### 18.4.2 Option B — adjust the expected cash flows (more explicit)
Adjustment of ESG factors in the **cash flows** would be more explicit. The source chapter shows how each factor can be incorporated:

| Factor | How to incorporate in expected cash flows |
|--------|------------------------------------------|
| **E — Environment** | Carry out **2-degree scenario analysis** (e.g., what happens if the temperature of the plant increases by 2 degrees); adjust cash flows by considering **carbon points** (carbon price/credits) |
| **S — Social** | Adjust the impact of **social-measures cost on revenue**, such as better labour working conditions, **CSR** and other welfare measures for the various stakeholders |
| **G — Governance** | Adjust the impact of **poor governance on revenue** in the form of **penalty, fines, taxes** |

## 18.5 Worked example 18.1: carbon cost — cash-flow vs discount-rate approach

*Vindhya Cement Ltd.* Expected FCFF next year ₹300 crore; growth 3%; WACC 11%. Baseline enterprise value = 300 ÷ (0.11 − 0.03) = **₹3,750 crore**.

The plant emits 1 million tonnes of CO₂ a year. A carbon price of ₹500 per tonne is expected to apply (full pass-through not possible): cost = 10 lakh × ₹500 = ₹50 crore a year before tax; after tax (25%) = **₹37.5 crore**, growing with the business at 3%.

**Option B — cash flows:** adjusted FCFF = 300 − 37.5 = ₹262.5 crore; EV = 262.5 ÷ 0.08 = **₹3,281 crore** → a decline of **12.5%** (₹469 crore).

**Option A — discount rate:** if the valuer instead adds a 0.5% ESG risk premium to WACC (11.5%): EV = 300 ÷ 0.085 = **₹3,529 crore** → a decline of 5.9%.

The two approaches give very different answers (12.5% vs 5.9%), and only the cash-flow approach tells the reader *why*. The cash-flow approach also lets the valuer test **mitigation**: for instance, an abatement investment of ₹150 crore that halves emissions would reduce the carbon cost to ₹18.75 crore after tax; EV with abatement = (300 − 18.75) ÷ 0.08 − 150 = 3,515.6 − 150 = **₹3,365.6 crore**, i.e., better than doing nothing (3,281) — the investment is **worth ₹84.4 crore**.

## 18.6 Worked example 18.2: governance penalty

A company expects a regulatory penalty of ₹40 crore with 25% probability in each of the next two years, and an increase in its annual compliance cost of ₹5 crore afterwards. Expected penalty cost = 0.25 × 40 = ₹10 crore a year for two years; compliance cost ₹5 crore a year in perpetuity after year 2 (after tax 3.75, PV at 11% as a perpetuity from year 3: 3.75 ÷ 0.11 × 0.8116 = 27.7 crore).

PV of penalties (after-tax 7.5 each year): 7.5 × (0.9009 + 0.8116) = 12.8. Total negative effect ≈ ₹40.5 crore — about 1.1% of a ₹3,750 crore value. A valuer who ignores it overvalues; one who adds a large "governance discount" without quantifying risks hiding the numbers.

## 18.7 ESG and the cost of capital

Evidence of a systematic ESG effect on the cost of capital is mixed, but several channels exist:

1. **Cost of debt:** sustainability-linked loans and green bonds can carry lower coupons; poor ESG can raise spreads or restrict lenders.
2. **Cost of equity:** high ESG investors may accept lower returns; poor governance may raise the required return (e.g., through concerns about minority treatment).
3. **Access to capital:** some funds exclude companies with poor ESG, narrowing the investor base.

In valuation, adjust the discount rate *only* for risks that are **systematic** and not already captured in cash flows; avoid double-counting.

## 18.8 ESG across the valuation approaches

| Approach | ESG channels |
|----------|--------------|
| **Income/DCF** | Cash-flow adjustments (carbon cost, capex for transition, fines, welfare costs, green revenue); discount rate; terminal growth (durability) |
| **Market/relative** | Peers' ESG-driven multiple premia/discounts; adjust peer set; ESG-related de-rating |
| **Asset-based** | Impairment of stranded assets; environmental liabilities (remediation, decommissioning); revaluation of green assets |
| **Distress** | Environmental claims raise probability of distress |

## 18.9 Data and frameworks

- Company-level disclosures: Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) for large listed Indian companies; global frameworks (ISSB/IFRS sustainability standards, GRI, TCFD).
- **ESG ratings** differ widely across providers; use primary data (emissions, accident rates, board composition) over scores where possible.
- **Materiality**: focus on issues that affect financial performance in the particular sector (e.g., emissions for cement; data security for IT; safety for mining).

## 18.10 Practical guidance

1. **Identify material ESG issues** for the sector.
2. **Quantify** exposure (emissions, water use, incident rates, governance flags).
3. **Build scenarios** (regulation, carbon price, demand shifts, physical risk).
4. **Insert** costs and revenues in cash flows with timing.
5. **Document** discount-rate choices; avoid double-counting.
6. Include **opportunities** (green products, lower financing cost, resilience) as well as risks.

## 18.11 Limits

ESG data are inconsistent; regulation is evolving; time horizons of ESG risks are long, beyond typical explicit forecast periods; scenario analysis requires judgement. A valuer should disclose the ESG assumptions and their sensitivity.

## Chapter 18 summary

- ESG = environmental, social, governance; now "must-have", with momentum in funds, green bonds, taxonomies, a single global reporting standard and SEBI's 2023 proposal.
- High-ESG companies may get lower cost of debt and access to green/sustainability-linked financing.
- Incorporate ESG in valuation via the discount rate (practical but opaque) or via expected cash flows (more explicit): E — 2-degree scenario and carbon points; S — cost of social measures; G — penalties and fines.
