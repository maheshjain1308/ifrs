# Chapter 30 — A Valuer's Questions and Answers

This chapter collects forty questions that students and practitioners ask again and again, with concise answers that point back to the chapter where the topic is developed. It can serve as a quick-reference review before an examination or an engagement.

## A. Concepts and approach

**1. Why do different valuers reach different values for the same company?**
Because valuation involves judgement about the future (growth, margins, risk) and about methods (which approach, which comparables). A good valuation makes the judgements explicit and shows a range (Chapters 1, 13, 29).

**2. Is "value" the same as the price in the market?**
No. Price is what the asset trades for; value is a reasoned estimate of worth. The gap between them is what investors try to exploit (Chapter 1).

**3. Which approach is best — asset, income or cash flow?**
It depends on the business and purpose. Asset-based suits holding companies, break-up and liquidation; income-based suits stable, profitable firms; DCF suits firms with forecastable cash flows. Use several and reconcile (Chapters 3–5, 13).

**4. Why not just use the P/E ratio for everything?**
P/E is relative, distorted by leverage and one-offs, and unusable when earnings are negative. It embeds the market's assumptions about growth and risk (Chapters 4, 8).

**5. When is the asset approach inappropriate?**
For knowledge-based firms (IT, consulting) and for any firm whose value lies in earning power rather than in assets (Chapter 3).

**6. What is the difference between fair value and fair market value?**
Fair value (reporting) is an exit price between market participants; fair market value is the price between a willing buyer and seller, neither compelled and both informed. The definitions overlap but are used in different contexts (Chapters 1, 13, 28).

**7. What is the minimum I should accept as a seller, and the maximum I should pay as a buyer?**
Seller's minimum = the higher of liquidation value and standalone going-concern value (or the next-best alternative). Buyer's maximum = the lowest of replacement cost, standalone value plus retainable synergies, and financial capacity (Chapter 13).

## B. Cash flows and DCF

**8. How many years should the explicit forecast cover?**
Long enough for growth, margins and returns to approach steady state — often 5 years for stable businesses, 7–10 for growth businesses. A short horizon puts more weight on the terminal value (Chapter 5).

**9. What growth rate should I use in the terminal value?**
No more than the long-run growth of the economy in the currency of the cash flows, and no more than the risk-free rate; for mature firms often inflation plus modest real growth. Tie it to reinvestment (Chapters 2, 5, 26).

**10. Why is the terminal value 70–90% of my DCF? Is that wrong?**
It is typical, because a going concern's cash flows beyond the forecast are valued in the terminal value. It signals that the terminal assumptions deserve the most scrutiny and sensitivity analysis (Chapter 5).

**11. Do I use FCFF or FCFE?**
FCFF discounted at WACC gives enterprise value; FCFE discounted at the cost of equity gives equity value directly. Never mix them (Chapter 5, 26).

**12. Should I use actual tax or tax on EBIT in FCFF?**
Tax on EBIT; the interest tax shield is in the after-tax cost of debt in WACC (Chapter 26).

**13. How should I treat one-time items?**
Remove one-time incomes and add back one-time expenses (after tax) to reach maintainable cash flows, as the syllabus illustration does (Chapters 5, 19).

**14. How do I deal with working capital?**
Forecast net operating working capital (excluding cash and borrowings) from days or a percentage of revenue and deduct its *increase* (Chapter 26).

**15. What if cash flows are negative in the early years?**
That is common for start-ups and platforms. Include them; the PV of early losses reduces value; ensure the terminal value reflects a plausible mature state and consider survival probability (Chapters 15, 16).

**16. Do I discount the terminal value?**
Yes — from the horizon back to today (Chapter 2, 5). In the syllabus illustration the figure labelled TV is already a present value (Chapter 5).

## C. Discount rates

**17. How do I choose the risk-free rate?**
A long-dated government bond yield in the same currency as the cash flows on the valuation date (Chapter 6).

**18. What is the market risk premium and where does it come from?**
The extra return of the market over the risk-free rate, estimated from long-run history, implied premiums or surveys; expect a range (Chapter 6).

**19. How do I get a beta for a private company?**
Take pure-play peers' equity betas, un-lever to asset betas, average, and re-lever to the target's capital structure (Chapter 7).

**20. What is the difference between asset beta and equity beta?**
Asset beta reflects only business risk; equity beta adds financial risk from debt; equity beta ≥ asset beta (Chapter 7).

**21. Do I use book or market values in WACC weights?**
Market values (Hansel Ltd., Chapter 21, Q6). If market values are unknown, use target weights.

**22. Should I add a "safety margin" to the discount rate?**
Not if the cash flows already reflect risk; use scenarios to express risk explicitly (Chapters 6, 29).

**23. Why does WACC change between high-growth and stable phases?**
Beta, capital structure and cost of debt change as the business matures (Chapter 21, Q7).

**24. When should I use a company-specific risk premium?**
For firms with risks not captured by beta (nascent platforms, key-person dependence) when scenario analysis is not feasible; disclose and justify (Chapter 16).

## D. Special situations

**25. Can I use DCF for a loss-making start-up?**
Yes, with adaptation: forecast the path to a mature state, discount at a suitable rate and weight by survival; supplement with VC, scorecard, comparable-transaction and First Chicago methods (Chapter 15).

**26. How do I value a distressed company?**
Use modified DCF, DCF plus distress value, APV or adjusted relative multiples; liquidation value is a floor; equity behaves like an option (Chapter 14).

**27. What is the best way to value a conglomerate?**
Sum-of-the-parts/chop-shop: value each business by its own peers and add; deduct corporate costs and net debt; compare with market value to see the conglomerate discount (Chapter 10).

**28. How do I value a professional firm?**
Normalise partners' pay to market rates and one-offs; capitalise or discount normalised earnings; cross-check with fee multiples; adjust for key-person and concentration risk (Chapter 17).

**29. How do I include ESG?**
Preferably in the cash flows: carbon cost and scenario analysis for E, welfare and CSR costs for S, penalties for G; avoid double-counting in the discount rate (Chapter 18).

**30. How do I value brands and patents?**
Relief-from-royalty for brands and technology; multi-period excess earnings for customer relationships; add unrecorded intangibles to invested capital in EVA (Chapter 27).

**31. What is the role of the going-concern assumption?**
Most valuations assume it; if the business has no value as a going concern, use liquidation value (Chapter 13).

## E. Deals and reporting

**32. How are synergies treated in price?**
Value them separately, risk-adjust, and pay the seller only a share; maximum price = standalone + synergies (Chapter 25).

**33. How do I compute an exchange ratio?**
Compute value per share of both companies on the same basis (e.g., weights on NAV and earnings) and divide target by acquirer (Chapter 21, Q4–Q5; Chapter 25).

**34. What are control and marketability discounts?**
Control premium is the extra paid for control; DLOC = 1 − 1/(1 + premium); DLOM reflects illiquidity of unlisted shares. Apply only where the standard of value requires and with evidence (Chapter 28).

**35. What does Ind AS 113 require?**
Fair value as an exit price using market-participant assumptions, with a three-level input hierarchy; most unlisted DCFs are Level 3 (Chapter 28).

**36. What goes into a valuation report?**
Purpose, standard and premise of value, valuation date, sources, methods, assumptions, calculations, sensitivity, reconciliation, conclusion, limitations (Chapter 29).

## F. Examination technique

**37. How should I present a computational answer?**
Formula, labelled steps, figures with units, one-line interpretation (Chapter 23).

**38. What if the question does not specify a method?**
Value by as many methods as the data allow, comment on each and conclude with a range (Chapter 13, 23).

**39. Which topics are most commonly examined?**
EVA/MVA, DCF (two-stage), exchange ratio in mergers, WACC with beta re-levering, and short notes on distressed firms, start-ups, platforms, ESG, going vs non-going concern (Chapters 11, 21, 23).

**40. How should I revise in the final week?**
Use the plan in Chapter 23: formulas, then worked questions (Chapters 21–22, Appendix D), then theory notes and the self-test (Appendix E).
