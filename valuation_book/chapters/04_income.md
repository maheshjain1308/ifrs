# Chapter 4 — The Income-Based (Earnings) Approach

## 4.1 Overcoming the limits of the asset approach

The asset approach tells us what the enterprise *has*; the income approach tells us what it *earns*. The income-based approach attempts to overcome the drawbacks of asset-backed valuation by referring to **earning potential**. It is more suitable where the acquiring company intends to **continue the business of the target** for the foreseeable future, without selling or liquidating its assets. If any **additional earning** arises from the acquisition (synergy, economies of scale), it should also be considered in the valuation.

Two versions of the same idea are in use:

1. the **P/E ratio** (price-earnings multiplier, or its reciprocal, the *earning yield*);
2. the **capitalisation rate** applied to maintainable earnings.

## 4.2 PE ratio (earning yield multiplier) method

This method is generally used for valuing **listed companies** whose PE ratios are available. Its benefit: it takes into account the **expected growth rate** of the company as well as **market expectations**, because the market's P/E already embeds growth and risk.

**Price per share = EPS × PE ratio**

Although mainly followed for listed companies, the PE ratio of **equivalent companies or the industry** can be used to value shares of **unlisted** companies. The method serves as the **minimum acceptable price** to the shareholders of the target company.

### Steps for an unlisted target
1. Choose the **PE ratio of an equivalent quoted company**.
2. Make an **adjustment downward** for the additional risk due to non-listing of shares (illiquidity, thinner information).
3. Determine the **future maintainable EPS**.
4. **Multiply** the EPS by the adjusted PE ratio.

*Worked example 4.1.* A comparable listed firm trades at P/E 12. The target is unlisted; the valuer applies a 20% reduction for non-listing: adjusted P/E = 12 × 0.80 = **9.6**. Future maintainable EPS = ₹8.16. Price per share = 8.16 × 9.6 = **₹78.34**. With 10 crore shares, equity value = **₹783 crore**.

### Strengths and limits of PE valuation
- **Strength:** simple, market-linked, captures growth and risk expectations.
- **Weakness:** depends on the comparability of the peer; P/E is distorted by leverage and by one-off items in EPS; cannot be used when earnings are negative; the discount for non-listing (here 20%) is judgemental.

## 4.3 Capitalisation of earnings

In this method the value of the business is computed by **capitalising the company's expected annual maintainable profit** using an appropriate **required rate of return** (yield or discount rate).

**Capitalised earning value = Expected annual maintainable profit ÷ Capitalisation rate (required earning yield)**

### 4.3.1 Maintainable profit
Annual expected maintainable profit can be calculated using the **weighted average of previous years' profits**, after adjusting for synergy benefits or economies of scale. Steps:

1. Collect 3–5 years of profit after tax.
2. **Adjust** for extraordinary and non-recurring items, non-operating income, and accounting differences (normalisation, Chapter 19).
3. Compute a **weighted average**, giving higher weights to recent years if the trend is rising.
4. Adjust for **future** changes: new product, additional capacity, synergy, loss of a customer.

*Worked example 4.2.* Profits after tax (₹ crore): Year 1: 62; Year 2: 70; Year 3: 78; Year 4: 85. Weights 1, 2, 3, 4:
(62×1 + 70×2 + 78×3 + 85×4) ÷ 10 = (62 + 140 + 234 + 340) ÷ 10 = **77.6**.
If the acquirer expects ₹4 crore of additional after-tax profit from economies of scale, maintainable profit = **81.6**.

### 4.3.2 The capitalisation rate
The capitalisation rate depends on many factors. It can be approximated as follows:

**Required earning yield = EPS ÷ Share price**
or
**Reciprocal of PE ratio = 1 ÷ PE ratio**

Alternatively, **CAPM** can be used to give a required return (Chapter 6): Ke = Rf + β(Rm − Rf).

*Worked example 4.3.* Maintainable profit ₹81.6 crore; required yield 14%. Value = 81.6 ÷ 0.14 = **₹582.9 crore**. At 13% the value is ₹627.7 crore; at 15% ₹544.0 crore — notice the sensitivity: a 1-point change in the rate moves value by roughly 7%.

### 4.3.3 Link between the two versions
A P/E of 10 corresponds to a capitalisation rate (earning yield) of 10%; a rate of 14% corresponds to P/E ≈ 7.1. The two are two ways of expressing the same thing. The capitalisation method is *explicit* about the required return; the P/E method takes it from the market.

## 4.4 Capitalisation of earnings and the Gordon model

Capitalising a constant earning stream is a **zero-growth perpetuity**: value = E ÷ r. If earnings grow at g, the corresponding formula is E₁ ÷ (r − g). So the capitalisation method silently assumes **no growth** unless the rate is adjusted: the rate used is effectively **(r − g)**. A valuer using a low rate for a growing company is doing exactly that. State it explicitly.

## 4.5 Advantages and disadvantages (source chapter)

- **Advantage:** it is a **forward-looking** approach (based on future maintainable profit) and is simple.
- **Disadvantages:** (i) estimation of expected future profit is subjective; (ii) differences in treatment of **extraordinary and exceptional items** across years and firms; (iii) no explicit view of cash flows and reinvestment; (iv) sensitive to the capitalisation rate.

## 4.6 Worked example: exchange ratio by earnings capitalisation (merger)

*Hari Ltd* and *Bala Ltd* plan a merger. Weighted average maintainable post-tax profits: Hari ₹300 crore; Bala ₹10 crore. Capitalisation rate for both: 8%. Hari has 3.5 crore shares; Bala 0.65 crore shares.

- Hari: 300 ÷ 0.08 = ₹3,750 crore → ₹1,071.43 per share.
- Bala: 10 ÷ 0.08 = ₹125 crore → ₹192.31 per share.

Earnings-based exchange ratio = 192.31 ÷ 1,071.43 = 0.1795 Hari shares per Bala share. (In Chapter 21 this is combined with the net asset approach to arrive at the fair-value ratio of 0.1787 as in the source question.)

## 4.7 Combining asset and earnings values

Often valuers compute value on **both** asset and income bases, then take a **weighted average**. Weights reflect the reliability of each (e.g., 1 : 3 for NAV : earnings capitalisation in the source chapter's merger question). If the earnings value is higher than the net asset value, the difference is **goodwill**:

**Goodwill (super-profit method) = (Maintainable profit − Normal return on net assets) × Capitalisation factor**

*Example.* Net assets 550; normal return 12% → 66; maintainable profit 81.6; super profit 15.6; capitalised at 14%: goodwill = 15.6 ÷ 0.14 = **₹111.4 crore**. Value = net assets + goodwill = 550 + 111.4 = **₹661.4 crore** — between the pure asset (550) and pure earnings (583–745) values.

## 4.8 Common pitfalls

1. Using *reported* profit including one-offs without normalising.
2. Applying a peer P/E without adjusting for growth/risk differences.
3. Using EPS after preference dividends inconsistently (preference dividends must be deducted to get earnings for equity; see the Eagle Ltd. question in Chapter 21).
4. Capitalising profit *before* tax at an after-tax rate (or vice-versa).
5. Forgetting that capitalisation values the *equity* (profit after interest) while profit before interest values the *enterprise*.

## Chapter 4 summary

- The income approach values the business on maintainable earnings: **P/E × EPS** or **profit ÷ capitalisation rate**.
- For unlisted targets use a listed peer's P/E, adjusted downward for non-listing.
- Maintainable profit = weighted average of past profits adjusted for synergies and one-offs.
- Capitalisation rate ≈ EPS/price = 1/PE, or CAPM.
- Forward-looking but subjective; no view of reinvestment.
