# Chapter 16 — Valuation of Digital Platforms

## 16.1 What is a digital platform?

A **digital platform** is a **software-based online infrastructure that facilitates interactions and transactions between users**. Platforms are principally built to facilitate **many-to-many interactions**. Based on the kind of services provided, the source chapter classifies them as follows:

| Category | Description | Examples |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| **Marketplace** | Multiple buyers are matched to multiple suppliers | Booking.com (guests–hotels), Uber (travellers–drivers), Amazon (sellers–buyers) |
| **Search engine** | Multiple people looking for information are matched to multiple information sources; a search request triggers the system to seek the desired information | Google, Bing, Baidu |
| **Repository** | Multiple suppliers "deposit" materials into a kind of library, to be retrieved by users later | Spotify, YouTube, GitHub |
| **Digital communication** | Multiple users send messages/documents or interact in real time by voice/video | WhatsApp, Microsoft Teams, Telegram, Slack |
| **Digital community** | People who want to remain virtually connected for a long time find each other and interact | Facebook, LinkedIn |
| **Payments platform** | Matching between those owing money and those wanting to be paid | Paytm, GPay |

The *principles* of valuation for digital platforms are largely like those for other types of companies, with certain nuances peculiar to the industry. Platforms are valued using the **income**, **market** and **cost** approaches.

## 16.2 Why platforms are tricky to value

1. **Network effects** — value to each user rises with the number of users; growth can be non-linear.
2. **Negative early cash flows** — platforms burn cash to acquire users ("penetrative strategies": cash burn and lower margins to create market share and popularise the platform).
3. **Capital-light** — low book value of assets; the main asset is code and data.
4. **Scarce comparables** — few listed peers; each platform's business model differs.
5. **Winner-takes-most dynamics** — outcomes are binary-ish.
6. **Dependence on management** — survival depends on the quality of promoters, investors and their capability to execute and adapt.

## 16.3 Income approach

Valuation methods under the income approach lay emphasis on **projected financial performance**, taking into account **future revenues and costs** using company-specific revenue and cost drivers and applicable capital expenditure and working-capital cycles.

### 16.3.1 Top-down approach
Backward working is required under the **top-down approach**, which starts with an analysis of the **total potential market** for the platform on a global or domestic scale, often called the **Total Addressable Market (TAM)**. The next step is to estimate the **share** the company expects to gain in the target market and the time to reach such share — referred to as the **Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM)** and **Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)**. Then the company estimates its business plan to accomplish its objectives and strategy: how it will gain market share, increase revenues while optimising or using cash.

The financial forecast must reflect the type and features of the platform's business model: a repository allowing streaming may earn revenue based on **subscribers**; a payments platform may earn on the **number of transactions**. Direct operating costs are unique to each type of platform.

*Worked example 16.1 (top-down).* TAM (online grocery in the country) ₹3,00,000 crore; SAM (cities where the platform will operate) = 25% → ₹75,000 crore; SOM (share obtainable by year 5) = 2% of SAM → **₹1,500 crore** of gross merchandise value. With a take-rate of 12%, revenue = ₹180 crore; at a 15% steady EBITDA margin, EBITDA = ₹27 crore in year 5. The path to that point includes years of negative cash flow; the DCF must fund them.

### 16.3.2 Bottom-up approach
The top-down approach can be **ambitious** for a nascent company, since estimating market size and market share poses practical challenges. Under the **bottom-up approach**, the platform estimates its earnings from the **limited resources it has**: a young platform estimates its revenue and costs given its financial constraints and deploys strategies to **target high-margin sales and cost-cutting** to generate more cash. This is more in line with efficient **capital-budgeting decisions**, which ultimately help forecast earnings and cash flows.

*Worked example 16.2 (bottom-up).* 20 lakh active users; average order frequency 1.5 a month; average order value ₹600; take-rate 10%; churn 20% a year; marketing budget funds 8 lakh new users a year. Revenue per user per year = 1.5 × 12 × 600 × 10% = ₹1,080. Year-1 revenue ≈ 20 lakh × ₹1,080 = ₹216 crore; subsequent growth depends on net new users (new − churned) = 8 − 4 = +4 lakh a year → +20%.

Under both scenarios, **the value of a digital platform depends on the quality of the financial forecasts** — and the growth and survival of an entity depend on its promoters, investors and stakeholders creating products or services that fill a market need, and on their capability to execute and adapt to unexpected circumstances.

### 16.3.3 Discount rate (11.1)
The discount rate should be based on the **type of cash flows being discounted**:

- **Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF)** → discount at the **Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC)**.
- **Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)** → discount at the **cost of equity (Ke)**.

CAPM: **R = Rf + β × (Rm − Rf)** (Chapter 6).

### 16.3.4 Specific considerations (11.1.2)
(a) **Beta** measures the sensitivity of a stock or company to the market. Practically, a company's beta is estimated from the sensitivity of its share price (or its comparables' or industry's) to the market. Given the unique nature of each platform and the **scarcity of listed traded comparables**, estimating beta is challenging: one might need to compare the general diversified sector, the industry driving the revenue, or international comparables.
(b) The **survival** of a platform depends upon the **quality of management, ability to adapt to change quickly and foresee opportunity**.

Thus there are specific risks that **cannot be estimated using CAPM with only the industry or general sector beta**. A **Company-Specific Risk Premium (CSRP)** or **alpha** needs to be estimated and added to determine the appropriate cost of equity used to discount the estimated cash flows. The CSRP for **nascent companies would be higher** than for mature platforms with large operations and a large customer base.

*Worked example 16.3.* Rf 7%, sector beta 1.2, market premium 6%, CSRP 4% for a nascent platform: Ke = 7% + 1.2 × 6% + 4% = **18.2%**; for a mature platform with CSRP 1%: **15.2%**.

> **Caution.** Where possible, reflect specific risks in *probability-weighted cash flows* rather than inflating the discount rate; the CSRP is a practical shortcut when scenarios are unavailable.

## 16.4 Market approach

The **market approach** values a company by comparing it with similar valued companies using multiples such as **P/E**, **EV/EBITDA**, **Price to Book Value** and **Price to Revenue/Sales**. Selection of comparables is vital; parameters such as **market capitalisation, revenue, profit margins and capital structure** are used.

For digital platforms the comparison becomes difficult because:
- **listed comparables are scarce** and even absent for many platforms;
- the underlying value — specifically **profit and EBITDA — may be negative**;
- platforms are **capital-light**, making their **book value very low**.

Hence the market approach for digital platforms **emphasises revenue** — and, more precisely, **the primary driver of revenue** for that kind of platform.

### 16.4.1 Revenue drivers by category

| Category | Drivers of revenue |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Marketplace** (supply and demand) | Number of bookings made, number of registered users, volume of transactions |
| **Payment** (billing and payments) | Number of active subscribers, number of merchants registered on the platform, compatibility and speed of the operating system, security, ease of use |
| **Community** (network of contacts) | Number of users, subscription fees, platform for professionals |
| **Communication** (network for messaging) | Number of users, sponsored links, advertising revenue |
| **Repository** (supply library) | Number of readers and contributors, authenticity of data, duration of use, quality and variety of data |
| **Search** (machine queries and information) | Number of users, relevant search results, time taken per search |

Two search engines can be compared on **total active users** and **average time taken to show relevant results**; the one with more relevant results in shorter time is valued at a premium. For a **repository** platform drawing subscription/advertising revenue per view and duration of visit, comparison can be drawn on the **number of users, average number of views per user and average revenue per user**.

*Example (source).* A search-engine company valued at **₹100 crore** with **50 million users** and content of 1,00,000 hours can be used to compare a similar platform with fewer users but the same or similar revenue parameters.

*Worked example 16.4.* Platform A: ₹100 crore ÷ 5 crore users = **₹20 per user**. Platform B: 3 crore users, with 25% higher average revenue per user than A.
Value of B = 3 crore × ₹20 × 1.25 = **₹75 crore** (versus ₹60 crore if ARPU were equal). Then adjust for differences in growth, engagement, and margin.

### 16.4.2 Regression-based comparison
With many comparables, regress EV/Revenue on growth and margin to estimate a "fair" multiple for the subject given its growth and margin — more defensible than a simple average.

## 16.5 Cost approach

The **cost approach** estimates value from the **sum total of the cost to build the same platform or a similar platform with the same utility**. Since the asset behind a digital platform is the **code**, the **hours spent by developers** to write it are the primary cost. However, this approach may not be most appropriate because it **fails to take into account the revenue-generating capacity of the platform**, which may create significantly higher value for shareholders than the cost of developing it.

*Worked example 16.5.* 40 developers × 18 months × ₹2.5 lakh a month = ₹18 crore; add design and testing ₹3 crore; add servers and tools ₹2 crore → **₹23 crore** cost to rebuild. If the platform has 2 crore users generating revenue, the income and market approaches would give far more; the cost approach provides a *floor* and is relevant for purchase price allocation of the technology asset.

## 16.6 Reconciliation

| Approach | Strength | Weakness for platforms |
|----------|----------|-----------------------|
| Income (DCF) | Captures growth, burn, scale economics | Highly sensitive to forecasts; long loss period |
| Market (revenue multiples) | Reflects market view | Scarce comparables; sentiment |
| Cost | Objective floor | Ignores revenue capacity |

**Conclusion (source):** "The valuation of digital platforms can be tricky based on the peculiarities mentioned above. However, the **fundamentals of valuation remain the same**. The understanding of the business, the revenue model, the quality of management, and the risk-reward parameters determine the value of the digital platform."

## 16.7 Useful metrics for analysis

| Metric | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **CAC** | Customer acquisition cost |
| **LTV** | Lifetime value of a customer (contribution margin × expected lifetime) |
| **LTV/CAC** | Healthy ≥ 3× |
| **Churn / retention** | Customers lost / retained per period |
| **ARPU** | Average revenue per user |
| **Take rate** | Platform revenue ÷ GMV |
| **GMV** | Gross merchandise value |
| **Burn rate; runway** | Cash consumed per month; months of cash left |

## Chapter 16 summary

- Platforms: marketplace, search, repository, communication, community, payments.
- Income approach: top-down (TAM → SAM → SOM) or bottom-up; discount FCFF at WACC, FCFE at Ke; add CSRP for nascent firms.
- Market approach: multiples are hard; use revenue drivers specific to the platform type.
- Cost approach: rebuild cost of code — a floor.
- Fundamentals of valuation remain unchanged.
