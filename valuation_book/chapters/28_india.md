# Chapter 28 — The Indian Context: Standards, Regulation and Practice

The syllabus chapter is written for Indian professionals; its illustrations refer to ₹, to SEBI's ESG proposal and to the work of chartered accountants, merchant bankers and registered valuers. This chapter gives an orientation to the Indian framework in which valuations are commissioned and reviewed, and to the practical issues that arise in India — in estimating inputs, in dealing with promoter-controlled companies, and in documenting the valuation.

> **Important.** The laws, rules and standards mentioned here are amended from time to time. This chapter explains the *structure* and the *principles*; before relying on any specific provision for an examination or an engagement, consult the current text and the latest amendments to the syllabus.

## 28.1 Who values, and why

| Context | Typical valuer | Purpose |
|---------|----------------|---------|
| Mergers, demergers, schemes of arrangement | Registered valuer; merchant banker (fairness opinion) | Share-exchange ratio; reporting to the tribunal and shareholders |
| Preferential allotments, issues for consideration other than cash (unlisted companies) | Registered valuer | Pricing compliance |
| Foreign investment and transfers involving non-residents | Chartered accountant, merchant banker or cost accountant | Pricing at or above/below fair value |
| Listed-company transactions (preferential issue, takeover, delisting, buy-back) | Independent valuer/merchant banker per SEBI rules | Minimum price; fairness |
| Financial reporting | Valuation specialists | Fair value (Ind AS 113), impairment (Ind AS 36), purchase price allocation (Ind AS 103), share-based payment (Ind AS 102) |
| Taxation | Chartered accountant/merchant banker | Fair market value of unquoted shares under the Income-tax Rules |
| Insolvency | Registered valuers | Fair value and liquidation value for the committee of creditors |
| Disputes | Independent expert | Fair value in oppression/mismanagement or appraisal proceedings |

## 28.2 The professional framework

### 28.2.1 Registered valuers
Under the Companies Act, 2013 and the **Companies (Registered Valuers and Valuation) Rules, 2017**, valuations required under the Act must be done by a **registered valuer** registered for the relevant **asset class**: *Securities or Financial Assets*, *Land and Building*, or *Plant and Machinery*. A business valuation (shares or financial assets) falls in the first class. Registered valuers must meet qualification and experience requirements, belong to a registered valuers' organisation, observe a code of conduct and declare independence.

### 28.2.2 Valuation standards
The **ICAI Valuation Standards** (issued by the Institute of Chartered Accountants of India) and the **International Valuation Standards (IVS)** set out the vocabulary, the bases of value, the approaches and methods (asset, income and market), the scope of work, documentation and reporting. The standards echo the structure of this book: the three approaches; the need to state the *basis* and *premise* of value; the requirement to consider and reconcile more than one approach; and the content of the report.

### 28.2.3 Independence and documentation
A valuation report should state the purpose, the valuation date, the basis and premise of value, the information relied upon, the methods used (and any considered but rejected, with reasons), the assumptions, the limitations, and a declaration of independence. The report should allow a reader to *follow and replicate* the numbers.

## 28.3 Fair value under Ind AS 113

Ind AS 113 defines **fair value** as the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an **orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date** — an *exit price* from the viewpoint of market participants.

### 28.3.1 The fair-value hierarchy

| Level | Inputs | Example |
|-------|--------|---------|
| **Level 1** | Quoted prices in active markets for identical assets | Listed shares |
| **Level 2** | Observable inputs other than Level 1 | Prices of similar assets; interest rates and yield curves |
| **Level 3** | Unobservable inputs | DCF of an unlisted company using management forecasts and a judgemental discount rate |

Valuations of unlisted businesses are typically **Level 3**; disclosure includes the valuation technique and the key unobservable inputs and a sensitivity.

### 28.3.2 Market-participant assumptions
Fair value uses the assumptions that **market participants** would use — not the entity's own synergies, unless market participants would also benefit from them. This is the *fair value* standard, and it differs from *investment value* (value to a specific buyer).

## 28.4 Impairment: value in use (Ind AS 36)

An asset or cash-generating unit (CGU) is impaired if its carrying amount exceeds its **recoverable amount** — the higher of **fair value less costs of disposal** and **value in use**. Value in use is a DCF with prescribed features:

- cash flows from reasonable and supportable assumptions, generally based on the **most recent budgets** (normally up to five years), extrapolated with a steady or declining growth rate;
- exclude **future restructurings** not yet committed and **enhancements** of the asset's performance;
- exclude **financing** cash flows and **income-tax** flows (in principle, a **pre-tax** discount rate is used);
- the discount rate reflects the market's current assessment of the time value of money and the risks specific to the asset.

*Illustration.* A CGU has a carrying amount of ₹800 crore. Value in use is ₹760 crore; fair value less costs of disposal ₹740 crore. Recoverable amount = ₹760 crore (the higher); impairment loss = 800 − 760 = **₹40 crore**.

## 28.5 Purchase price allocation (Ind AS 103) and share-based payment (Ind AS 102)

- **Ind AS 103:** identifiable assets acquired and liabilities assumed are measured at fair value; the residual is goodwill (Chapter 27).
- **Ind AS 102:** options granted to employees are measured at **grant-date fair value** (option-pricing model — Black–Scholes or binomial) and expensed over the vesting period; for an unlisted company the underlying share value comes from a valuation.

## 28.6 Valuation in specific Indian transactions (overview)

### 28.6.1 Schemes of arrangement and share-exchange ratios
A scheme under the Companies Act normally requires a **valuation report** from a registered valuer and a **fairness opinion** from a merchant banker on the share-exchange ratio. The report weighs asset, income and market approaches (with weights explained) — as in Q4 (H Ltd./B Ltd.) and Q5 (AB Ltd./XY Ltd.) in Chapter 21. For listed companies, SEBI requirements on pricing and on the role of the audit committee and independent directors also apply.

### 28.6.2 Foreign investment and FEMA pricing
Where an Indian company issues shares to, or transfers shares from, non-residents, the price must satisfy FEMA pricing guidelines — broadly, the price should be determined using an **internationally accepted pricing methodology on an arm's length basis**, certified by a chartered accountant, merchant banker or cost accountant. DCF is widely accepted as such a methodology.

### 28.6.3 Taxation of unquoted shares
The Income-tax Rules prescribe methods for determining the **fair market value of unquoted equity shares** for specified purposes; for certain transactions, a **DCF-based valuation** by a merchant banker or an accountant is permitted alongside a net-asset-value formula. Check which method applies to the section concerned.

### 28.6.4 Insolvency
Under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, **registered valuers** determine the **fair value** and **liquidation value** of the corporate debtor's assets; these are used by the committee of creditors when evaluating resolution plans (Chapter 14).

## 28.7 Estimating inputs in an Indian context

| Input | Practical points |
|-------|------------------|
| **Risk-free rate** | Yield on government securities of comparable (long) maturity on the valuation date; rupee flows → rupee yield |
| **Equity risk premium** | Estimated from long-run Indian returns, implied premiums or surveys; include country risk if starting from a mature-market premium |
| **Beta** | Regression against NSE/BSE indices; Blume adjustment; bottom-up from peers for unlisted firms (Chapter 7) |
| **Cost of debt** | Borrowing rate of comparable credit; Kd(1 − t) with the marginal tax rate |
| **Tax rate** | Statutory regime elected (old regime with deductions vs concessional regime), incentives and their expiry; terminal at marginal |
| **Growth** | Inflation plus real growth; terminal ≤ long-run nominal growth of the economy and ≤ the risk-free rate |
| **Currency and inflation** | Match currency of flows and discount rate; avoid discounting rupee flows at a dollar rate |

## 28.8 Promoter-controlled and family-run companies

Many Indian companies are controlled by promoters or families. Valuation implications:

1. **Related-party transactions** may not be at arm's length; normalise (Chapter 19) and consider *leakage*.
2. **Governance risk:** reflect specific risks in cash-flow scenarios rather than as a vague discount.
3. **Minority discounts** are context-dependent: in oppression or appraisal cases a minority holder is often entitled to a pro-rata share of the company's value (no minority discount), whereas in a negotiated sale of a small unlisted stake discounts for lack of control or marketability may be argued.
4. **Pledged shares, cross-holdings and group guarantees** affect risk and should be disclosed.
5. **Key-person risk** (Chapter 17).

## 28.9 Marketability and control in practice

| Adjustment | Typical contexts | Caution |
|------------|------------------|---------|
| **Control premium** | Acquisition of control from a minority base value | Premiums are deal-specific; include synergies |
| **Discount for lack of control (DLOC)** | Valuing a minority stake from a control value | DLOC = 1 − 1/(1 + control premium) |
| **Discount for lack of marketability (DLOM)** | Unlisted minority stakes | Not for valuing the whole company for a strategic buyer; support with evidence |

*Illustration.* DCF equity value (control basis) ₹1,000 crore; a 10% unlisted stake. With a 20% DLOC and 15% DLOM: 1,000 × 10% × 0.80 × 0.85 = **₹68 crore** (compared with ₹100 crore pro-rata). Applying both discounts multiplicatively (not additively) is the usual convention.

## 28.10 Documentation checklist for an Indian valuation report

1. Engagement terms and purpose; valuation date; premise and standard of value.
2. Sources of information and management representations.
3. Business and industry overview.
4. Historical financial analysis and normalisation.
5. Methodology: approaches considered, adopted and rejected, with reasons.
6. Assumptions: forecast, WACC build-up (Rf, ERP, beta, Kd, weights), terminal value.
7. Calculations and results; sensitivity; cross-checks.
8. Reconciliation and conclusion; range and point estimate.
9. Limitations; independence declaration; registration details.
10. Subsequent events considered.

## Chapter 28 summary

- Indian valuations are shaped by the Companies Act (registered valuers), SEBI rules, FEMA pricing, Income-tax Rules, Ind AS 113/36/103/102 and the IBC.
- Ind AS 113 fair value: market-participant exit price; Level 1–3 hierarchy; most business valuations are Level 3.
- Ind AS 36 value in use: DCF with restrictions (pre-tax rate; no future restructuring or enhancement).
- Promoter-controlled firms need normalisation of related-party flows and scenario treatment of governance risk.
- Discounts and premiums depend on the standard of value; apply with support.
