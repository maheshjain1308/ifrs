# Chapter 1 — The Conceptual Framework of Valuation

## 1.1 Why valuation matters

The basic purpose of any enterprise is to earn profits in order to sustain itself and promote growth. Managements everywhere — in a sole proprietorship or a multinational — work toward this. As an enterprise grows, so does the number of its stakeholders: shareholders, lenders, employees, suppliers, customers, regulators. Each of them, sooner or later, asks the same question: **what is this enterprise really worth?**

Corporate valuation has a long history. The (United) East India Company, often described as the first corporation to be valued and floated through an initial public offering, is also an early example of a corporatised way of doing world trade — and of an institution focused on wealth maximisation, though not always by ethical means. Today almost any enterprise that generates positive cash flow and employs people feels the need to know its "value", whether to approach the local bank for debt financing or to prepare for an initial public offering.

Valuation is not a single technique but a body of approaches developed by practitioners, analysts, intermediaries and academics. It has become an inseparable part of **strategic financial management**. In a true sense it imbibes both the **science** (mathematics, models, data) and the **art** (judgement about the future) of finance.

## 1.2 Reasons for valuing an enterprise

The need for a proper assessment of an enterprise's value typically arises for:

| # | Purpose | Explanation |
|---|---------|-------------|
| (a) | **Information for internal stakeholders** | Owners and managers want to know the worth of what they run |
| (b) | **Comparison with similar enterprises** | Understanding relative management efficiency |
| (c) | **Future public listing** | Setting an issue price; assessing readiness |
| (d) | **Strategic planning** | Finding the *value drivers* of the enterprise; deciding how to deploy surplus cash |
| (e) | **Ball-park price for acquisition** | An approximate price to start negotiations in a merger or acquisition |

The source chapter emphasises mergers and acquisitions: valuing the *target* is essential to gauge the price to be offered. A target may be **listed** or **unlisted**:

- If **unlisted**, the price is *negotiated* and must be acceptable to both parties.
- If **listed**, stock-market value (market capitalisation) forms a basis. But the market price is only a guide: it does not necessarily tell what the target is *worth*, because market prices depend on **market efficiency**, sentiment and liquidity. Also, in some cases only a small portion of total shares is quoted, so the price reflects a marginal slice of capital. This calls for further analysis.

## 1.3 Value, price and cost

Three words are often used interchangeably but must be kept apart.

- **Cost** — what was paid to acquire or build an asset (historical).
- **Price** — what the asset trades for in a market on a given day (observed).
- **Value** — what the asset is *worth* given the benefits it will produce and the risk of those benefits (estimated).

A balance-sheet figure is a *cost-based* number; a stock quote is a *price*; a DCF result is an *estimate of value*. Investing is the business of spotting when price and value diverge; valuation is the business of estimating the second.

> **Key idea.** Price is what you pay; value is what you get. A valuation is a *reasoned estimate*, expressed with an honest range, not a single "true" number.

## 1.4 Standards of value

The meaning of "value" depends on the purpose; professional practice therefore defines *standards of value*.

| Standard | Meaning | Typical use |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| **Fair market value** | Price at which property would change hands between a willing buyer and willing seller, neither compelled and both reasonably informed | Tax and legal contexts |
| **Fair value (reporting)** | Price to sell an asset or transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date | Financial reporting (Ind AS 113) |
| **Investment value** | Value to a specific investor given that investor's own synergies and required return | Acquisitions |
| **Intrinsic value** | Value implied by a rational analysis of fundamentals (present value of expected benefits) | DCF analysis |
| **Liquidation value** | Net proceeds from a break-up sale after paying liabilities | Distress, insolvency |
| **Going-concern value** | Value of a business expected to continue operating | Most valuations |

Always state **the standard of value** and **the premise** (going concern or liquidation) at the start of any valuation.

## 1.5 Who needs which kind of value?

The source chapter notes that fair value looks different to different observers: a chartered accountant would think of a transaction **at arm's length**; a financial analyst, of the **present value of an entity in cash terms**; a speculative investor, of the **arbitrage opportunities** that arise when similar entities carry dissimilar values. In upward boom periods valuations can defy fair value (the dot-com boom is the standard example): when the downturn arrived, some of those companies vanished and others barely held their ground.

## 1.6 Three families of approach

All valuation methods fall into three broad families. The source chapter calls them:

1. **Asset-based valuation models** — value from the balance sheet of assets less liabilities.
2. **Earning-based models** — value from the earning capacity of the business.
3. **Cash-flow-based models** — value from the free cash flows the business will generate, discounted at an appropriate rate.

In addition there are **relative valuation** (comparing with similar firms) and several **other approaches** (LBO, chop-shop, EVA, MVA, SVA). The next chapters take them one by one.

| Approach | What it asks | Strength | Weakness |
|----------|--------------|----------|----------|
| Asset-based | What are the net assets worth? | Objective; gives a floor | Ignores future earning power |
| Earnings-based | What are maintainable earnings worth? | Simple; market-linked | Needs a reliable multiple/capitalisation rate |
| Cash-flow-based | What is the present value of future free cash flows? | Most complete; intrinsic | Sensitive to assumptions (WACC, terminal value) |
| Relative | What do comparable assets sell for? | Reflects market conditions | Peers are never identical |

## 1.7 There is no single "correct" value

An important message of the source chapter is that, because every method has strengths and weaknesses, there is **no single answer** to which method is correct. It is better to determine a **range** of values — the **minimum acceptable to the seller** and the **maximum payable by the buyer** — and let the final price be settled by negotiation. Valuation supplies the boundaries of the negotiation, not its outcome.

## 1.8 The valuation process

1. **Define** the purpose, standard and premise of value, the valuation date and the interest being valued (100%, a minority stake, specific shares).
2. **Understand** the business, its industry and its competitive position.
3. **Gather and analyse** information: audited financial statements, tax returns, management projections, industry data, market data.
4. **Normalise** earnings and balance-sheet items (remove one-offs, non-operating items, related-party distortions).
5. **Select methods** and apply them (as many as the data allow).
6. **Reconcile** the results into a range; consider adjustments (control, marketability) where the standard of value requires.
7. **Report** with assumptions, limitations and sensitivity.

## 1.9 Information: the key to valuation

"Information holds the key to a successful valuation." The approaches in this book can be applied freely to *publicly traded* companies, where earnings, assets and the board's views on growth are readily available, and where audited statements are widely used by analysts to prepare review scorecards. In a developing economy such as India, many *privately held* firms in sunrise sectors (e-retail, hospitality, service management) attract venture capitalists and angel investors; directors approach chartered accountants for a valuation, and the qualified accountant is involved in the process. Valuing such firms without public information is demanding but, if done meticulously, yields real benefits — one reason why the cost-of-capital and beta issues of Chapter 7 matter.

## 1.10 Worked mini-example: three views of one company

*Anand Foods Ltd* has net assets (book) of ₹500 crore, maintainable annual profit after tax of ₹70 crore, and its shares are quoted such that market capitalisation is ₹620 crore.

- **Asset view:** ₹500 crore (book) — a floor, if assets are fairly stated.
- **Earnings view:** if a comparable company's P/E is 10 and Anand's shares are less liquid (adjust to 9), value ≈ 70 × 9 = ₹630 crore.
- **Market view:** ₹620 crore — but this reflects today's sentiment and the free float.

The three numbers are close, which gives comfort; if they were far apart (say ₹500 crore vs ₹900 crore), the valuer would have to explain *why* before choosing. This habit — **triangulation** — is the heart of professional valuation.

## 1.11 Common misconceptions

1. *"The balance sheet shows what the company is worth."* — It shows historical cost (adjusted for depreciation), not market worth or earning power.
2. *"The market price is the value."* — It is the price; it may be above or below value.
3. *"A higher valuation is always better."* — Not for a buyer; and a seller who insists on an unsupportable price loses credibility.
4. *"DCF is exact."* — It is exact arithmetic on uncertain assumptions.
5. *"One method is enough."* — Use several and reconcile.

## Chapter 1 summary

- Valuation serves internal information, comparison, listing, strategy and acquisition pricing.
- Value ≠ price ≠ cost; state the standard and premise of value.
- Three core approaches (asset, earnings, cash flow) plus relative and other approaches.
- Aim for a *range* — seller's minimum to buyer's maximum — not one number.
- Information quality determines valuation quality.
