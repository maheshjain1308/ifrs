# Chapter 15 — Valuation of Start-ups

## 15.1 Why start-ups are different

The three most common globally accepted methods of valuing a business are:

1. **Earning / cash-flow approach** — estimated cash flows for the foreseeable future are discounted to present value.
2. **Asset approach** — used when the business is not a going concern (liquidation, untimely losses); assets and liabilities are valued at current realisable value.
3. **Market approach** — values a business based on comparable companies in the same or similar industries, adjusted for their specific parameters.

One common feature of these approaches is that they **presuppose an established business that generates cash flows using its assets**. A start-up is not "established" in any sense, and its cash flows (if not already spent on marketing) cannot be assumed constant. **Profitability** seems to be a cursed word in start-up investor circles.

Start-up valuation is often required to bring in investment (equity or debt). The most significant differentiating factor is that there is **no historical data** on which future projections can be drawn. The value rests entirely on **future growth potential**, which in many cases depends on an **untested idea** not based on adequate sampling of consumer behaviour, and on the **competence, drive and self-belief** of the (often highly qualified) managers and their capacity to convert a promising idea into commercial success.

The major roadblock is the **absence of past performance indicators**: "founders walking in the dark and making the investors believe that they are wearing night-vision goggles." This is why valuation becomes critical: a professional helps investors navigate the dark **using facts, rather than fairy tales**.

## 15.2 Why traditional methods cannot be applied

| Method | Why it fails for a new-age start-up |
|--------|-------------------------------------|
| **Income approach** | A vast majority of start-ups operate on the assumption of **not generating positive cash flows in the foreseeable future** (a model now accepted by investors). With no or minimal positive cash flows, it is not easy to value the business correctly |
| **Asset approach** | (i) Start-ups have **negligible assets**; a large chunk of value is in **intellectual property and other intangibles**, which are hard to value and harder to agree with investors. (ii) Start-ups are new but usually operate under the **going-concern assumption**, so value should not be limited to the realisable value of assets today |
| **Market approach** | New-age start-ups are **disruptors**; they function in markets without established competitors, so numbers may be skewed and **not comparable** enough to form a base. However, elements of the market approach are used in **advanced funding rounds** |

## 15.3 Value drivers for start-ups

Every start-up is different, but the following drivers recur. Most are **highly subjective**, which is why standard methods (next section) are needed.

| Driver | Impact on valuation |
|--------|---------------------|
| **Product** | Uniqueness and readiness of the product/service; a functional prototype attracts higher value than an "idea"; market testing and customer response are sub-drivers |
| **Management** | More than half of Indian unicorn founders come from IIT/IIM; credentials and **balance of the team** (engineers + finance professionals + MBAs) matter; the profile of owners plays a crucial role |
| **Traction** | **Quantifiable evidence** that the product works and there is demand |
| **Revenue** | More revenue streams ⇒ more value; revenues are not mandatory but a better indicator than traction alone |
| **Industry attractiveness** | Logistics, distribution channels, customer base; a tourism start-up is worth less if lockdowns are expected |
| **Demand–supply** | If the industry is attractive there will be more investor demand, raising individual company value |
| **Competitiveness** | Fewer competitors ⇒ more value; first-mover advantage; but an existing proven model (e.g., Uber for Ola) makes it easier to convince investors, though it adds the burden of differentiation |

## 15.4 Methods for valuing start-ups

### 15.4.1 Berkus approach
Created by American venture capitalist and angel investor **Dave Berkus**, this approach values a start-up on a detailed assessment of **five key success factors**:

1. **Basic value** (soundness of the idea)
2. **Technology** (prototype)
3. **Execution** (quality of management)
4. **Strategic relationships** in its core market
5. **Production and consequent sales**

Each factor is assigned a monetary value up to a cap, and the sum is the valuation. The method **caps pre-revenue valuations at $2 million and post-revenue valuations at $2.5 million**. Although it ignores other market factors, its limited scope is useful for businesses looking for an uncomplicated tool.

*Worked example 15.1.* Using a five-factor scale with up to $0.4 m per factor (total $2 m pre-revenue): idea 0.35; prototype 0.30; management 0.25; relationships 0.10; early sales 0.00 → total **$1.0 million**.

### 15.4.2 Cost-to-duplicate approach
Takes into account **all costs and expenses** of the start-up and its product development, including purchase of physical assets; the sum is the fair market value. Criticised for **not focusing on future revenue projections or the assets** of the start-up. It gives a floor.

*Example.* Product development ₹2.4 crore + equipment ₹0.6 crore + legal/IP ₹0.2 crore + marketing and brand ₹0.8 crore = **₹4.0 crore**.

### 15.4.3 Comparable transactions method
Using the **market approach**, this is lucrative for investors because it is built on **precedent**: "How much were similar start-ups valued at?"

*Example from the source chapter.* **XYZ Ltd.**, a logistics start-up, was acquired for **₹560 crore**. It had **24 crore active users** — about ₹23 per user. **ABC Ltd.**, another logistics start-up, has **1.75 crore users**; at ₹23 per user, ABC's valuation ≈ **₹40 crore**.

*Computation:* 560 ÷ 24 = ₹23.33 per user; × 1.75 crore = ₹40.8 crore ≈ ₹40 crore.

"With any comparison model, one needs to factor in ratios or multipliers for anything that is a **differentiating factor**" — proprietary technology, intangibles, industry penetration, locational advantages. Depending on these, the multiplier may be adjusted (e.g., ABC's users are in lower-ARPU cities ⇒ 0.8× adjustment ⇒ ₹32.6 crore).

### 15.4.4 Scorecard valuation method
Another option for **pre-revenue** businesses; it compares the start-up with others already funded, with added criteria.

1. Find the **average pre-money valuation of comparable companies**.
2. Consider how the business stacks up on **qualities**, each with a weight:

| Quality | Weight |
|---------|-------:|
| Strength of the team | 0–30% |
| Size of the opportunity | 0–25% |
| Product or service | 0–15% |
| Competitive environment | 0–10% |
| Marketing, sales channels and partnerships | 0–10% |
| Need for additional investment | 0–5% |
| Others | 0–5% |

3. Assign each quality a **comparison percentage**: on par (100%), below average (<100%) or above average (>100%) versus competitors/industry. E.g., the marketing team has a 150% score (well trained, tested customer base): multiply its 10% weight by 150% to get a factor of **0.15**.
4. **Sum** the factors; multiply by the **average valuation in the sector** to get the pre-revenue valuation.

*Worked example 15.2.* Average pre-money valuation of comparable seed-stage companies: ₹10 crore.

| Quality | Weight | Comparison | Factor |
|---------|-------:|-----------:|-------:|
| Team | 30% | 125% | 0.375 |
| Opportunity | 25% | 110% | 0.275 |
| Product | 15% | 100% | 0.150 |
| Competition | 10% | 80% | 0.080 |
| Marketing/sales | 10% | 150% | 0.150 |
| Need for additional investment | 5% | 90% | 0.045 |
| Others | 5% | 100% | 0.050 |
| **Sum** | 100% | | **1.125** |

**Pre-revenue valuation = 1.125 × ₹10 crore = ₹11.25 crore.**

### 15.4.5 First Chicago method
Combines a **discounted cash flow approach** and a **market approach**: valuation is done for **three scenarios** — **worst case, normal case and best case** — each valued and multiplied by a **probability factor** to arrive at a **weighted average value**.

*Worked example 15.3.* Exit value in year 5 and probabilities; discount rate 25% (factor 0.3277):

| Scenario | Probability | Exit value (₹ crore) | PV | Weighted |
|----------|------------:|---------------------:|---:|---------:|
| Best | 20% | 1,000 | 327.7 | 65.5 |
| Normal | 50% | 300 | 98.3 | 49.2 |
| Worst | 30% | 10 | 3.3 | 1.0 |
| **Value today** | | | | **₹115.7 crore** |

### 15.4.6 Venture capital method
Venture capital firms made this method famous. Investors seek a return equal to some **multiple of their initial investment** or strive to achieve a specific **IRR** according to the perceived risk. The method uses the relevant time frame in **discounting a future value** attributable to the firm. The **post-money value** is calculated by discounting at the investor's required rate of return; the investor may seek 10×, 20×, 30× their original investment at exit.

**Post-money value = Expected exit value ÷ Required multiple (or ÷ (1 + IRR)ⁿ)**
**Pre-money value = Post-money value − Investment**
**Investor's required ownership = Investment ÷ Post-money value**

*Worked example 15.4.* Expected exit value in 5 years ₹300 crore. The investor puts in ₹10 crore and wants **10×**.
- Post-money = 300 ÷ 10 = **₹30 crore**; pre-money = 30 − 10 = **₹20 crore**.
- Ownership = 10 ÷ 30 = **33.3%**.
- Equivalent IRR = 10^(1/5) − 1 = **58.5%** a year.
If the investor expects 25% dilution by the founders' later rounds, it needs 33.3% ÷ 0.75 = **44.4%** today.

> **Pitfall.** A 10× or 58% "required return" is not a cost of capital: it embeds the high failure rate of start-ups. Mixing it with probability-weighted outcomes (as in the First Chicago method) double-counts risk.

## 15.5 Choosing a method

| Stage | Preferred methods |
|-------|-------------------|
| Idea / pre-revenue | Berkus, scorecard, cost-to-duplicate, comparable transactions |
| Early revenue | Scorecard, comparable transactions, VC method |
| Growth | VC method, First Chicago, DCF with scenarios, multiples (EV/revenue) |
| Late stage | DCF, multiples, precedent rounds |

Use more than one method and present a **range**.

## 15.6 The role of the professional

"While the traditional methods fall short, there is no shortage of new innovative methods used to value start-ups based on their value drivers. However, the valuation of a start-up is much more than the application of ways — it is about understanding the **story of the future trajectory** and communicating that narrative using **substantial numbers**." The valuer's job is to test the story against facts: market size, unit economics, team, traction, burn rate, runway.

## 15.7 Red flags

1. Revenue projections growing 10× with no cost of acquisition.
2. Market size defined top-down only ("1% of a huge market").
3. No evidence of traction.
4. Team without relevant experience.
5. Valuation requested based solely on the last round's price.
6. Cap-table complexity (liquidation preferences) ignored.

## Chapter 15 summary

- Traditional approaches presuppose established, cash-generating businesses; start-ups have no history, little assets and no comparables.
- Value drivers: product, management, traction, revenue, industry attractiveness, demand–supply, competitiveness.
- Methods: Berkus ($2m/$2.5m caps), cost-to-duplicate, comparable transactions (₹23 per user example), scorecard (weights 30/25/15/10/10/5/5), First Chicago (three scenarios), venture capital (post-money = exit ÷ multiple).
