# Chapter 17 — Valuation of Professional and Consultancy Firms

## 17.1 The nature of the business

**Professional services firms** can be defined as firms that provide **customised, knowledge-based services to clients** — chartered accountants, advocates, management consultancy firms, architects, doctors' practices, design agencies. Even within the industry, firms vary significantly because of the different nature of the services each provides.

Their economic characteristics make valuation special:

| Feature | Implication for valuation |
|---------|---------------------------|
| **Assets are people** — fee-earners, partners, relationships | Little balance-sheet value; the asset approach is of limited use |
| **Key-person dependence** | Value depends on whether clients and staff stay after a change in ownership |
| **Owner-managers** | Partners' remuneration mixes salary and profit; normalisation is critical |
| **Revenue is repeat or project-based** | Recurring work (audit, retainers) is worth more than one-off assignments |
| **Low capital intensity** | High return on capital; working capital in the form of receivables and unbilled work |
| **Cyclicality/regulation** | Demand for consulting is cyclical; regulated professions have rules on ownership |

## 17.2 The information used

As with any business valuation, understanding the **present and projected industry trends** plays a significant role in determining an accurate valuation amount. But experts generally look at the firm's **historical data** and compare it with **industry Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and benchmarks**. Valuation experts also compare the firm against its **competitors**. The main sources of information are **audited annual statements and income-tax returns** (and, for partnership firms, partners' capital and drawings accounts).

## 17.3 Projections and risk

When using the **income approach**, although historical data is important, **projected growth (terminal value) also impacts the overall value**. Valuation experts plan for future growth and compare it with projected trends after conversations with management, but there is an **inherent risk associated with using future earnings potential**, since results may or may not materialise. Hence this **risk should be factored into the overall calculation** — through a higher discount rate or a lower projection.

## 17.4 Normalisation of income and cash flows

In addition to analysis of financial statements and comparison with industry standards, **normalisation of net income and cash flows** is another important aspect. This step allows **comparison of firms on an equal footing**. It involves **adding back non-cash items and specific items that might not apply to a new firm**; the normalised cash flows are then applied to the chosen valuation method and used in calculating the overall value.

### Typical normalisation adjustments

| Adjustment | Reason |
|------------|--------|
| Replace partners' actual remuneration by **market-rate** pay for equivalent professionals | The buyer will have to hire or pay equivalent professionals |
| Add back personal, non-business expenses (vehicles, travel, club fees) | Not recurring for a new owner |
| Remove one-time fees (a large non-recurring assignment) | Not maintainable |
| Remove non-operating income (interest, rent from surplus property) | Valued separately |
| Convert cash-basis accounting to accrual (receivables, WIP) | Comparability |
| Adjust rent to market rates if premises are owned by a partner | Related-party |
| Provide for unrecorded liabilities (retirement dues, leave encashment) | Real obligations |

## 17.5 KPIs and benchmarking

"One commonly used method is to analyse the extent to which a firm meets expectations in comparison to **current industry benchmarks and KPIs**. Since professional services include several different types of firms, KPIs can vary greatly, and hence it is equally important to look at **specific indicators which align with the acquirer firm's goals**."

| KPI | Why it matters |
|-----|----------------|
| Revenue per partner / per professional | Productivity |
| **Utilisation (billable hours ÷ available hours)** | Efficiency |
| **Realisation rate** (fees collected ÷ standard fees) | Pricing power |
| **Leverage** (professionals per partner) | Profit per partner |
| Client concentration (% of fees from top 5 clients) | Risk |
| Recurring-revenue share | Stability |
| Staff attrition | Continuity |
| Receivable days; WIP days | Cash conversion |
| Profit margin before partner pay | Operating profitability |

## 17.5.1 The valuation methods

1. **Income approach — capitalisation or DCF** of normalised earnings (preferred).
2. **Market approach — multiples of revenue (fees) or of normalised EBITDA/earnings** from transactions of similar firms. Revenue multiples are common in practice (e.g., 0.8× to 1.5× annual recurring fees, depending on sector, recurring share and growth), but should be sanity-checked against earnings.
3. **Asset approach** — net tangible assets plus goodwill (excess earnings).
4. **Excess-earnings method** (capitalised "super profit"): value = net tangible assets + goodwill, where goodwill = (Normalised earnings − return on net tangible assets) ÷ capitalisation rate.

## 17.6 Worked example 17.1 — A chartered-accountancy firm

*Mehta & Associates* (₹ crore):

| | Reported | Normalised |
|---|---:|---:|
| Gross fees | 12.0 | 11.4 (removing a one-time ₹0.6 crore special assignment) |
| Operating costs excluding partner pay (staff, rent, etc.) | (6.0) | (6.0) |
| Partners' pay (4 partners × ₹1.0 crore) | (4.0) | (3.2) market rate ₹0.8 crore × 4 |
| **Profit before tax** | **2.0** | **2.2** |

*Calculation of the normalised figure:* 11.4 − 6.0 − 3.2 = **2.2**. Tax at 25% → after-tax maintainable earnings = 1.65.

**Capitalisation:** Because of key-person risk and a high concentration of fees (top five clients 40%), the valuer selects a required return of 25%:
Value of the practice = 1.65 ÷ 0.25 = **₹6.6 crore** (equity).

**Cross-check by revenue multiple:** 0.6× normalised fees = 6.8 (at the lower end of the typical 0.6–1.0× range for a practice with high concentration) ✔.

**Excess-earnings:** Net tangible assets (receivables, WIP, equipment) ₹1.5 crore; normal return 12% → 0.18; excess earnings = 1.65 − 0.18 = 1.47; capitalised at 25% → goodwill 5.88; total value = 1.5 + 5.88 = **₹7.4 crore**.

**Range:** ₹6.6–7.4 crore. With a retention agreement (earn-out over 3 years) for the two partners who control most clients, the buyer might pay **70% upfront** and 30% contingent on retention of 90% of fees.

## 17.7 Adjusting for key-person risk

- **Scenario analysis:** if two rainmaker partners leave, 30% of fees go over two years → value falls by about 25–35%.
- **Transition arrangements:** non-compete, non-solicitation, earn-outs, deferred consideration.
- **Discount rate:** a higher required return (e.g., 25% vs 18%) for key-person risk — or reflect the risk in cash-flow scenarios (preferred).

## 17.8 Special points for partnership firms in India

- **Partners' capital and drawings** and goodwill on admission/retirement (accounting) are different from market-based valuation but may be a starting point.
- **Profit-sharing ratios** determine who bears the value.
- **Regulation**: restrictions on ownership of professional firms (e.g., by non-professionals) limit the buyer universe and therefore value.
- **Tax:** personal taxation of partners; conversion to LLP; goodwill treatment.

## 17.9 Common pitfalls

1. Capitalising reported profit before replacing partners' pay with market salaries.
2. Ignoring client concentration and key-person dependence.
3. Applying a revenue multiple from a firm with a different recurring share.
4. Ignoring work-in-progress and receivable quality.
5. Using historical growth for a practice built on one-off assignments.

## Chapter 17 summary

- Professional firms are people businesses; value rests on client relationships and recurring revenue.
- Compare historical data with industry KPIs and benchmarks; sources are audited statements and tax returns.
- Normalise income and cash flows (partner pay, non-recurring, personal items) for comparison.
- Factor risk of future earnings into the discount rate or projections.
- Use KPIs aligned with the acquirer's goals.
