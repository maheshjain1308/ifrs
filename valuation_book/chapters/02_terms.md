# Chapter 2 — Important Terms Associated with Valuation

Before applying any valuation model one must master the vocabulary the models rest on. Some of these terms evolved over time and now stand on their own as the precursors to the models themselves. This chapter covers present value, internal rate of return, return on investment, the perpetual growth (Gordon) model and terminal value.

## 2.1 Present value (PV) of cash flows

A rupee received a year from now is worth less than a rupee today, because money today can be invested to earn a return — the **time value of money**.

**PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ**

where r is the discount rate and n the number of periods. The factor 1/(1 + r)ⁿ is the **discount factor**.

*Worked example 2.1.* ₹1,000 received twelve months from now, discounted at 10%: PV = 1,000 ÷ 1.10 = **₹909** (approximately). If received in two years: 1,000 ÷ 1.21 = ₹826.

**Why it matters.** Every cash-flow valuation is a sum of present values. The *choice of discount rate* is the central judgement: it must reflect the risk of the cash flows (Chapters 6–7).

### Annuities and perpetuities

| Pattern | Formula |
|---------|---------|
| Level payment C for n years (annuity) | PV = C × [1 − (1 + r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r |
| Level payment C for ever (perpetuity) | PV = C ÷ r |
| Payment growing at g for ever | PV = C₁ ÷ (r − g), r > g |

*Example.* ₹100 per year for 5 years at 10%: 100 × (1 − 0.6209) ÷ 0.10 = **₹379.08**.

## 2.2 Internal rate of return (IRR)

IRR is the discount rate that makes the present value of cash **inflows** equal to the present value of cash **outflows**; equivalently, the rate at which **NPV = 0**. It can be pictured as the discount rate that equates the PV of inflows to the investment.

*Worked example 2.2.* A project costs ₹800 and returns ₹250, ₹300, ₹350 and ₹400 at the end of years 1 to 4. At 11% the NPV is
−800 + 225.23 + 243.49 + 255.92 + 263.49 = **₹188.13**.
Increasing the rate to about 20.7% makes NPV zero; the **IRR is about 20.7%**.

> **Decision rule (source chapter).** The higher the IRR of a project, the more likely it is selected for further investment. (A project is acceptable if IRR exceeds the required return.)

**Limitations.** IRR (i) assumes interim cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself; (ii) ignores scale; (iii) can give multiple answers when cash flows change sign repeatedly. NPV is the safer criterion for ranking mutually exclusive projects.

## 2.3 Return on investment (ROI)

**ROI = (Return ÷ Investment)**, expressed as a percentage: the return over the investment made, from a stakeholder's point of view.

### 2.3.1 A simple standalone transaction
A stakeholder sells shares valued at 1,400 that he bought for 1,000:

ROI = (1,400 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = **40%**

This is the *absolute rate of return on cash investment* for a single transaction.

### 2.3.2 When holdings change during the year
If a stakeholder's holdings were worth 1,000 at the beginning of the year, he invested an additional 200 during the year, and the investment is valued at 2,000 at the end, his ROI is calculated on the **average investment** held during the year:

- Return = 2,000 − 1,200 = 800
- Average investment = (1,000 + 1,200) ÷ 2 = 1,100
- **ROI = 800 ÷ 1,100 = 72.7%** (the source chapter rounds to 72%)

The average cost of investments recognises the *timing* of the additional investment. A more precise stakeholder may weight by actual days of the additional investment; those with several investments and portfolios use the average-cost approach.

### 2.3.3 ROI from the entity's point of view
For a company the most significant use of ROI is to calculate the return generated by each individual or incremental investment or project. A company that has launched two new business lines during the year can compute ROI on each and decide whether to correct course or channel more funds into the more successful line. Note that **ROI is a historical ratio**: it supports corrective action, not prediction.

### 2.3.4 From ROI on projects to returns on total assets
A natural next question is how to measure returns against total investments — the total assets held by the enterprise. After all, all assets exist for wealth maximisation. This leads to the concepts of ROCE/ROIC and eventually EVA (Chapter 11).

## 2.4 Perpetual growth rate: the Gordon model

As in the cost-of-capital topic of the Intermediate paper, **Gordon's model** assumes dividends grow at a constant rate g forever. A potential investor looking for stable inflows takes the latest dividend, grows it, and relates it to the expected rate of return:

**P₀ = D₁ ÷ (Ke − g)**  (also written D₀(1 + g) ÷ (Ke − g))

*Worked example 2.3.* Next year's dividend ₹12, Ke = 13%, g = 5%: **P₀ = 12 ÷ 0.08 = ₹150.** If the market price were ₹180, the implied growth would be g = Ke − D₁/P = 13% − 6.67% = 6.33%.

### Why the Gordon model is rarely used directly
The source chapter notes that potential investors rarely use this model because:
1. there are more parameters to be factored in, and
2. dividends rarely grow perpetually at a steady rate.

It remains the darling of academicians because it fits neatly into a "constant rate" model for deliberation. Its real importance in valuation is as the engine of the **terminal value** (next section).

## 2.5 Terminal value (TV)

"Terminal" means the end of something. In the valuation world, to terminate is to exit from a particular investment or line of business. When an investor decides to pull out and book profits, he expects a fair value of the value created, and he also looks to the **horizon** to evaluate future cash flows, incorporating them into the selling price. Hence terminal value is also called **horizon value**: the value that an investor forecasts for his investment at the exit point.

**Estimating TV.** Mostly the perpetual growth (Gordon) model:

**TVₙ = CFₙ₊₁ ÷ (r − g) = CFₙ × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g)**

*Why TV matters.* In most DCF valuations it is a large share — often more than half — of total value; the growth rate and assumptions must therefore be tested carefully (Chapter 5).

### Illustration of TV
Free cash flows: Year 1 ₹120, Year 2 ₹140, Year 3 ₹160; WACC 10%; stable growth 4% afterwards.

- TV at the end of year 3 = 160 × 1.04 ÷ (0.10 − 0.04) = **₹2,773.3**
- Present value of TV = 2,773.3 ÷ 1.10³ = **₹2,083.7**
- PV of explicit flows = 109.09 + 115.70 + 120.21 = 345.0
- **Value = 345.0 + 2,083.7 = ₹2,428.7**

TV share = 2,083.7 ÷ 2,428.7 = **85.8%**. Such dominance is typical and explains why the growth assumption deserves most scrutiny.

> **Pitfall.** The TV is a value *at the end of year n* and must be discounted back to today by (1 + r)ⁿ. Adding an undiscounted TV to the PV of explicit flows overstates value.

## 2.6 Other basic ideas used throughout

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Going concern** | An enterprise is assumed to continue in operation for the foreseeable future |
| **Free cash flow (FCF)** | Cash left after operating costs, taxes and reinvestment |
| **WACC** | Weighted average cost of capital — blended required return of all capital providers |
| **Capitalisation rate** | Rate at which maintainable earnings are converted into value (reciprocal of a multiple) |
| **EBIT / EBITDA** | Operating profit before interest and tax / plus depreciation and amortisation |
| **Enterprise value (EV)** | Market value of equity plus debt minus cash |
| **P/E ratio** | Price per share ÷ earnings per share |
| **Beta** | Measure of a security's market (systematic) risk |

## Chapter 2 summary

- PV = FV/(1 + r)ⁿ; NPV sums PVs; IRR is the rate that gives NPV = 0.
- ROI is historical; use the *average* investment where capital changes during the year.
- Gordon model: P₀ = D₁/(Ke − g); a theoretical workhorse, rarely used raw.
- Terminal (horizon) value = value at exit; discount it to today.
