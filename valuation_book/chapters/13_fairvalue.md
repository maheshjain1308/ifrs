# Chapter 13 — Arriving at Fair Value; Going-Concern and Non-Going-Concern Valuation

## 13.1 What is "fair value" for an acquirer?

The ultimate purpose of a potential acquirer of a **controlling stake** or of the **takeover** of a company is that "he would purchase the same at the **fair price** — no less, no more". The approaches to valuation in this book, together with the various methods of value-added analysis (EVA, MVA, SVA), aim to identify entities that are **attractive in terms of true value** to a potential investor.

The same phrase means different things to different people:

| Perspective | What "fair value" means |
|-------------|------------------------|
| **Chartered accountant** | A transaction measured at **arm's length** |
| **Financial analyst** | The **present value of an entity in cash terms** |
| **Speculative investor** | The **arbitrage opportunities** that open up among similar entities that carry dissimilar values |
| **Financial reporting** | Exit price between market participants at the measurement date (Ind AS 113) |

In an upward boom, valuations defy fair value. The dot-com boom saw companies valued at astronomical sums; when the downturn arrived, some vanished and others were just able to stand their ground. Fair value is therefore anchored in *fundamentals*, not in momentum.

## 13.2 No single method is "correct"

"Though the methods have their own pros and cons, it depends on the **vision of the ultimate decision maker** which method is suitable for his/her purpose. Further, it can be said that there is **no single answer to the method of valuation as the correct one** and it will be better if a **range of values**, i.e., **minimum acceptable by seller and maximum payable by the buyer**, could be determined. Ultimately the final deal would depend on the **negotiation** among the parties."

### 13.2.1 Building the range

| Method | Typically indicates |
|--------|--------------------|
| Net realisable (liquidation) value | **Floor** — a seller will not accept less |
| Net asset value (book) | Lower reference |
| Capitalisation of earnings / P/E | **Minimum acceptable** to the target's shareholders (for P/E on a peer basis) |
| DCF | Intrinsic value for a going concern |
| Replaceable value | **Maximum an acquirer would pay** for equivalent assets |
| DCF with synergies | The buyer's *walk-away* price |

The **seller's minimum** is the maximum of (liquidation value, standalone going-concern value, next-best alternative). The **buyer's maximum** is the minimum of (replacement cost, standalone value + synergies it can realise and keep, its own financial capacity). A deal is possible only if the buyer's maximum exceeds the seller's minimum; the gap is the **bargaining zone**.

## 13.3 The answer-writing approach for valuation questions

The source chapter gives a three-point approach to questions involving *evaluation and synthesis*:

1. **Unless specified otherwise, calculate valuation by as many methods as possible with the available data.**
2. **Give comments on the valuation by each of these methods.**
3. **Supplement your conclusion with any additional information if available.**

The merger question on AB Ltd. and XY Ltd. (Chapter 21, Q5), where the merchant bankers recommend averaging the DCF value and the net-asset value, is a typical application.

## 13.4 The going-concern assumption

One of the basic accounting assumptions is that an enterprise is a **going concern** and will continue in operation for the foreseeable future. It is assumed that the enterprise has **neither the intention nor the need to liquidate or curtail materially the scale of its operations**; if such an intention or need exists, financial statements may have to be prepared on a different basis and, if so, the basis used needs to be disclosed.

The valuation of the assets of a business entity depends on this assumption. Traditionally **historical costing** is followed in the majority of cases.

## 13.5 Non-going-concern (liquidation) valuation

**Non-going-concern valuation** is also known as **liquidation valuation**, because it is the **net value realised after disposing of all assets and discharging all liabilities**. An on-going firm could continue to earn profits, which contribute to its value in addition to its liquidation value; the **going-concern value** is therefore known as the **total value**.

### 13.5.1 Why going-concern value is generally higher

1. When a business is acquired as a going concern, the buyer pays for the value of its assets **and** for the value of its **future profitability, intangible assets and goodwill**, so the acquired firm can charge a premium.
2. **Liquidation** not only implies laying off employees, but also creates a **feeling of bad reputation** among potential investors; it destroys organisation value and relationships.
3. Assets sold piecemeal or in a forced sale fetch less than their value in use.

> **Rule.** Valuation based on non-going-concern should be applied **only when investors are of the view that the firm has no longer value as a going concern.**

### 13.5.2 The relationship

**Going-concern value (total value) = Liquidation value + Value of going-concern premium (goodwill, intangibles, future profitability)**

*Worked example 13.1.* For Sunrise Textiles (Chapter 19): liquidation (NRV) value ₹680 crore; going-concern DCF value ₹761 crore; going-concern premium = 761 − 680 = **₹81 crore**. If the DCF were *lower* than NRV (say ₹600 crore) the rational action would be to liquidate — the business is worth more dead than alive.

### 13.5.3 When is liquidation value the right basis?

| Situation | Basis |
|-----------|-------|
| Business continuing with positive cash flows | Going concern |
| Business that will earn less than the return on its liquidation proceeds | Liquidation (consider selling) |
| Legal winding-up, insolvency liquidation | Liquidation |
| Finite-life project at end of life | Salvage/liquidation |
| Distressed but recoverable business | Both: probability-weighted (Chapter 14) |
| Accounting for impairment (Ind AS 36) | Higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use |

## 13.6 Liquidation: orderly versus forced

| | Orderly liquidation | Forced (distress) liquidation |
|--|---------------------|-----------------------------|
| Time | Sufficient time to market assets | Immediate; auction |
| Prices | Close to market | Heavy discounts |
| Costs | Moderate | High |
| Use | Planned wind-up; lender security assessment | Insolvency |

Valuation reports must state which premise applies.

## 13.7 A worked comparison

*Kaveri Plastics Ltd* (₹ lakh): Estimated going-concern DCF value of the enterprise: 900. Debt 400. Realisable value of assets (orderly): 780; liquidation expenses 40; liquidation (forced) discounts reduce assets to 600.

| | Orderly | Forced |
|---|---:|---:|
| Realisable assets | 780 | 600 |
| Less: liquidation costs | (40) | (40) |
| Net realisable value of the enterprise | 740 | 560 |
| Less: debt | (400) | (400) |
| **Equity — liquidation** | **340** | **160** |
| Equity — going concern (900 − 400) | 500 | 500 |
| **Going-concern premium over liquidation** | 160 | 340 |

The going-concern value of equity (500) is above liquidation value under both premises; the continuing business is preferable to closure, and the equity holders should not accept a price below ₹340–₹500 lakh depending on the strength of their alternatives.

## 13.8 Fair value and the bargaining zone: a numerical illustration

A seller values its business at ₹800 crore standalone (DCF), with a liquidation floor of ₹620 crore. A buyer's DCF with synergies is ₹930 crore; replacement cost for similar capacity is ₹1,050 crore; the buyer's financial capacity is ₹950 crore.

- **Seller's minimum** = max(620, 800) = **₹800 crore**.
- **Buyer's maximum** = min(1,050, 930, 950) = **₹930 crore**.
- **Bargaining zone** = 800 to 930 = ₹130 crore.

A price of ₹865 crore (the midpoint) would split the synergy gain equally. Negotiating strength (competition, alternatives, information) shifts the price within the zone.

## 13.9 Valuation date and information

Fair value is measured at a **specific date**; only information known or knowable at that date should be used. Subsequent events matter only to the extent they provide evidence about conditions at the valuation date.

## Chapter 13 summary

- Fair value is the price a buyer should pay — "no less, no more" — and its meaning depends on the viewpoint (arm's length, PV, arbitrage).
- No single method is correct; determine a range from seller's minimum to buyer's maximum.
- For synthesis questions: use as many methods as data allow, comment on each, add supplementary information.
- Going-concern value > liquidation value (future profitability, intangibles, goodwill; no reputational damage). Use liquidation value only when no going-concern value remains.
