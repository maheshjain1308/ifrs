# Chapter 8 — Relative Valuation

## 8.1 Intrinsic versus relative

The three approaches of Part II (asset, earnings, cash flow) arrive at the **intrinsic value** of an enterprise. **Relative valuation** arrives at a *relative* value using a **comparative analysis** with peers or similar enterprises. Increasingly, contemporary analysts use relative valuation in conjunction with the other approaches to **validate** the intrinsic value arrived at earlier.

**The concept.** One way to look at the practical implementation of fair value is to identify assets **similar to the ones held** by the acquiree so that values can be compared. This is a significant departure from intrinsic value. Trying to get a value nearest to the **market price** means that the valuation of a particular portfolio, or a divestiture in an entity, would happen at an agreeable price that fits into the **normal distribution**.

In one sense relative valuation is already used in a limited way whenever we speak of expected market returns or an index-based comparative: the more the pricing of an asset is correlated with similar assets in the market, the more inclusive it gets. When comparing bonds, the closer a bond's YTM to the government index of return, the more credible the pricing.

Relative valuation is also called **valuation by multiples**: it uses financial ratios to derive a desired metric (the *multiple*) and then compares it with that of **comparable firms** — those with similar asset and risk dispositions assumed to continue so over the comparison period. In the process there may be extrapolations to the desired range.

## 8.2 The four steps

1. Find the **drivers** that best represent the multiple.
2. Determine the results on the chosen driver(s) through **financial ratios**.
3. Find the **comparable firms** and carry out the comparative analysis.
4. **Iterate** the value of the firm obtained to smoothen out the deviations.

### Step 1 — Choose the driver (and so the type of multiple)

| Family | Multiples | Best used for |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| **Enterprise-value based** | **EV/EBITDA**, **EV/Invested capital**, **EV/Sales** | EV/EBITDA is the most popular and uses critical inputs; EV/Invested capital suits **capital-intensive** enterprises; EV/Sales suits companies that are **cash-rich, have a huge order book** and forecast organic growth through own capital |
| **Equity-value based** | **P/E ratio**, **PEG ratio** | P/E is the celebrated equity multiple; **PEG = P/E ÷ expected growth rate** is more suitable for relative valuation of **high-growth or sunrise industries** |

### Step 2 — Choose the right financial ratio
A **factor-based** approach helps. A firm earning most revenue from **exports** will be heavily influenced by foreign-exchange fluctuations; a pure P/E ratio may not reflect that (for instance, the impact that Brexit triggered on currency values). EV/Invested capital would be a **misfit** for a company that is light on core assets or has significant investment properties.

### Step 3 — Choose the right comparables
Perhaps the most challenging step: **no two entities can be the same**, even if operating within the same risk and opportunity perimeter. Software company *X* may seem comparable to similar-sized company *Y* — same capital structure, operating environment, head count — yet X may derive revenue from dedicated service contracts with **Full-Time Equivalent (FTE) pricing**, whereas Y earns through a **Unit Transfer Pricing (UTP)** model. This changes the risk structure dramatically — "take benchmarks with a pinch of salt".

For a **niche market** where comparables are scarce, one may look beyond the current operating market to similarly structured companies in other industries. Alternatively, take closely relevant firms and perform a **regression** to arrive at the comparable metrics.

### Step 4 — Iterate / extrapolate
Smooth out deviations and arrive at an estimate. Conclude: relative valuation is a **comparative-driven** approach that assumes the value of similar firms is a good indicator of the value of the tested firm.

## 8.3 Assumptions inherent in relative valuation

1. The **market is efficient** (it prices peers correctly on average).
2. The function between **fundamentals and multiples is linear**.
3. Comparable firms are **similar in structure, risk and growth pattern**.

Violations: bubbles and panics (market not efficient); non-linear value drivers (e.g., growth options); peers with different risk.

## 8.4 Enterprise value (EV): two ways

**EV = Market capitalisation + Debt (+ preference + minorities) − Cash and cash equivalents.**

The source chapter lists two approaches:
(a) take **entity value as the base** and adjust for debt values to arrive at EV; or
(b) take a **balance-sheet approach** and arrive at EV.

## 8.5 Illustration 2 — A Ltd. (per-share earning value and equity value)

*A Ltd. made a gross profit of ₹10,00,000 and incurred indirect expenses of ₹4,00,000. Issued equity shares 1,00,000. Debt ₹3,00,000; surplus funds ₹5,00,000. Risk-free rate 4.5%; market rate 12%; beta 0.9.*
Determine (a) per-share **earning value** and (b) **equity value** if the applicable EBITDA multiple is 5.

### Solution
**(a) Capitalisation rate by CAPM** = 4.5% + 0.9 × (12% − 4.5%) = 4.5% + 6.75% = **11.25%**.

| (₹ thousand) | |
|---|---:|
| Gross profit | 1,000 |
| Less: indirect expenses | (400) |
| **EBITDA** | **600** |
| Earning value of the company = 600 ÷ 0.1125 | 5,333.33 |
| Number of shares | 1,00,000 |
| **Earning value per share** | **₹53.33** |

**(b) Equity value using the EBITDA multiple of 5**

| (₹ thousand) | |
|---|---:|
| EBITDA | 600 |
| EBITDA multiple | 5 |
| Capitalised value (EV) | 3,000 |
| Less: debt | (300) |
| Add: surplus funds | 500 |
| **Equity value** | **3,200** |

### Commentary
- Method (a) values 600 at a CAPM-based capitalisation rate ⇒ an implied multiple of 1/0.1125 = 8.9×, hence a higher value (5,333) than the market multiple of 5× (3,000).
- In (b), the bridge from EV to equity subtracts debt and adds *surplus* funds — the standard treatment.
- The two estimates (₹53.33 per share by earning value; ₹32.00 per share by multiple) differ widely; a valuer would examine whether the 5× peer multiple fairly reflects this company's growth and risk, or whether 11.25% understates the required return.

## 8.6 Illustration 3 — H K Ltd. (Enterprise value from a balance sheet)

*The balance sheet of H K Ltd. (₹ thousand):*

| Assets | | Liabilities | |
|--------|---:|-------------|---:|
| Non-current assets | 1,000 | Shareholders' funds | 800 |
| Current assets: trade receivables | 500 | Long-term debt | 200 |
| Cash and cash equivalents | 500 | Current liabilities and provisions | 1,000 |
| **Total** | **2,000** | **Total** | **2,000** |

*Shares are actively traded; CMP ₹12 per share. Shareholders' funds represent 70,000 shares of ₹10 each and the rest is retained earnings. Calculate the EV.*

### Solution
| | |
|---|---:|
| Shares outstanding | 70,000 |
| CMP | ₹12 |
| **Market capitalisation** | **₹8,40,000** |
| Add: debt | ₹2,00,000 |
| Less: cash and cash equivalents | (₹5,00,000) |
| **Enterprise value** | **₹5,40,000** |

Note: current liabilities and provisions (₹10,00,000) are *operating* liabilities (payables, provisions) and are not treated as debt. Cash is netted because a buyer of the enterprise would also get the cash.

## 8.7 Choosing and adjusting multiples in practice

1. **Same definition across firms** — e.g., EBITDA after or before lease rent (Ind AS 116 effects), same treatment of exceptional items.
2. **Calendarise** to the same period (trailing 12 months or forward).
3. **Use a median** of several peers; examine outliers.
4. **Adjust** for differences: growth, margin, size, leverage, liquidity.
5. **Apply** to the subject's *normalised* metric.
6. **Move from EV to equity** with the bridge.

### 8.7.1 What drives a multiple?
A multiple is a compressed DCF. Higher growth, higher return on capital and lower risk all justify higher multiples. For a stable firm:
- P/E ≈ payout × (1 + g) ÷ (Ke − g);
- EV/EBITDA rises with margin and growth and falls with capital intensity and risk.
So before applying a peer's multiple, ask whether the peer's growth/risk justify it *for your company*.

## 8.8 Limitations

- Peers never perfectly comparable; "pinch of salt".
- Inherits market mispricing: if the sector is in a bubble, relative valuation reproduces it.
- Accounting differences distort ratios.
- Hides assumptions behind a single number.
- Not helpful for unique businesses or start-ups without comparables.

## 8.9 Relative valuation vs intrinsic: using both

A sound practice: do a DCF and a multiples analysis; compare the *implied multiple* from the DCF with the peer multiples (e.g., DCF EV ÷ EBITDA = 8.6× vs peers 6–7×). If they diverge, find out why — different growth? lower margin? A **reverse DCF** (solving for the growth or margin that the market price implies) is a neat way to see what the market believes.

## Chapter 8 summary

- Relative valuation = comparative analysis with similar firms using multiples; used to validate intrinsic value.
- Four steps: drivers → ratios → comparables → iterate.
- EV multiples (EV/EBITDA, EV/Invested capital, EV/Sales) and equity multiples (P/E, PEG).
- Assumptions: efficient markets, linearity, comparable structure and risk.
