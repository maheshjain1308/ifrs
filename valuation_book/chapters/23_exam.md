# Chapter 23 — Answering Valuation Questions: Technique and Model Theory Answers

## 23.1 The anatomy of a valuation question

Almost every question on this chapter falls into one of three kinds:

1. **Computational** — compute value by a named method (NAV, capitalisation, DCF, EVA, exchange ratio).
2. **Evaluation and synthesis** — value by several methods, comment, conclude with a range.
3. **Theory / short notes** — explain a concept (relative valuation, EVA vs MVA, distressed firms, start-ups, ESG, going concern).

A reliable approach to each follows.

## 23.2 Computational questions — a seven-point checklist

1. **Read the data twice**: units (₹ lakh, ₹ crore, ₹ thousand), tax rate, dates, "per share" vs total.
2. **Write the formula** before the numbers — examiners award marks for method.
3. **Normalise** when profit includes extraordinary items; gross up to PBT if the items are pre-tax.
4. **Use the right base**: after-tax profit for earnings approaches; EBIT(1 − t) for NOPAT; FCFF for DCF.
5. **Label each step** (e.g., "Step III — WACC").
6. **Discount properly**: terminal value at the horizon must be discounted; use the PV factors given.
7. **State the answer with units** and a one-line interpretation.

### 23.2.1 Common computational traps

| Trap | Example question | Correct approach |
|------|------------------|------------------|
| Forgetting preference dividend | Eagle Ltd. (Q2) | Deduct from earnings before dividing by equity shares |
| Not grossing up | Eagle Ltd. | PBT = PAT ÷ (1 − t) before removing extraordinary items |
| Using debt/total capital as D/E | Illustration 1 | "40:60" is D/E = 40/60 for beta; weights 40% and 60% in WACC |
| Book weights in WACC | Hansel Ltd. (Q6) | Convert to market values |
| Omitting unrecorded intangibles | Herbal Gyan, Constant Engineering (Q15–Q16) | Include in invested capital |
| Wrong share count | Partly-paid shares (Q5) | Convert to fully-paid equivalents |
| Terminal value not discounted | Two-stage DCF (Q7–Q8) | Multiply by the cumulative factor |
| Ignoring contingent liability | H Ltd. (Q4) | State the assumption and deduct |
| Mixing EV and equity | EBITDA-multiple questions | Bridge: EV − debt + surplus cash |

## 23.3 Evaluation and synthesis questions — the three-point approach

As the source chapter puts it:
1. Unless specified otherwise, calculate valuation by **as many methods as possible** with the available data.
2. **Give comments** on the valuation by each of these methods.
3. **Supplement your conclusion** with any additional information if available.

**Template.**

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| Method 1 (asset) | Calculation; comment: lower limit, ignores earning power |
| Method 2 (earnings) | Calculation; comment: depends on maintainable profit and rate |
| Method 3 (DCF) | Calculation; comment: sensitive to WACC and TV |
| Method 4 (market/relative) | Calculation; comment: peers' comparability |
| Summary | Table of values; range |
| Conclusion | Range; point estimate; seller's minimum, buyer's maximum |
| Additional information | Synergies, control premium, contingent liabilities, subsequent events |

## 23.4 Model answers to theory questions

### 23.4.1 Differentiate between EVA and MVA *(syllabus Theoretical Question 1)*

| Basis | Economic Value Added (EVA) | Market Value Added (MVA) |
|-------|----------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Meaning** | Excess of NOPAT over the capital charge (invested capital × WACC) — the *economic* profit of a period | Excess of the market value of the firm over the invested capital — the *wealth* created for investors |
| **Formula** | NOPAT − (Invested capital × WACC) | Market value of the firm − Invested capital |
| **Nature** | A **flow**, measured for a period; derived from accounting data (with adjustments) | A **stock**, measured at a point in time; derived from market prices |
| **Perspective** | Internal — performance of management; "derived value added" for the discerning investor | External — the market's perception of value created |
| **Link** | MVA equals the present value of future EVAs | |
| **Availability** | Can be computed for any business unit or unlisted company | Needs a market value (listed companies) |
| **Strengths** | Holds management accountable for capital; separates operations from financing | Reflects market's view of future prospects; includes value drivers beyond the accounts |
| **Limitations** | Historical; relies on accounting adjustments | Reflects sentiment; does not give a basis for share valuation; includes the effect of market volatility |
| **Short-term relationship** | Companies with high MVA may be "pricey" and show slightly negative correlation with EVA in the short term; the gap closes over time | |

**Illustration:** NOPAT 307, IC 920, WACC 8.44% → EVA 229.35; market value 1,000 → MVA 80.

### 23.4.2 Relative valuation *(syllabus Theoretical Question 2)*

*Relative valuation is the method to arrive at a "relative" value using a "comparative" analysis to its peers or similar enterprises. Elaborate.*

**Model answer.**
- The asset-based, earnings-based and cash-flow-based approaches arrive at the **intrinsic** value of an enterprise. **Relative valuation**, in contrast, arrives at a **relative** value by comparing the enterprise with **similar enterprises (peers)**, and is increasingly used with the other approaches to **validate** the intrinsic value.
- It uses **multiples** — financial ratios — derived from comparable firms (those with similar asset and risk dispositions) and applied to the subject.
- **Steps:** (1) find the **drivers** that best represent the multiple; (2) determine the results through **financial ratios**; (3) find the **comparable firms** and perform the comparative analysis; (4) **iterate** the value to smoothen deviations.
- **Types of multiples:** *enterprise-value based* — EV/EBITDA, EV/Invested capital, EV/Sales; *equity-value based* — P/E and PEG (more suitable for high-growth or sunrise industries).
- **Choosing the right ratio** is vital: e.g., a firm with export revenue is exposed to currency fluctuations, so a pure P/E may not reflect that; EV/Invested capital is a misfit for asset-light firms.
- **Choosing comparables** is the most challenging step: no two entities are the same — e.g., FTE pricing versus Unit Transfer Pricing software firms have different risk structures; for niche markets, look at similarly structured firms in other industries or use regression.
- **Assumptions:** efficient markets; a linear relationship between fundamentals and multiples; comparable firms are similar in structure, risk and growth.
- **Illustration:** earning value per share and equity value from an EBITDA multiple (Illustration 2); enterprise value from a balance sheet (Illustration 3).
- **Conclusion:** relative valuation is a comparative-driven approach that assumes the value of similar firms is a good indicator of the value of the tested firm — benchmarks must be taken "with a pinch of salt".

### 23.4.3 Going concern vs non-going concern
- **Going concern:** the enterprise is assumed to continue for the foreseeable future with no intention or need to liquidate or curtail materially; assets are valued on that basis (historical cost in accounts); value includes future profitability, intangibles and goodwill — the **total value**.
- **Non-going concern (liquidation):** net value realised after disposing of all assets and discharging all liabilities.
- **Relation:** going-concern value is generally greater than liquidation value (premium for future profitability, goodwill; liquidation brings job losses and reputational damage).
- **When to use liquidation value:** only when investors believe the firm has no longer value as a going concern.

### 23.4.4 Valuation of distressed companies
- Define distress (high debt, inability to meet operating expenses; fixed costs, illiquid assets, cyclical revenue); not necessarily worthless.
- Why conventional methods fail (infinite-life TV, negative cash flows, discount rates, going-concern assumption).
- Four methods: modified DCF (expected CF = CF × (1 − P)); DCF + distress value (equity = DCF × (1 − P) + distress value × P); APV (unlevered value + tax benefits − expected bankruptcy costs); relative valuation (e.g., 2× → 1.25× revenue).
- Illustration; liquidation floor.

### 23.4.5 Valuation of start-ups
- Traditional methods presuppose established business; start-ups lack history, assets, comparables.
- Value drivers: product, management, traction, revenue, industry attractiveness, demand–supply, competitiveness.
- Methods: Berkus ($2m/$2.5m caps), cost-to-duplicate, comparable transactions, scorecard, First Chicago, venture capital method.

### 23.4.6 Valuation of digital platforms
- Types (marketplace, search, repository, communication, community, payments).
- Income approach: top-down (TAM/SAM/SOM) or bottom-up; discount FCFF at WACC / FCFE at Ke; CSRP for nascent platforms.
- Market approach: revenue-driver multiples because earnings and book values are low or negative.
- Cost approach: rebuild cost of code (a floor).

### 23.4.7 Valuation of professional firms
- People-based; normalise net income and cash flows; compare with industry KPIs; factor risk in projections; use KPIs aligned with the acquirer.

### 23.4.8 Impact of ESG on valuation
- Define ESG; list factors; momentum (ESG funds, green bonds, taxonomies, single reporting standard, SEBI's Feb 2023 proposal).
- Incorporate in discount rate (practical but not explicit) or cash flows (explicit): E — 2-degree scenario, carbon points; S — cost of social measures; G — penalties, fines, taxes.
