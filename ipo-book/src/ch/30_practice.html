  • Amounts in ₹ crore unless stated; “lakh” = 1,00,000; “crore” = 1,00,00,000 (₹10 million)

A. Eligibility and structure

Problem 1 — Track-record test

Quanta Tools Ltd has the following restated, consolidated figures (₹ crore) for the three preceding full years: net tangible assets 4, 5, 12; of which monetary assets 3, 2, 5; operating profit 18, 9, 20; net worth 3, 4, 9. Can it undertake an IPO on the track-record route?

Solution. NTA exceeds ₹3 crore in all years ✔. Monetary share: 3 ÷ 4 = 75% (Year 1), 2 ÷ 5 = 40%, 5 ÷ 12 = 41.7%. Year 1 exceeds 50%, so the company needs firm commitments to use the excess monetary assets (or the IPO must be a pure OFS). Operating profit average = (18 + 9 + 20) ÷ 3 = 15.67 ≥ ₹15 crore ✔ and positive each year ✔. Net worth exceeds ₹1 crore ✔. Conclusion: eligible, subject to the Year 1 monetary-asset condition.
Problem 2 — Minimum offer to the public

Three companies expect post-IPO market capitalisation (at the IPO price) of ₹800 crore, ₹2,000 crore and ₹5,000 crore. What is the minimum offered to the public in each case?

Solution. ₹800 crore (up to ₹1,600 crore): 25% = ₹200 crore. ₹2,000 crore (between ₹1,600 and ₹4,000 crore): offer must be worth ₹400 crore = 400 ÷ 2,000 = 20%. ₹5,000 crore (above ₹4,000 crore): 10% = ₹500 crore. For the second and third companies the initial public holding is below 25%, so they must raise it to 25% within three years of listing.
Problem 3 — Promoters’ contribution

Post-IPO fully diluted capital will be 25 crore shares. The promoters hold 12 crore shares, of which 2 crore were acquired six months ago at ₹10 per share (IPO price ₹150) and 1 crore are pledged to a lender. How many shares are eligible, how many must be locked in for three years, and how many for one year (assume the promoters sell no shares in the IPO)?

Solution. Ineligible shares: acquired in the preceding year below the IPO price (2 crore) and pledged (1 crore) = 3 crore. Eligible = 12 − 3 = 9 crore. Required contribution = 20% × 25 = 5 crore, so eligibility is sufficient. Three-year lock-in: 5 crore. One-year lock-in: the remaining promoter shares = 12 − 5 = 7 crore. (The pledged shares need special attention because the lender’s rights must be reconciled with the lock-in.)
Problem 4 — Lock-in of a venture fund

A SEBI-registered Category I AIF bought 3 crore shares of the issuer eight months before the date of allotment in the IPO. For how long are its shares locked in after allotment?

Solution. AIF shares are locked in for one year from the date of purchase, not from allotment. Eight months have already passed, so the remaining lock-in is 12 − 8 = 4 months after allotment.
Problem 5 — Promoter group

Ravi is the promoter. (a) His brother holds 25% of Brookline Ltd. (b) His uncle holds 40% of Corner Foods Ltd. (c) Ravi’s spouse and a firm in which Ravi is a member hold 15% of Delta Traders each (total 30%). Which entities are in the promoter group, and which can only be group companies?

Solution. (a) Brookline: an immediate relative (brother) holds 20% or more → promoter group. (b) Corner Foods: an uncle is not an immediate relative (spouse, parent, brother, sister, child of the person or spouse) → not promoter group by this limb; it could be a group company if it had related-party transactions with the issuer in the disclosure period or the board considers it material. (c) Delta Traders: the promoter, immediate relatives and a firm in which he is a member together hold 30% (≥ 20%) → promoter group.

B. Allocation, anchors and bidding

Problem 6 — Splitting the offer

A company that does not meet the track-record conditions has a net offer of ₹600 crore. Compute the maximum or minimum allocation to each category, the maximum anchor portion, the mutual-fund reservation in the anchor portion, and the maximum number of anchor investors.

Solution. QIB at least 75% = ₹450 crore; NII at most 15% = ₹90 crore; retail at most 10% = ₹60 crore. Anchor up to 60% of QIB = 0.60 × 450 = ₹270 crore. One-third reserved for Indian mutual funds = ₹90 crore. ₹270 crore exceeds ₹250 crore: 5–15 anchors for the first ₹250 crore plus up to 10 more for the excess → maximum 25, minimum ₹5 crore each.
Problem 7 — Anchor price difference

Anchor investors were allocated 40 lakh shares at ₹250. Compute the additional payment if the final IPO price is ₹260, and the position if it is ₹240.

Solution. At ₹260: extra ₹10 × 40 lakh = ₹4 crore payable by the anchors. At ₹240: no refund; anchors keep the shares at ₹250 (a higher cost than the public pays by ₹10 × 40 lakh = ₹4 crore in aggregate).
Problem 8 — Bid options

In a band of ₹100–₹110, a bidder submits three options: 80 shares at ₹110, 100 shares at ₹108, and 120 shares at ₹106. (a) What amount is blocked? (b) What is considered for allotment if the Issue Price is ₹108? (c) If ₹110?

Solution. Values: 80 × 110 = 8,800; 100 × 108 = 10,800; 120 × 106 = 12,720. (a) The highest value, ₹12,720, is blocked. (b) At ₹108, options at or above ₹108 are the first two; the maximum number of shares is 100 at ₹108 = ₹10,800 (subject to allotment); ₹1,920 is released. (c) At ₹110, only the first option qualifies: 80 shares = ₹8,800.
Problem 9 — Price discovery

An offer of 50 lakh shares has a price band of ₹100–₹110. Bids (lakh shares) at each price: ₹110: 30; ₹108: 15; ₹106: 25; ₹104: 30; ₹102: 20; ₹100: 20. (a) Build the cumulative demand. (b) What is the highest price at which the offer is fully covered? (c) What coverage would ₹104 give?

Solution. Cumulative at or above: ₹110: 30; ₹108: 45; ₹106: 70; ₹104: 100; ₹102: 120; ₹100: 140. (b) Demand first exceeds 50 lakh at ₹106 (70 lakh, 1.4× coverage); at ₹108 only 45 lakh (0.9×). (c) At ₹104: 100 ÷ 50 = 2.0×.
Problem 10 — Green Shoe Option

The base IPO is 80 lakh shares at ₹150. The issuer has a Green Shoe Option for the maximum permitted over-allotment. The stabilising agent buys 9 lakh shares in the market. What is the over-allotment, the cash held for it, and how is the shortfall met?

Solution. Over-allotment = 15% × 80 = 12 lakh shares; cash raised for them = 12 lakh × ₹150 = ₹18 crore. 9 lakh shares are bought and returned to the lender; the shortfall of 12 − 9 = 3 lakh shares is met by fresh issue of equity shares by the issuer to the lender.
Problem 11 — Offer for sale expenses

Total expenses of an IPO are ₹36 crore. The IPO has a fresh issue of 4 crore shares and an OFS of 2 crore shares. Who bears what?

Solution. Total shares offered = 6 crore. Company share = 4 ÷ 6 = 2/3 → ₹24 crore. Selling shareholders = 2 ÷ 6 = 1/3 → ₹12 crore.

C. Other routes

Problem 12 — QIP pricing and allotment

Closing prices in the two weeks before a board meeting: Week 1 high ₹520, low ₹500; Week 2 high ₹512, low ₹496. The shareholders approved a 5% discount. (a) Compute the floor price and the lowest permissible price. (b) For a ₹300 crore QIP, state the minimum number of allottees, the maximum to one QIB and the minimum to mutual funds.

Solution. (a) Floor = (520 + 500 + 512 + 496) ÷ 4 = ₹507; with 5% discount = 507 × 0.95 = ₹481.65. (b) Size above ₹250 crore → at least five allottees; no single QIB above 50% = ₹150 crore; mutual funds at least 10% = ₹30 crore (if undersubscribed by mutual funds, the balance may go to other QIBs).
Problem 13 — Preferential issue

The shareholders’ resolution for a preferential issue is on 12 June 2019. (a) What is the relevant date? (b) By what date must allotment be completed? (c) If the 26-week average is ₹85 and the 2-week average ₹92, what is the minimum price?

Solution. (a) 30 days before 12 June 2019 = 13 May 2019. (b) 15 days after the resolution = 27 June 2019; otherwise a fresh special resolution is needed. (c) The higher of the two = ₹92.
Problem 14 — Rights issue

A company offers 2 new shares for every 5 held at ₹60; the market price is ₹90. Compute the theoretical ex-rights price, the value of the right per new share, and the cost to a holder of 500 shares who subscribes fully.

Solution. TERP = (5 × 90 + 2 × 60) ÷ 7 = 570 ÷ 7 = ₹81.43. Value of the right = 81.43 − 60 = ₹21.43 per new share. A holder of 500 shares is entitled to 500 × 2/5 = 200 new shares, costing 200 × 60 = ₹12,000.
Problem 15 — NCD timetable

NCDs are allotted on 10 January 2019 after a private placement. By which dates must the return of allotment be filed and the debenture trust deed be executed? Is an electronic book required for a ₹250 crore issue?

Solution. Return of allotment within 15 days → 25 January 2019 (the issuer may use net proceeds only after filing). Debenture trust deed within 60 days of allotment → 11 March 2019. For issues of ₹200 crore (₹2,000 million) or more the electronic book mechanism applies, so yes for ₹250 crore.
Problem 16 — InvIT eligibility

An InvIT holds assets of ₹1,200 crore, of which ₹950 crore are completed and revenue-generating and ₹250 crore are under construction. Can it make a public offer?

Solution. Revenue-generating share = 950 ÷ 1,200 = 79.2% (< 80%); under-construction = 250 ÷ 1,200 = 20.8% (> 10% cap). No — not for a public offer. Because under-construction investment exceeds 10%, units may be issued only by private placement (5 to 1,000 institutional investors and body corporates, minimum ₹1 crore each).

D. Governance, documents and calendars

Problem 17 — Board composition

A company has a board of 12. How many independent and non-executive directors does it need if (a) the chairman is an executive director; (b) the chairman is a non-executive, non-promoter director?

Solution. At least 50% non-executive in both cases: 6 of 12. (a) Executive chairman → at least half independent = 6 (independents count among the six non-executives). (b) Non-executive chairman unrelated to promoters → at least one-third independent = 4; the board needs at least two more non-executive directors (6 − 4) to meet the 50% non-executive test.
Problem 18 — Audit committee

The board constitutes a six-member audit committee. How many must be independent, and who chairs it?

Solution. Two-thirds independent: 6 × 2/3 = 4 independent directors. The chairman must be an independent director.
Problem 19 — Financial staleness

The DRHP is dated 1 October 2019 with audited financials to 31 March 2019. The issue opens on 20 December 2019. Are the financials adequate? What if the opening slips to 15 January 2020?

Solution. Statements to 31 March 2019 become more than six months old after 30 September 2019, so they are stale on 1 October. A stub period to 30 June 2019 or later is needed. Statements to 30 June 2019 are valid up to 31 December 2019, so a 20 December opening is fine, but 15 January 2020 is outside that window: the issuer needs statements to 30 September 2019 or later.
Problem 20 — Research black-out

The DRHP is filed on 10 October 2019 and the IPO is priced on 5 March 2020. (a) When does the research black-out begin? (b) When does the post-pricing black-out end?

Solution. (a) At 12.00 a.m. IST on the day before filing — 9 October 2019. (b) 40 days following the pricing day: 5 March 2020 + 40 days = 14 April 2020. (If a BRLM also underwrites, no report may be distributed for 25 days from the Issue Opening Date, and 15 days either side of any lock-up event.)

Chapter summary

  • Every rule in this book becomes an arithmetic or date calculation: eligibility ratios, dilution percentages, lock-ins, allocation splits, price coverage, pricing floors and statutory deadlines.
  • When solving, write the rule first, then substitute; most errors come from using the wrong base (post-IPO capital, QIB portion or total offer) or the wrong date convention.
