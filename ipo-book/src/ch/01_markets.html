What capital markets do

A capital market is the machinery through which savings become investment. Households, insurers, pension funds and foreign investors hold money they do not need today; businesses need money to build factories, hire people and develop products. Capital markets connect the two, and in doing so perform three jobs that no bank loan can perform alone.

First, they mobilise savings into productive assets, raising the long-term growth prospects of the economy. Second, they price risk continuously: the share price of a listed company is a running vote on its future, which allocates capital towards businesses investors believe in. Third, they give investors an exit. Because a share can be sold on an exchange, investors accept the risk of funding a business that will not return their money on a fixed date. That liquidity is the reason equity capital is available at all for long, uncertain projects.

India’s journey: from a closed economy to a deep market

The Indian economy changed fundamentally over a few decades. The 1980s were a closed economy; the 1990s were a decade of liberalisation; the 2000s saw unprecedented growth. The capital markets grew with the economy and, in the post-liberalisation era, became one of the primary destinations for domestic and global businesses seeking expansion capital. In fiscal 2019, Indian markets delivered double-digit returns despite global and domestic headwinds, outperforming developed markets such as the United States and the United Kingdom and developing economies such as China and Brazil.

The handbook attributes the strengthening of the markets to a cluster of mutually reinforcing factors:

  • a stable central government and certainty in policy making;
  • rising domestic savings and a growing economy;
  • a transparent and increasingly efficient regulatory system;
  • liberalised investment guidelines for banks, insurers, retail institutions and other sectors, which widened the range of investment choices;
  • growing foreign portfolio investor (FPI) participation, which added confidence; and
  • rising retail participation, much of it indirect through mutual funds and life insurance companies.

Large IPOs marketed to domestic investors played their own part: each successful large offering deepened the investor base and made the next issuer’s task easier.

Equity capital market activity in recent years

Key facts — fiscal 2018 and fiscal 2019
  • Fiscal 2018 was a record year: the highest ever resource mobilisation through IPOs and rights issues. Financial-services companies led, including the first issue by a reinsurance company, issues by insurers (including the first by a general insurer), asset management companies, commercial banks and non-banking finance companies.
  • 2018 also saw the listing of the first privately placed infrastructure investment trust (InvIT), followed by two public InvITs, creating a yield-focused asset class.
  • Fiscal 2019 was comparatively subdued. Market corrections, global volatility, trade wars, lender liquidity stress triggered by defaults and currency volatility all weighed on issuance.
  • Fiscal 2019 nevertheless produced India’s first listed real estate investment trust (the largest in Asia by office portfolio area), the first IPO by an asset management company, and offer documents for two of the largest rights issues ever.

The lesson for an issuer is that the market is cyclical. Windows open and close, and the timetable of an IPO — typically many months from kick-off to listing — must be planned so that a filed offer can use a favourable window when it appears. The validity of SEBI’s final observation (twelve months, covered in Chapter 18) is one of the tools that makes this possible.

The regulator’s direction of travel

Disclosure that looks international

A glance at a recent Indian offer document shows a close resemblance to international prospectuses. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) notified the SEBI (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations, 2018 (the “ICDR Regulations”), effective 10 November 2018, replacing the 2009 regulations. They reflect changes in market practice and in the regulatory environment in India and abroad. Two themes stand out:

  • Rationalised disclosure. SEBI adopted a materiality-based approach for items such as pending litigation and government approvals, so that disclosure is concise and meaningful rather than exhaustive.
  • Transparency throughout. SEBI emphasises transparency both in what the company and its promoters disclose and in the bidding process itself.

Stronger corporate governance

SEBI has also strengthened the governance framework for listed companies through a series of amendments. In June 2017 it constituted a committee under Mr Uday Kotak, which submitted its report in October 2017 with recommendations to revamp India’s corporate governance regime for listed companies. SEBI has been giving effect to these recommendations in phases, bringing more transparency, less concentration of power, fewer conflicts of interest and a broader disclosure regime. For an aspiring issuer this has a direct consequence: governance must be built before filing, not after listing (Chapter 20).

Why companies list

Companies go public for a mix of reasons. The handbook is neutral on motive, so the following is a practical summary rather than a restatement of the rules.

MotiveWhat listing provides
Growth capitalA fresh issue raises equity for expansion, debt reduction or working capital — subject to the objects being clearly disclosed (Chapter 16).
Liquidity for shareholdersAn offer for sale lets promoters, private-equity funds and early investors sell part of their holding (Chapter 8).
Valuation and currencyA traded share price gives an objective valuation and a currency for acquisitions and employee stock plans.
Access to further capitalListed companies can later use QIPs, rights issues, preferential issues and debt listings (Part VI).
Credibility and brandRegulated disclosure and independent oversight raise trust with customers, lenders and partners.
Regulatory complianceFor some regulated sectors, public shareholding is itself a regulatory objective.

What listing costs

  • Disclosure and scrutiny. Quarterly results within 45 days, annual accounts within 60 days of year end, and disclosure of price-sensitive events (Chapter 21).
  • Loss of privacy and some control. Related-party dealings, litigation, remuneration and shareholding are public. Special rights of select shareholders may have to be removed.
  • Governance obligations. Independent directors, committees, policies and a compliance calendar.
  • Lock-ins and dilution. Promoters must keep a minimum stake locked in, and a minimum percentage must be offered to the public.
  • Liability. Civil and criminal liability for misstatements in the offer document (Chapter 17).
  • Direct and indirect costs. Fees to advisers and intermediaries, management time, and the market’s short-term expectations.
Practical note — is the company ready?

Before engaging bankers, a founder can ask ten blunt questions. (1) Do we have three years of clean, auditable financials that can be restated? (2) Do we meet one of the two eligibility routes (Chapter 4)? (3) Is the promoter group clearly identified and are all promoter shares dematerialised? (4) Is the capital structure free of outstanding convertibles and special rights? (5) Are litigation, tax and regulatory matters mapped? (6) Do we have independent directors and a functioning audit committee? (7) Are related-party transactions at arm’s length and documented? (8) Is the use of proceeds specific and defensible? (9) Can management live with quarterly disclosure? (10) Is there a credible equity story? A “no” is not fatal, but each one is a workstream with a lead time.

Chapter summary

  • Capital markets turn savings into investment, price risk and give investors an exit.
  • India’s markets deepened on the back of policy stability, rising savings, regulatory efficiency, FPI and retail participation.
  • Fiscal 2018 was a record year for fundraising; fiscal 2019 was subdued but produced important firsts (first REIT, first AMC IPO).
  • SEBI’s ICDR Regulations 2018, effective 10 November 2018, favour materiality-based, international-standard disclosure; the Kotak Committee reshaped governance.
  • Listing brings capital, liquidity and credibility, at the cost of disclosure, governance obligations, lock-ins and liability.
