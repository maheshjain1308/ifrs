No concept in an IPO carries more consequences than “promoter”. Being named a promoter in an offer document affects lock-ins, liability, takeover obligations, disclosure and the ability to sell shares — and the label typically lasts long after the IPO. The regulations therefore define the term functionally, so that a person who in substance controls the company cannot avoid it.

Who is a promoter?

The SEBI Regulations and the Companies Act define a promoter in a deliberately subjective manner. A person is a promoter if they are any of the following:

  1. identified as a promoter in the offer document or named as such by the issuer in the annual return;
  2. a person who has control over the affairs of the issuer, directly or indirectly; or
  3. a person in accordance with whose advice, directions or instructions the board of directors is accustomed to act.

Exceptions. The third test does not catch directors or officers who give advice purely in their professional capacity, and promoter status does not attach to certain regulated institutions: financial institutions, scheduled commercial banks, FPIs (other than Category III FPIs), mutual funds, venture capital funds, AIFs, FVCIs and insurance companies.

“Control” in this context includes the right to appoint a majority of the directors, or to control the management and policy decisions of the company, whether by shareholding, management rights, shareholders’ agreements or voting agreements. A company may also voluntarily name someone as a promoter even if they have no control.

Promoter group

The promoter group extends the circle beyond the promoter personally. It matters because many consequences — disclosure, shareholding aggregation, ineligibility tests, public-shareholding calculations — apply to the whole group.

Where the promoter is an individual

  • The promoter’s immediate relatives: spouse, parent, brother, sister, child — of the promoter or of the spouse.
  • Any body corporate in which 20% or more of the equity share capital is held by the promoter or an immediate relative, or by a firm or Hindu undivided family in which the promoter or relatives are members.
  • Any body corporate in which a body corporate of the kind just described holds 20% or more of the equity capital.
  • Any firm or HUF in which the aggregate shareholding of the promoter and immediate relatives is 20% or more of the total capital.
  • All persons whose shareholding is aggregated under “shareholding of the promoter group” in the offer document.

Where the promoter is a body corporate

  • Its subsidiary or holding company.
  • A company in which it holds 20% or more of the equity share capital.
  • A company that holds 20% or more of its equity share capital.
  • Any body corporate in which a group of individuals or companies (or both), acting in concert, hold 20% or more of the equity — if that same group also holds 20% or more of the issuer.

Group companies

The third circle is group companies. They are not the promoter’s relatives or controlled entities; they are other companies the investor needs to know about. The regulations define them to include:

  • companies (other than promoters and subsidiaries) with which the issuer had related-party transactions, as covered by the applicable accounting standard, during the period for which financial information is disclosed in the offer document; and
  • other companies considered material by the issuer’s board.

Because the first limb depends on actual transactions, a company can enlarge its group-company list simply by dealing with a related party. Plan related-party arrangements early.

What must be disclosed about group companies

  • Date of incorporation and nature of activities, for all group companies.
  • For listed group companies, selected financials for the last three years: equity capital, reserves (excluding revaluation reserve), sales, profit after tax, earnings per share and diluted earnings per share, and net asset value.
  • If there are more than five listed group companies, the selected financials may be restricted to the five largest by market capitalisation.
  • If fewer than five are listed, give them for all listed group companies plus the largest unlisted ones by turnover, so that the total does not exceed five.
  • Pending litigation involving group companies that may materially affect the issuer.

Disclosure about promoters

The SEBI Regulations, the Companies Act and the rules require extensive information on promoters, including the securities they hold in the issuer and litigation involving them.

If the promoter is…Typical disclosure
An individualA complete profile: age, date of birth, educational qualifications, experience, past positions, other directorships, special achievements, financial activities, permanent account number, Aadhaar number and driving-licence number.
A companyA brief history (date of incorporation, changes in activities, present activities); history of the company’s own promoters; names of natural persons in control (holding 15% or more of voting rights) or on the board of the promoter company; details of any change of control, including who held the controlling interest in the preceding three years, and compliance with the Takeover Regulations.

Disciplinary action — including penalties imposed by SEBI or the stock exchanges against promoters in the last five fiscals, and any outstanding action — must also be disclosed.

What follows from being a promoter

Key facts — consequences of promoter status
  • Permanence. Once named a promoter in an offer document, an entity is treated as a promoter under the Takeover and Insider Trading Regulations, in annual reports and filings, and for later offerings, unless it is later “de-promoterised”.
  • Continuing disclosure. Promoters must make regular and quarterly disclosures of their shareholding.
  • Inter-se transfers. They may use the exemption for inter-se transfers between promoters if conditions are met.
  • Exit offer. If the objects of the issue are changed, or the terms of a contract referred to in the offer document are varied, promoters or controlling shareholders must give an exit offer to dissenting shareholders (Chapter 21).
  • Lock-in. Promoter shares are locked in (Chapter 7). Inter-se transfers and transfers to persons acquiring control under the Takeover Regulations are permitted; the lock-in continues in the new promoters’ hands.
  • Liability. Promoters may be held liable for misstatements in the RHP and Prospectus and carry other obligations under the Companies Act (Chapter 17).

Can a company list without a promoter?

Yes, but it is a question of fact. The test of promotership is control. Where shareholding is so dispersed that no shareholder or group controls the company, there may be no identifiable promoter, and the company is described as professionally managed. The handbook notes that there have been recent precedents of IPOs by companies with no identifiable promoters, especially in regulated sectors, but the determination is made case by case.

A lack of an identifiable promoter has knock-on effects: promoter lock-in does not apply (Chapter 7), and SEBI may look unfavourably on companies with “unidentified promoters”. In fact, SEBI can reject an offer document where unidentified promoters are among the reasons the investor cannot assess the risks (Chapter 16).

Practical note — decide who is a promoter deliberately

The label should be assigned after analysis, not after a negotiation. Typical judgement calls: whether a financial investor with board seats and veto rights has “control”; whether a founder who has sold down still “controls”; whether a corporate parent’s ultimate individual owners must be named. These choices determine lock-ins, who signs the offer document, and who bears liability. Ask counsel for a written view early.

Chapter summary

  • Promoter status is functional: named in the offer document or annual return, having control, or being someone the board is accustomed to follow.
  • The promoter group captures relatives and entities within 20% ownership links; group companies capture related-party counterparties and material companies.
  • Promoters face detailed disclosure, lock-in, continuing filings, exit-offer duties and liability for misstatements.
  • A company with truly dispersed ownership may list without an identifiable promoter, but this is decided case by case.
