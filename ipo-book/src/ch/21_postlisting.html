Listing is not the finish line. From the day the shares begin trading, the company is under a continuous disclosure regime and is accountable to public shareholders, to the stock exchanges and to SEBI. The shift in tempo — from a few board meetings a year to quarterly results, event disclosures and constant scrutiny — is one of the biggest adjustments for new listed companies.

Periodic reporting

A listed company must report its quarterly shareholding pattern and its quarterly and annual financial statements to the stock exchanges. Under Regulation 33 of the SEBI Listing Regulations:

Worked example — a deadline calendar for a March year-end company

Fiscal ends 31 March. Q1 (April–June): results due by 14 August (45 days after 30 June). Q2 (July–September): by 14 November. Q3 (October–December): by 14 February. Q4 and annual (to 31 March): by 30 May (60 days). The shareholding pattern is filed every quarter. A newly listed company therefore faces its first quarterly deadline soon after listing; the finance team should dry-run the close process during the IPO period.

Event-based disclosure

The Listing Regulations require listed companies to disclose three categories of events:

  1. Deemed material events — events that the regulations treat as material without further assessment (for example, acquisitions, change in directors or key management, rating changes and outcomes of board meetings);
  2. Mandatory disclosures — items that must be disclosed in prescribed form and within prescribed times; and
  3. Other disclosures the board considers material under its materiality policy.

Insider trading

All listed companies, and companies about to list, must comply with the SEBI Insider Trading Regulations in respect of unpublished price sensitive information (UPSI) — that is, information about the company that is not generally available and that would likely affect the price of its securities. Each listed company must adopt two documents:

  • a Code of Conduct for Regulating, Monitoring and Reporting of Trading by Insiders; and
  • a Code of Practices and Procedures for Fair Disclosure of Unpublished Price Sensitive Information.

In practice these codes impose trading windows, pre-clearance requirements for designated persons, restricted lists and a structured digital database of persons with access to UPSI. Promoters and others must also make regular disclosures of their holdings (see below).

Changing the objects of the IPO

Money raised in an IPO must be used for the objects described in the Prospectus. If the issuer later wants to change these objects:

  1. it must obtain shareholders’ approval by special resolution passed through a postal ballot; and
  2. the promoters or controlling shareholders must give an exit opportunity to the dissenting shareholders in accordance with Schedule XX of the SEBI Regulations.

The same exit-offer principle applies where the terms of a contract referred to in the offer document are varied. Where the monitoring agency applies (fresh issue above ₹100 crore), its reports track utilisation against objects.

Minimum public shareholding

If the public shareholding after listing is below 25% — as will be the case for large issuers allowed to dilute less (Chapter 5) — the issuer must raise it to 25% within three years of listing through methods specified by SEBI (such as an OFS by promoters, a QIP by promoter-group sell-down, or an FPO).

Promoters after listing

  • Regular and quarterly shareholding disclosures under the Takeover and Insider Trading Regulations.
  • Lock-in compliance — locked shares cannot be sold or pledged except as permitted (Chapter 7).
  • The label of “promoter” persists until a person is formally de-promoterised.
  • Future offerings by the company carry the promoters’ continuing obligations.

Practical note — expect scrutiny after the IPO

In the months after listing, the first results are read against the offer-document narrative, the use of proceeds is tracked by the monitoring agency and analysts, and lock-in expiries are anticipated by the market. Treat the first two or three quarters as part of the IPO’s credibility: consistent delivery against the prospectus builds the base for future QIPs and rights issues.

Chapter summary

  • Quarterly results within 45 days (60 days for the last quarter and annual accounts); quarterly shareholding patterns.
  • Deemed material events, mandatory disclosures and board-determined material events must be disclosed promptly.
  • Two insider-trading codes are mandatory.
  • Changing the IPO objects needs a special resolution by postal ballot and an exit opportunity for dissenters.
  • Public shareholding must reach 25% within three years where initially lower; promoters keep disclosure and lock-in obligations.
