An IPO is not sold to “the public” as a single pool. Investors are divided into three categories, and the offer is divided among them in fixed proportions. The division serves two purposes: it protects small investors by guaranteeing them a share of the offer, and it ensures that institutions with the ability to analyse a company are present in the book.

The three categories

CategoryWhoKey feature
Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIBs)Public financial institutions; FPIs; scheduled commercial banks; SEBI-registered mutual funds, VCFs, AIFs and FVCIs; state industrial development corporations; insurance companies registered with IRDAI; provident funds and pension funds with a corpus of at least ₹250 million (₹25 crore); multilateral and bilateral development financial institutions; the National Investment Fund; insurance funds of the armed forces and the Department of PostsSophisticated; may bid through the syndicate; only category that can be an anchor investor
Non-Institutional Investors (NIIs)All bidders that are neither QIBs nor retail individual investorsHigh-net-worth individuals, companies, trusts and similar bidders above the retail limit
Retail Individual Investors (RIIs)Individuals, including Hindu undivided families and non-resident Indians, who bid for a value of not more than ₹0.2 million (₹2 lakh) in any of the bidding optionsMust apply through the ASBA process; guaranteed minimum share of the offer in the standard regime

Two allocation regimes

How the net offer is divided depends on whether the issuer meets the track-record eligibility conditions explained in Chapter 4.

{{FIG:alloc}}
Retail (RII)Non-institutional (NII)QIB
Issuer meets the track-record conditions (book-built IPO)Not less than 35%Not less than 15%Not more than 50%
Issuer does not meet themNot more than 10%Not more than 15%Not less than 75%

Within the QIB category, 5% is allocated to registered mutual funds, and the balance is allocated to all QIBs, including mutual funds. Mutual funds can therefore compete for the balance, so their total share can exceed 5%.

Worked example — splitting a ₹1,000 crore net offer

Regime 1 (meets track-record conditions), maximum QIB share. QIB 50% = ₹500 crore. NII at least 15% = ₹150 crore. Retail at least 35% = ₹350 crore. (50 + 15 + 35 = 100.)

Inside the ₹500 crore QIB portion. Up to 60% may go to anchor investors: 0.60 × 500 = ₹300 crore, of which one-third (₹100 crore) is reserved for Indian mutual funds. The remaining ₹200 crore is the net QIB portion. Of it, 5% (₹10 crore) is reserved for mutual funds, and the balance ₹190 crore goes to all QIBs including mutual funds.

Regime 2 (does not meet the conditions). QIB at least 75% = ₹750 crore. NII up to 15% = ₹150 crore. Retail up to 10% = ₹100 crore. (75 + 15 + 10 = 100.)

The one-third reservation for mutual funds in the anchor portion, and the 5% reservation in the net QIB portion, mean that mutual funds are structurally guaranteed access to the QIB category.

How shares are allotted within each category

  • QIBs and NIIs: proportionate. If a category is oversubscribed, each valid bidder receives shares in proportion to the quantity bid.
  • Retail: minimum lot first. Each retail bidder is allotted the minimum bid lot, subject to availability, and thereafter the balance is allotted proportionately to those who bid for more.
Worked example — oversubscribed proportionate allotment (NII)

The NII portion is 30 lakh shares. Valid NII bids at or above the issue price total 90 lakh shares — a 3× subscription. A bidder who applied for 6,000 shares is allotted, on a proportionate basis, 6,000 × (30 ÷ 90) = 2,000 shares. Final rounding is done by the registrar in consultation with the exchange as part of the basis of allotment.

Practical note — what retail “minimum lot” means in practice

In a heavily oversubscribed retail category, there may be fewer shares than lots needed to give every applicant one lot. The detailed procedure for that situation is in the regulations’ allotment schedule, which is not summarised in the handbook; in practice successful applicants are chosen by a draw among valid applications. Retail investors should therefore expect all-or-nothing outcomes at high subscription levels, rather than a small partial allotment.

Since 2019

The non-institutional category has since been split into two sub-categories, by size of bid; the retail limit remains ₹2 lakh. Also, reservations for employees and for shareholders of a listed parent are common features of larger IPOs, though not covered in this handbook. See Chapter 29.

Chapter summary

  • Investors fall into QIBs, NIIs and RIIs; retail means bids up to ₹2 lakh.
  • Issuers meeting the track-record conditions give retail at least 35%, NII at least 15% and QIBs at most 50%; others give QIBs at least 75%, NII at most 15% and retail at most 10%.
  • Inside the QIB category, 5% is for mutual funds, and up to 60% of the QIB portion may go to anchors.
  • QIB and NII allotments are proportionate; retail gets minimum lots first.
