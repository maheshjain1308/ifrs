What is a rights issue?

A Rights Issue is an offer of equity shares or other securities by a company to its existing shareholders as on a specified record date. The shareholder’s “right” is to subscribe to new shares in a fixed ratio to existing holdings — for example, one new share for every four held. It is a way for a company to raise capital without bringing in new shareholders; and, because each existing holder can subscribe in proportion, there is no dilution for those who exercise their rights.

Rights are normally renounceable: a shareholder who does not wish to subscribe can sell the entitlement in the market. This makes rights issues useful for large, balance-sheet-repairing capital raises, when the issuer wants to keep control with existing holders, including the promoter, while giving minorities a fair chance to participate.

The fast-track route

An issuer that complies with the criteria in the SEBI Regulations may use the fast-track mode. In a fast-track issue, the Draft Letter of Offer is not required to be filed with SEBI; the issuer files the Letter of Offer directly with the stock exchanges. This saves months of review. The handbook lists the conditions:

#Condition
1Listing history: equity shares listed on a stock exchange for at least three years before the date of filing the Letter of Offer with the designated exchange (the “Reference Date”)
2Promoter group demat: the entire shareholding of the promoter group held in dematerialised form on the Reference Date
3Market capitalisation: average market capitalisation of the public shareholding at least ₹2,500 million (₹250 crore)
4Trading turnover: annualised trading turnover during the six calendar months before the Reference Date month at least 2% of the weighted average number of shares listed in that period
5Delivery-based turnover: annualised delivery-based trading turnover in the same six months at least 10% of the annualised trading turnover
6Listing compliance: compliance with the listing agreement or SEBI Listing Regulations for at least three years before the Reference Date (lapses on board composition are deemed cured if disclosed and rectified by filing; a monetary fine alone is not a ground for ineligibility)
7No suspension from trading as a disciplinary measure in the last three years
8No SEBI proceedings: no prosecution or show-cause notices pending against the issuer, its promoters or whole-time directors on the Reference Date
9Investor grievances: at least 95% of investor complaints received up to the end of the previous quarter redressed
10No settlements: no consent or settlement of alleged securities-law violations by the issuer, promoters, promoter group or directors in the last three years
11No conflict of interest between the BRLMs and the issuer or its group companies
12Promoters must subscribe to their portion (except for renunciation within the promoter group or to meet minimum public shareholding)
13No auditor qualifications on the audited accounts for the fiscals disclosed in the Letter of Offer

These conditions identify a stable, liquid, well-governed listed company that need not be re-reviewed by SEBI. Failing any one pushes the issuer onto the ordinary route, where a Draft Letter of Offer is filed with SEBI first.

Limited disclosure

Even outside the fast-track route, an issuer can provide limited disclosures in its Draft Letter of Offer and Letter of Offer if it satisfies all of the following:

  1. it has been filing the periodic reports, statements and information required under the listing agreement or SEBI Listing Regulations for the three years before filing the Draft Letter of Offer with SEBI or the Letter of Offer with the designated exchange;
  2. those reports are available on the website of a stock exchange;
  3. it has an investor grievance-handling mechanism — frequent meetings of the stakeholders’ relationship committee, appropriate delegation of power on share transfers and clear systems for timely redressal;
  4. its management has not changed pursuant to an acquisition of control under the Takeover Regulations (and it is not undertaking its first rights issue after such a change); and
  5. its shares have not been listed under a relaxation granted by SEBI under the SCRR pursuant to a court-sanctioned scheme (and it is not undertaking its first rights issue after such a listing).

The logic is that investors in such a company already have a continuous stream of public information, so the offer document does not need to repeat it.

Pricing

There are no specific pricing requirements for a rights issue. The board of directors decides the price, in consultation with the BRLMs (or lead managers). In practice rights are priced at a discount to the market price to induce subscription.

Worked example — rights ratio and value of a right

Horizon Power Ltd announces a rights issue of 1 new share for every 4 held at ₹80 per share. The market price before the announcement is ₹100.

Theoretical ex-rights price (TERP) = (4 × ₹100 + 1 × ₹80) ÷ 5 = ₹480 ÷ 5 = ₹96.
Value of one right (the per-share entitlement) = TERP − issue price = 96 − 80 = ₹16 for each new share the right entitles the holder to buy.

A shareholder with 1,000 shares can subscribe to 250 new shares, paying 250 × ₹80 = ₹20,000. The holding becomes 1,250 shares worth 1,250 × ₹96 = ₹1,20,000 on a theoretical basis, against an investment of ₹1,00,000 (market value of the original shares) + ₹20,000 = ₹1,20,000. A holder who does not subscribe can sell the entitlement and receive approximately ₹16 × 250 = ₹4,000, which compensates for the fall in the share price from ₹100 to ₹96.

Practical note — rights versus other routes

A rights issue treats all shareholders alike and is often the preferred route where the promoter wants to maintain control and public investors are happy to follow. Its risks: success depends on the market price staying above the issue price during the offer, and large issues need underwriting or promoter backstops. The handbook notes that fiscal 2019 saw filings for two of the largest rights issues ever.

Chapter summary

  • A rights issue offers new shares to existing holders in a fixed ratio; entitlements are normally tradable.
  • Fast-track issuers skip SEBI’s review and file the Letter of Offer with the exchanges, if they satisfy thirteen conditions on listing history, liquidity, compliance and governance.
  • Limited-disclosure documents are allowed for companies with a clean three-year disclosure record.
  • There is no regulatory pricing formula; the board sets the price with the BRLMs.
