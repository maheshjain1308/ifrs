What is being sold?

An IPO can consist of three kinds of offer, each with a different destination for the money.

StructureWho sellsWhere the money goesEffect on shareholding
Fresh issueThe company issues new sharesTo the company, for its stated objectsTotal shares increase; existing holders are diluted
Offer for Sale (OFS)Existing shareholders sell shares they already holdTo the selling shareholdersTotal shares unchanged; holdings move from sellers to public
CombinationBothSplit between company and sellersBoth effects

The “minimum percentage offered to the public” rule below counts shares offered through a fresh issue, an OFS, or the two together.

Minimum dilution: how much must be offered?

A listed company should have a real public float. The regulations therefore require a minimum percentage of each class of equity shares to be offered to the public. The percentage depends on the size of the post-IPO equity capital, measured at the IPO price — in effect, the post-offer market capitalisation.

Minimum offer to the public (as at 31 July 2019)
Post-IPO equity capital at the IPO priceMinimum offered to the public
Up to ₹16,000 million (₹1,600 crore)25% of each class of equity shares
More than ₹1,600 crore, up to ₹4,000 croreSuch percentage as equals ₹4,000 million (₹400 crore)
₹4,000 crore (₹40,000 million) or more10% of each class of equity shares

“Public” means everyone other than the promoters, the promoter group, and the subsidiaries and associates of the issuer. Shares in the hands of those persons never count towards the public float.

Worked example — three issuers, three tiers

Issuer A — post-IPO market value ₹1,000 crore. It is below ₹1,600 crore, so at least 25% must be offered: 0.25 × 1,000 = ₹250 crore.

Issuer B — post-IPO market value ₹2,500 crore. It lies in the middle band, so the offer must be worth ₹400 crore. As a percentage: 400 ÷ 2,500 = 16%.

Issuer C — post-IPO market value ₹6,000 crore. It is above ₹4,000 crore, so 10% suffices: 0.10 × 6,000 = ₹600 crore.

Notice that the rule does not make larger companies offer less in absolute terms. Issuer C offers ₹600 crore against Issuer A’s ₹250 crore; only the percentage falls as size rises. The sliding scale recognises that a 25% sale of a very large company could be more than the market can absorb at one time.

If the initial public holding is below 25%

Companies in the second and third tiers will end up with public shareholding below 25%. The rule is that the issuer must raise public shareholding to 25% within three years from the date of listing, in the manner specified by SEBI. This ties the IPO to the continuing listing requirement of minimum public shareholding, discussed in Chapter 21.

Pre-IPO placement

Companies sometimes want to raise a final round of capital from selected investors before the public offer — to strengthen the order book, show market validation or reduce the size of the public issue. The handbook defines this precisely: a pre-IPO placement is an issuance of equity shares, or securities convertible into equity shares, after filing of the DRHP with SEBI and before filing of the RHP.

  • The DRHP must disclose the maximum number of shares (including shares to be issued on conversion) or the maximum amount that may be raised in such a placement.
  • Convertible securities issued in a pre-IPO placement must be converted into equity before the RHP is filed — consistent with the bar on outstanding convertibles (Chapter 4).

Corporate approvals

An IPO needs authority at several levels. Missing any one of them can delay the filing.

LevelWhat is needed
Board of directorsAuthorisation to undertake the issue of equity shares (for a fresh issue) and to approve the offer documents. For an OFS, the issuer’s board must approve it in addition to whatever the selling shareholders need.
ShareholdersA special resolution if the IPO involves a fresh issue of shares (a special resolution requires a 75% majority of votes cast).
Selling shareholdersTheir own corporate authorisations under applicable law, where they are companies, funds or trusts.
Regulators and ministriesIn certain cases, approvals from the RBI and relevant ministries for issues to non-residents; approval of a sector regulator (for example IRDAI for an insurer).
SEBI and exchangesSEBI’s observations on the DRHP; in-principle approval and then final listing and trading approval from the stock exchanges.
Third partiesConsents under financing documents, shareholder agreements and joint-venture agreements — often overlooked until a lender points out a negative covenant.
Practical note — the consents calendar

Lender consents, JV partner consents and shareholder-agreement waivers are typically the slowest items because they depend on third parties. Start a “consents tracker” at the kick-off meeting. Also check whether any shareholder agreement terminates on listing, and whether special rights need to be deleted from the articles before filing (Chapter 20).

Chapter summary

  • An IPO may be a fresh issue, an OFS, or both; only a fresh issue brings money to the company.
  • Minimum offer: 25% up to ₹1,600 crore of post-IPO value; a fixed ₹400 crore between ₹1,600 and ₹4,000 crore; 10% at ₹4,000 crore and above.
  • If the initial public stake is below 25%, it must be raised to 25% within three years of listing.
  • A pre-IPO placement happens between DRHP and RHP, is capped by a disclosed maximum, and any convertibles must convert before the RHP.
  • Approvals are needed from the board, shareholders (special resolution for fresh issues), sector regulators where applicable, SEBI, the exchanges and relevant third parties.
