The problem it solves

Newly listed shares can be volatile. A sudden wave of selling in the first days can push the price below the IPO price, hurting the investors who have just bought and damaging confidence in the issuer. The Green Shoe Option is a mechanism in the SEBI Regulations designed to ensure price stability for a limited period — 30 days after listing — and to protect both the issuer and the investors from price volatility immediately after listing. (The name comes from the first company that used such an option, which was the Green Shoe Manufacturing Company.)

How it works

  1. Decision at filing. The decision to have a Green Shoe Option must be taken when the DRHP is filed.
  2. Decision at pricing. The actual decision to exercise the option is taken at the time of pricing the IPO.
  3. Borrowing the shares. Additional equity shares — up to 15% of the IPO size — are borrowed from a promoter or a pre-issue shareholder (the “lender”) and allotted to investors at the time of allotment. This over-allotment is the “Green Shoe Option Portion”.
  4. Stabilisation. After listing, the stabilising agent, which must be one of the BRLMs, may support the price for 30 days by buying shares from the market if the price falls below the IPO price.
  5. Returning the shares. The shares bought in the market are returned to the lenders at the end of the stabilisation period.
  6. Shortfall. If the shares bought back are fewer than those borrowed — for example, because the price rose and no support was needed — the shortfall in shares lent is met by issuing fresh equity shares to the lender.
  7. Consideration. The lender receives consideration for lending its shares.
Key facts — Green Shoe Option
  • Maximum over-allotment: 15% of the IPO size.
  • Stabilisation period: 30 days after listing.
  • Stabilising agent: one of the BRLMs.
  • Source of borrowed shares: a promoter or pre-issue shareholder.
  • The handbook notes that the option has been used in very few IPOs in India.
Worked example — two outcomes

Delta Foods’ base IPO is 100 lakh shares at ₹200. It has a Green Shoe Option, so the BRLMs over-allot 15 lakh additional shares (15% of 100 lakh), borrowed from the promoters, bringing the shares sold to investors to 115 lakh. Cash raised: 115 lakh × ₹200 = ₹230 crore, of which ₹30 crore (15 lakh shares) is held in a special account pending stabilisation.

Case A — price falls. After listing, the stock drifts to ₹185. The stabilising agent buys 15 lakh shares in the market using the ₹30 crore, at an average price of ₹190 — ₹28.5 crore. The 15 lakh shares are returned to the promoters. Any surplus left in the stabilisation account (₹1.5 crore here, the difference between ₹30 crore and ₹28.5 crore) is dealt with as the regulations prescribe — it is not a profit for the stabilising agent. Market buying has supported the price, and no new shares are issued.

Case B — price rises. The stock rises to ₹230 and the agent never needs to buy. At the end of 30 days, the 15 lakh borrowed shares cannot be returned from the market, so the issuer allots 15 lakh fresh shares to the lender (a shortfall issue) and the ₹30 crore stays with the issuer. The issuer ends up having raised ₹230 crore, and the promoters’ stake is diluted by only those fresh shares.

The mechanism has two elegant properties. If the price falls, the stabilisation buying supports it, and the borrowed shares are returned from the market. If the price rises, the issuer raises more money through fresh shares. In both cases investors have received the shares they bought at the IPO.

Practical note — why is it rarely used?

The handbook notes that the Green Shoe Option has been used in very few Indian IPOs. Reasons include procedural complexity (an escrow of the stabilisation money, a share lending arrangement, bookkeeping of stabilisation purchases), the fact that most Indian IPOs list at a premium, and the cost of a stabilising arrangement. Still, for large offerings where aftermarket volatility could damage the issuer’s reputation, it remains a useful tool.

Chapter summary

  • The Green Shoe Option allows up to 15% over-allotment, supported by borrowed shares, to stabilise price for 30 days after listing.
  • Decided at DRHP filing; exercised at pricing; run by a BRLM acting as stabilising agent.
  • Borrowed shares are returned after market purchases; any shortfall is made up by fresh issue.
  • Used in very few Indian IPOs.
