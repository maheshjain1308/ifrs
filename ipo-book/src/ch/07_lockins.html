Investors in an IPO buy from a company whose founders know it best. The regulations insist that those founders stay invested after listing, and that other early holders do not exit the moment the shares begin trading. Two mechanisms achieve this: the promoters’ contribution, which sets how much promoter stake must be put up, and lock-ins, which fix how long shares cannot be sold or transferred.

Promoters’ contribution

Promoters must have contributed not less than 20% of the post-IPO equity share capital, on a fully diluted basis. “Fully diluted” means counting all shares that would exist if every outstanding option or convertible right were exercised — so the denominator includes the IPO shares and any such shares permitted to remain outstanding (for example, employee options).

Shares that do not count

Not every share held by a promoter qualifies. The following, among others, are ineligible for computing the promoters’ contribution:

  • Non-cash shares. Shares acquired in the last three years for consideration other than cash, or from a revaluation of assets or capitalisation of intangible assets.
  • Bonus shares from paper profits. Shares issued as a bonus out of revaluation reserves or unrealised profits, or bonus shares issued on the basis of ineligible shares in the last three years.
  • Cheap shares bought recently. Shares acquired in the preceding one year at a price lower than the IPO price (subject to other requirements and exceptions).
  • Pledged shares. Shares pledged with any creditor.

The common thread is genuine commitment: shares that were bought with real money, at a real price, and are free of third-party claims. A promoter who received a large block of shares in a restructuring last year, or who pledged shares to a lender, may find the eligible pool smaller than the headline holding.

Two kinds of lock-in

The regulations impose two distinct lock-ins:

  1. a lock-in on the promoters’ contribution; and
  2. a lock-in on all pre-IPO shareholders — including the promoters, for their holding above the minimum contribution.

Promoters

  • 20% of post-IPO capital (fully diluted) is locked in for three years. The lock-in starts from the later of the date of allotment in the IPO and the date of commencement of commercial production; the three years run from the date of allotment.
  • The remaining promoter shareholding is locked in for one year from the date of allotment (or commencement of commercial production, whichever is later).
  • The promoter lock-in does not apply to companies that have no identifiable promoters.

Everyone else who held shares before the IPO

The entire pre-IPO equity share capital held by persons other than promoters is locked in for one year. Three exemptions apply:

  1. Shares allotted to employees (current or former) under a stock-option scheme before the IPO, provided disclosures are made in the offer document as required.
  2. Shares held by an employee stock option trust, or transferred to employees by such a trust on exercise, under an ESOP or ESPS.
  3. Shares held by a VCF, Category I AIF, Category II AIF or FVCI — but these shares are locked in for one year from the date of purchase by the fund. A fund that bought the shares three years ago therefore has nothing locked in; one that bought six months ago stays locked for another six months.

Anchor investors

Shares allotted to anchor investors are locked in for 30 days from the date of allotment (Chapter 10).

Worked example — who can sell on day one?

Orion Foods Ltd has 8 crore shares before its IPO: promoters hold 5 crore, a venture-capital fund (bought four years ago) holds 2 crore, and employees and others hold 1 crore (of which 0.2 crore were allotted under ESOPs). The IPO is a fresh issue of 2 crore shares and an offer for sale of 0.5 crore shares by the VC fund. Post-IPO share capital: 8 + 2 = 10 crore shares (fully diluted, ignoring unexercised options for simplicity).

Promoters’ contribution: 20% × 10 crore = 2 crore shares, locked in for three years. (Assume all 5 crore promoter shares are eligible.)
Remaining promoter shares: 5 − 2 = 3 crore, locked in for one year.
VC fund: 2 − 0.5 (sold in the OFS) = 1.5 crore remain. Because the fund bought four years ago, the one-year lock-in from purchase has expired — free to trade.
Employees/others: 1 crore pre-IPO shares; the 0.8 crore not allotted under ESOPs are locked in for one year; the 0.2 crore ESOP shares are exempt.
Public shares from the IPO: 2 + 0.5 = 2.5 crore, or 25% of 10 crore.

Freely tradable on listing day: 2.5 crore (IPO) + 1.5 crore (VC) + 0.2 crore (ESOP) = 4.2 crore shares (42% of capital), even though the public float created by the IPO is only 25%. The VC fund’s residual block is the “overhang” the market will keep in mind.

Practical note — plan the cap table before filing

Because lock-ins attach to particular shares, and because shares acquired too cheaply or too recently may be ineligible for the contribution, restructuring decisions — bonus issues, preferential allotments, pledges, intra-group transfers — should be reviewed for lock-in consequences at least twelve to eighteen months before the IPO. Inter-se transfers among promoters are permitted, and transfers to persons acquiring control under the Takeover Regulations too; the lock-in simply continues with the transferee.

Since 2019

SEBI has since shortened several of these lock-in periods — for the minimum promoters’ contribution and the excess promoter holding, and for pre-IPO non-promoter shareholders (to six months) — with the applicable period depending on the objects of the issue, and has tightened the treatment of anchor-investor lock-ins (split into 30-day and 90-day tranches). The figures in this chapter reflect the 31 July 2019 position; confirm the current periods in the ICDR Regulations. See Chapter 29.

Chapter summary

  • Promoters must contribute 20% of post-IPO fully diluted capital; non-cash, revaluation-based, recently cheap and pledged shares do not count.
  • That 20% is locked in for three years; the rest of the promoters’ shares for one year; all other pre-IPO shares for one year.
  • Exemptions: ESOP shares and ESOP-trust shares; VCF/AIF/FVCI shares (locked one year from their purchase).
  • Anchor investor shares are locked in for 30 days (at 2019).
  • No promoter lock-in applies where no promoter is identifiable.
