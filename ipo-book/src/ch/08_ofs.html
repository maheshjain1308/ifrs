What an Offer for Sale is

In an Offer For Sale (OFS), existing shareholders offer shares they already own to the public for subscription, through the offer document in the IPO. Unlike a fresh issue, no new shares are created and the company receives no money: proceeds go to the sellers. An OFS can be the whole IPO, or can sit alongside a fresh issue in a single combined offer.

Typical sellers are promoters seeking partial liquidity, and private-equity or venture-capital investors who have held their stake for several years and want to realise the value they have built. For the issuer, an OFS can be attractive because it provides a wider shareholder base and a price-discovered listing without increasing the share count. For investors, an OFS-heavy IPO sometimes signals that insiders are selling, which is why the use of proceeds and the identity of the sellers are closely read.

Who can sell? The one-year rule

A selling shareholder must have held the securities being offered for at least one year before the DRHP is filed with SEBI. If the shares to be sold arise from the conversion or exchange of convertible instruments, the holding period of the convertible instruments is counted as well. This rule prevents someone from buying shares cheaply shortly before an IPO and immediately offloading them to the public.

Key facts — exceptions to the one-year holding rule
  1. Government and infrastructure entities. OFS of securities of a government company, statutory authority or corporation, or a special-purpose vehicle set up and controlled by them in the infrastructure sector.
  2. Scheme shares. Securities acquired under a scheme approved by a High Court or a tribunal under the Companies Act, in exchange for business and invested capital that had existed for more than one year before the approval.
  3. Bonus shares. Securities issued as bonus on securities that had themselves been held for at least one year before the DRHP, subject to conditions.

Separately, a selling shareholder must not be debarred from the capital markets (Chapter 4), and the issuer’s board must approve the OFS in addition to any approvals the sellers need under their own constitutions or applicable law.

Who bears the expenses?

Section 28 of the Companies Act places the cost of an OFS on the selling shareholders. They must collectively authorise the issuer to take all actions required for the OFS and reimburse all the expenses the issuer incurs for it. When the IPO combines a fresh issue and an OFS, expenses are shared in proportion to the number of shares offered by each side.

Worked example — sharing offer expenses

Atlas Logistics Ltd launches an IPO of 5 crore shares: 3 crore shares as a fresh issue and 2 crore shares as an OFS by existing investors. Total offer expenses (BRLM fees, legal, registrar, advertising, listing fees and so on) are ₹25 crore.

Company share = 3 ÷ 5 = 60% → ₹15 crore.
Selling shareholders’ share = 2 ÷ 5 = 40% → ₹10 crore (split among the sellers according to the shares each sells).

Had the IPO been a pure OFS of 2 crore shares, the sellers would bear the whole ₹25 crore.

The practical consequence is that sellers do not receive the full IPO price per share: the net they receive is the price less their share of expenses (and any applicable taxes).

Documents the selling shareholder signs

  • The corporate authorisations for the OFS.
  • The transaction agreements: offer (issue) agreement, registrar agreement, cash escrow agreement, syndicate agreement, share escrow agreement and underwriting agreement (Chapter 15).
  • Confirmation of the disclosures in the DRHP, RHP and Prospectus that relate to them and their shares.
  • Additional due diligence certificates and documents requested by regulators or the BRLMs.

In an OFS, a separate share escrow agreement is signed among the issuer, the selling shareholders and an escrow agent, under which the offered shares are deposited — typically before the RHP is filed — so that they are available for delivery to successful bidders.

How far does the seller’s liability go?

A selling shareholder may be held liable for misstatements and omissions in the Prospectus to the extent of the disclosure made about themselves and about the shares they offer, together with whatever further liabilities they assume towards the other intermediaries in the transaction documents (for example indemnities in the offer agreement). They are not, by virtue of being sellers, liable for every statement about the company’s business — but statements about their identity, holding, title to the shares and similar matters are their responsibility.

Warning

A seller who is also a promoter has both sets of obligations. A venture fund that has a nominee on the board may be asked to give wider confirmations. Sellers should negotiate the scope of their representations and indemnities in the offer agreement with advice; the template that the issuer’s counsel circulates is a starting point, not the end of the discussion.

Chapter summary

  • An OFS lets existing shareholders sell through an IPO; the money goes to them, not the company.
  • Sellers must have held the shares for at least one year before DRHP filing, with exceptions for government/infrastructure entities, court-approved scheme shares and certain bonus shares.
  • Selling shareholders bear the OFS expenses (Section 28), shared pro rata with the company in a combined offer.
  • They sign the transaction agreements, including a share escrow agreement, and are liable for statements about themselves and their shares.
