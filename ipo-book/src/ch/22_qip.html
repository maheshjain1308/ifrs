What is a QIP?

A Qualified Institutions Placement (QIP) is the issue of eligible securities by a listed issuer to QIBs only, on a private placement basis, in accordance with the SEBI Regulations. It can also include an Offer For Sale of specified securities by the promoters or promoter group, on a private placement basis.

The QIP has become popular because it is quick. There is no draft offer document to file with SEBI for review; the issuer prepares a placement document in line with market standards and goes directly to institutional investors. A QIP can typically be completed in days rather than the months an IPO or rights issue may take, making it well suited to opportunistic fundraising or to repairing a balance sheet.

Eligibility

  1. Shareholder approval. The QIP must be approved by the shareholders by special resolution, which states that the allotment is proposed through a QIP and specifies the relevant date for pricing. No shareholder approval is needed where the QIP is an OFS by promoters or promoter group to meet the minimum public shareholding requirement.
  2. Listing history. Subject to some exceptions (such as a QIP to meet minimum public shareholding), the equity shares of the same class must have been listed on a recognised stock exchange for at least one year before the date of notice to shareholders for the special resolution.
  3. No fugitive economic offender. Neither the promoters nor the directors may be a fugitive economic offender.
  4. Six-month gap. A subsequent QIP cannot be undertaken until six months have passed since the prior QIP made under one or more special resolutions.

Who can subscribe?

Only QIBs as defined in the SEBI Regulations (Chapter 9) may participate. The Companies Act caps private placements at 200 persons in a financial year, but that cap excludes QIBs, so there is no limit on the number of QIBs that can participate in a QIP.

Pricing

A QIP must be made at a price not less than the average of the weekly high and low of the closing prices of the shares quoted on the exchange where the issuer’s shares are traded during the two weeks preceding the date on which the board decides to open the QIP. The issuer may offer a discount of up to 5% on that price, with prior shareholder approval.

For convertible instruments, the issuer may fix the relevant date as the board meeting that approves opening of the QIP or the date on which the holder of the convertible securities becomes entitled to apply for the equity shares. The price of convertible instruments may be adjusted for corporate events such as bonus issues, rights issues, share splits or consolidations and reclassification.

Worked example — computing the QIP floor price

On the day its board approves opening a QIP, Meridian Steel Ltd looks at the closing prices on its exchange over the previous two weeks:

Highest close (₹)Lowest close (₹)
Week 1210200
Week 2206196

Floor price = (210 + 200 + 206 + 196) ÷ 4 = 812 ÷ 4 = ₹203.00.
Maximum discount (5%) = ₹203 × 0.95 = ₹192.85, available only if the shareholders’ special resolution allowed a discount.
So the QIP can be priced anywhere at or above ₹192.85 if a discount was approved, or at or above ₹203 if not.

Allotment checks for a ₹400 crore QIP. Size above ₹250 crore, so at least five allottees. No single QIB can take more than 50% (₹200 crore). Mutual funds should receive at least 10% = ₹40 crore, unless they fail to subscribe it.

Disclosure and process

Unlike an IPO, the SEBI Regulations prescribe only indicative disclosure requirements for QIPs. Placement documents are typically prepared in accordance with customary market standards, together with Section 42 of the Companies Act read with the PAS Rules, which add some disclosures. Under the Companies (Amendment) Act, 2017, subscribers to a private placement must now pay the subscription money along with the application, in the prescribed form.

The SEBI Listing Regulations require the issuer to give the stock exchanges prior intimation of the board meeting at which the QIP and the determination of the issue price will be considered.

Practical note — why QIP is the workhorse for listed companies

Compared to a public follow-on offering, a QIP needs no SEBI review, lighter documents, and a shorter timetable; investors are institutions that can price risk themselves. The trade-off is that the investor base is limited to QIBs, so a very large raise may need a broader route. Because QIPs are priced off recent trading, they are best done when the share price is steady and the market is receptive.

Since 2019

The QIP regime remains in broad outline as above, though SEBI has amended detailed conditions over time (for example in respect of timelines, pricing details and the treatment of promoters’ participation). Verify the current ICDR chapter on QIPs.

Chapter summary

  • A QIP is a private placement of eligible securities by a listed issuer to QIBs only, with no limit on the number of QIBs.
  • Needs a special resolution, a one-year listing history and a six-month gap between QIPs.
  • At least 10% to mutual funds; no promoter-related allottees; at least two (up to ₹250 crore) or five allottees; no QIB above 50%.
  • Price at or above the two-week average of weekly high and low closing prices; a discount of up to 5% with shareholder approval.
