What is “listing”?

Listing means admission of securities to dealings on a recognised stock exchange. A listed company is one whose securities — including its debt securities — are listed on a recognised stock exchange. In India the principal exchanges are BSE Limited and the National Stock Exchange of India Limited (NSE).

The handbook draws a distinction that is easy to miss and important in practice:

Key facts — how an unlisted company can list
  • Equity shares (or securities convertible into equity shares): an unlisted company can list them only pursuant to a public issue.
  • Debt securities: may be listed whether issued through a public issue or by way of a private placement (Chapter 25).

This book concentrates on equity listing. Unless stated otherwise, “listing” means listing of equity shares.

What is a public issue?

Under the SEBI regulations, a public issue is an offer of equity shares, or securities convertible into equity shares, to the public. It takes one of two forms:

  • an initial public offer (IPO) by an unlisted company; or
  • a further public offer (FPO) by an already listed company.

A public issue may also be made through an Offer For Sale (OFS) of existing securities by their holders, subject to conditions (Chapter 8). Whether an offering is “public” depends on the number of potential investors to whom shares are offered and on the nature of the offer document prescribed under the Companies Act. This is the reason the Companies Act caps private placements at 200 persons in a financial year (QIBs excluded — Chapter 22): beyond that, the offer is treated as public and the full public-issue regime applies.

What can be issued to the public?

A company can issue to the public equity shares, preference shares, debt instruments, or other instruments convertible into equity. Non-convertible debt securities of an Indian company can also be issued to the public and listed. Each category has its own rule book:

Securities in dematerialised form

Every issuer making a public offer must issue securities in dematerialised (electronic) form only. Two depositories hold the records: National Securities Depository Limited (NSDL) and Central Depository Services (India) Limited (CDSL). Investors hold shares through a depository participant. In practice this means that an issuer must, before filing, arrange demat accounts for the company and ensure promoters’ holdings are already dematerialised (a condition that also appears in the fast-track rights issue criteria, Chapter 23).

The three offer documents

Three documents carry the same issuer through the IPO, becoming progressively more complete. Their abbreviations are used everywhere.

The word “red herring” comes from the cautionary red legend printed on the cover of a preliminary prospectus: the document is not yet an offer to sell. In a fixed-price issue there is no RHP, because the price is already known (Chapter 11).

The family of offerings

The IPO is the headline event, but the same vocabulary and similar machinery apply to the other routes. A Rights Issue offers new shares to existing shareholders in a fixed ratio. A Qualified Institutions Placement (QIP) issues to institutions only on a private-placement basis. A Preferential Issue allots to a chosen group. Part VI treats each in depth, and Chapter 28 helps choose among them.

Chapter summary

  • Listing is admission of securities to a recognised exchange; unlisted companies can list equity only through a public issue, but can list debt by private placement.
  • A public issue is an IPO (unlisted issuer) or FPO (listed issuer), possibly with an OFS component.
  • Public-offer securities must be dematerialised; NSDL and CDSL are the depositories.
  • The DRHP, RHP and Prospectus are successive versions of the offer document.
