An offer document is not marketing copy. It is a legal document on which investors will rely to commit money, and the law attaches serious consequences to statements in it that are false, misleading or incomplete — including omissions. This chapter summarises who can be held liable, by whom, and with what consequences.

Liability under the Companies Act

The Companies Act provides for both civil and criminal liability for misstatements in an offer document.

TypeWho is liableWhen
Civil(a) the issuer; (b) its directors; (c) its promoters; (d) every person who has authorised the issue of the Prospectus; and (e) expertsFor any inclusion or omission in a Prospectus that misleads an investor and causes damage
CriminalEvery person who authorises the issue of the ProspectusFor any misstatement made with an intention to defraud; punishable by imprisonment and/or fine

The relevant provisions include Sections 34 (criminal liability) and 35 (civil liability) of the Companies Act. Experts (such as auditors giving a report and valuers) are liable for their expert statements, which is why they must consent to being named.

SEBI’s enforcement powers

Under Section 24 of the Companies Act, SEBI has the power to take any enforcement action against an issuer undertaking an IPO and all other relevant persons for violations of the Companies Act, including breaches of Sections 34 and 35. SEBI’s action may include directions restraining persons from accessing the securities market, prohibiting dealings in securities, orders of disgorgement and monetary penalties.

Key facts — a real-world illustration

The handbook cites an instance in which a company, one of the leading players in its industry, and some of its directors were restrained by SEBI from accessing the securities market and prohibited from dealing in securities for three years on the ground of active and deliberate suppression of material information in its Red Herring Prospectus and Prospectus, so as to mislead and defraud investors. The case shows that scale and reputation offer no protection if material facts are suppressed.

Liability of intermediaries

The BRLMs must give SEBI a due diligence certificate on the contents of the offer document and can therefore be held liable by SEBI. The regulations state that their liability continues even after the IPO process is complete for matters relating to the IPO. More generally, every intermediary can be held liable under the SEBI regulations under which it is registered — for example, the registrar for lapses in processing, banks for handling of money, and auditors under accounting and professional rules.

Private action by investors

Any person or group of persons who has sustained loss or damage due to misstatements in a prospectus or fraudulent conduct may approach any court of appropriate jurisdiction to claim compensation. The right is independent of any regulatory action.

Due diligence in practice

Nobody can verify every word in an offer document, so the law and market practice rely on a structured process in which people can show they took reasonable steps. A well-run due diligence process typically includes:

  1. Kick-off and information request. A detailed request list to management covering corporate records, contracts, licences, litigation, tax, employees, intellectual property, environmental and regulatory matters.
  2. Management due diligence calls and site visits. Interviews with business heads and visits to major facilities.
  3. Legal due diligence. Review of title, approvals, material contracts, litigation and compliance by counsel, culminating in a legal opinion.
  4. Financial and tax due diligence. Work with the auditors and sometimes independent accountants, culminating in comfort letters.
  5. Verification (“bring-down”). Each factual statement in the draft is checked against a source document or certified by a responsible person.
  6. Certificates. Certificates from the issuer, directors, promoters and sellers; consents of experts; and finally the BRLMs’ due diligence certificate to SEBI.
  7. Bring-down due diligence calls. Repeated just before the RHP, pricing and closing, to catch any change.
Practical note — what a director should do before signing

A director signing the DRHP should: read the whole document, not just the sections for their function; ask about every risk factor whether it is complete; check that facts about the director (shareholding, other directorships, interests) are right; ask how related-party transactions and litigation were identified; confirm that the audit committee has reviewed the financial statements; and keep a record of questions raised and answers received. These steps do not eliminate liability, but a record of diligent inquiry matters for any later challenge.

Chapter summary

  • Misstatements and omissions in a prospectus attract civil liability (issuer, directors, promoters, authorisers, experts) and criminal liability (fraud).
  • SEBI can act against issuers and all relevant persons under Section 24 of the Companies Act, as in the case where a company and directors were barred from the market for three years.
  • BRLMs give a due diligence certificate; their liability continues after the IPO.
  • Investors can sue for compensation in the courts.
  • A structured due diligence process — legal, financial, verification, certificates and bring-downs — is the practical defence.
