Two ways to price an issue

Under the SEBI Regulations an issuer may determine the price in consultation with the lead merchant banker in one of two ways: as a fixed-price issue or through the Book Building Process.

Fixed-price issueBook-built issue
PriceFixed before the issue opens and stated in the offer documentDiscovered from investors’ bids within a price band (or at or above a floor price) after the issue closes
Investor’s choiceTake it or leave it at the stated priceBid at prices within the band; each bidder may specify up to three options
Filing stagesTwo-stage: DRHP to SEBI; Prospectus (with the price) to the RoCThree-stage: DRHP to SEBI; updated DRHP / RHP to the RoC; Prospectus (with the Issue Price) to the RoC
Price announcementPart of the offer documentFloor price or price band is advertised at least two working days before the issue opens

In practice, almost all significant IPOs in India are book-built, because price discovery lets the market, rather than the issuer and its bankers, set the valuation.

What is book building?

Book building is the process of eliciting and building up demand for the securities to be issued and assessing the price at which that demand can be satisfied. The “book” is the running record of all bids — how many shares are demanded at each price. The Book Running Lead Managers (BRLMs) maintain and analyse it.

The price band

For a book-built issue, the RHP states a Price Band: the range between the Floor Price (the lower end, below which the issue price will not be fixed and no bids will be accepted) and the Cap Price (the upper end, above which the issue price will not be fixed and no bids will be accepted). The issuer announces the floor price or price band in all the newspapers in which the pre-issue advertisement was published, at least two working days before the issue opens (Regulation 29(4)).

How a bid works

  1. During the issue period, the investor submits a Bid cum Application Form to an SCSB, a BRLM, a syndicate member, a registered broker, a CDP or an RTA, indicating the number of shares and the price within the band at which the investor wants to subscribe. Retail investors can bid only through SCSBs; from 1 January 2019 they can also bid and block funds using a UPI ID (Chapter 12).
  2. Each form allows the bidder to bid at three optional prices within the band, with a quantity against each.
  3. The intermediary enters each bid option into the exchange’s electronic bidding system as a separate bid.
  4. The Bid Amount payable (and blocked) is the highest value among the optional bids.
  5. After the Issue Price is determined, only the maximum number of shares bid for at or above the Issue Price is considered for allotment; the rest of the bids, irrespective of bid price, are automatically rejected.
Worked example — one bidder, three options

The price band is ₹190–₹200. A non-institutional bidder fills in three options:

OptionPrice (₹)SharesValue (₹)
119810019,800
219615029,400
319420038,800

Amount blocked: the highest value — option 3, ₹38,800.

If the Issue Price turns out to be ₹196: options 1 and 2 are at or above ₹196 (option 3, at ₹194, is below it and is rejected). The maximum number of shares bid at or above ₹196 is 150. The bidder is considered for up to 150 shares at ₹196 = ₹29,400 (subject to the proportionate allotment for the category); ₹9,400 (38,800 − 29,400) of the blocked money will be released.

If the Issue Price turns out to be ₹194: all three options qualify; the maximum is 200 shares at ₹194 = ₹38,800.

Who fixes the price?

After the issue closes, the BRLMs, the issuer and any selling shareholder analyse the demand generated at each price level. The issuer, in consultation with the selling shareholder (if any) and the BRLMs, then finalises the Issue Price and the number of shares to be allotted to each category of investors. The date on which this is done is the Pricing Date. Immediately after, the Underwriting Agreement is signed and the Prospectus with the Issue Price is filed with the Registrar of Companies.

Reading: At the cap price of ₹200 the offer is not fully covered (0.80×). The highest price at which the entire offer is covered is ₹198 (1.15×). The issuer could price there, maximising proceeds, or accept a lower price to leave a larger cushion: ₹194 gives 2× coverage.

Money raised: at ₹198, 100 lakh × ₹198 = ₹198 crore; at ₹194 = ₹194 crore — a difference of only ₹4 crore (about 2%). Many issuers prefer the deeper book, because a price that leaves investors with a better chance of a gain tends to support aftermarket trading.

Practical note — price discovery is a judgement, not a formula

The “highest price at which the book is covered” is a ceiling, not an instruction. Bankers look at the quality of demand (long-only institutions versus short-term traders), the distribution across categories, how many bids sit exactly at the cap, and how the book developed day by day. A book with demand concentrated in one price point or one type of investor is weaker than the same headline subscription spread widely. Retail and employees can also bid at a “cut-off” price (the final price, whatever it turns out to be) — a feature of the mechanism in practice that the handbook does not describe.

Chapter summary

  • Price is either fixed (two-stage filing) or discovered by book building (three-stage filing).
  • A price band — floor and cap — is advertised at least two working days before the issue opens.
  • A bidder can specify three options; the largest value is blocked, and only the maximum quantity bid at or above the Issue Price is considered.
  • After the issue closes, the issuer, selling shareholders and BRLMs analyse the book and fix the Issue Price and category-wise allocation.
