Questions that decide the route

  1. Is the company listed? If not, the only way to list equity is a public issue (IPO). Debt can be listed without an equity IPO.
  2. Whose money? Retail and institutions (IPO/FPO); existing shareholders (rights); institutions only (QIP); a strategic or financial investor (preferential); lenders (NCDs).
  3. How quickly? QIPs and preferential issues are fast; IPOs and FPOs are slow.
  4. Who must keep control? A rights issue protects proportionate ownership; a preferential issue can change it.
  5. How much? Very large amounts may need public markets or rights; small, targeted amounts suit a preferential issue.
  6. What does the market prefer now? Institutional appetite favours QIPs; weak markets favour rights with promoter support or debt.

Scenarios

Six situations and the natural route

1. A profitable, unlisted manufacturer wants growth capital and an exit for a PE investor. IPO with a fresh issue and an OFS (Chapters 5 and 8). Check the track-record route.

2. A listed company needs ₹800 crore within six weeks to take advantage of an acquisition. QIP: no SEBI review, institutional demand, pricing off the two-week average (Chapter 22).

3. A listed company with a stable, widely held base needs ₹3,000 crore to reduce debt; the promoter wants to keep their percentage. Rights issue — fast-track if it meets the thirteen conditions (Chapter 23).

4. A listed company wants a strategic partner to take 10% and sign a long-term supply agreement. Preferential issue to the partner; check pricing floor, six-month rule and 15-day window (Chapter 24).

5. An unlisted infrastructure company wants a source of long-term funds, not equity. Public issue or private placement of listed NCDs, with a rating and a debenture trustee (Chapter 25).

6. A developer owns ₹1,000 crore of leased office parks and wants to release capital. Sponsor a REIT: public issue of units; check the 80/20 rule and sponsor eligibility (Chapter 26).

Combining routes over time

Routes are not exclusive: they form a ladder across a company’s life. A company may begin with private equity, list its NCDs, then execute an IPO; after listing, it may use a QIP for opportunistic growth, a rights issue to repair its balance sheet, a preferential issue to bring in a strategic partner, and an OFS by promoters to build public shareholding. Each step builds on the disclosure and governance infrastructure created for the IPO.

Practical note — the cost of delay

Market windows are narrow. A company that expects to need capital in the next 12–24 months should complete the lengthy preparation (governance, restated accounts, cap-table clean-up) in advance, so that it can choose a route when conditions are right instead of being forced by need.

Chapter summary

  • The right route depends on whether the company is listed, who the investors are, speed, control and amount.
  • Equity can be listed only through a public issue; debt can be listed privately or publicly.
  • Rights issues preserve control; QIPs are fastest for large institutional demand; preferential issues serve strategic investors.
  • A company’s capital-raising typically uses several routes over time.
