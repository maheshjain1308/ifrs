The disclosure standard

Disclosure standards in India for the DRHP, RHP and Prospectus are substantially at par with other securities markets in the world, with certain additional requirements. The overriding rule is simple: every disclosure must be true and adequate to enable investors to make a well-informed decision about the investment.

Key areas covered

Alongside the customary sections — business and industry, risk factors (internal and external), financial statements, management’s discussion and analysis of results, and management — the SEBI Regulations require specific disclosures that Indian practitioners watch closely:

  1. details of promoters, promoter group members and group companies (Chapter 6);
  2. comprehensive details of the use of proceeds and how the amount has been ascertained;
  3. the build-up of the capital structure, the shareholding pattern and shareholder arrangements;
  4. pending litigation relating to the issuer, subsidiaries, promoters, directors and group companies; and
  5. government approvals relevant to the business of the issuer and its material subsidiaries.

Objects of the issue

Investors are entitled to know exactly what their money will be used for and why the amount is what it is. The regulations require detailed disclosure of use of proceeds and the basis on which the amount has been determined — in certain cases supported by third-party reports.

If the objects include…The offer document must disclose
Repayment of loansThe name of each lender, brief terms and conditions and the amount outstanding; and a certificate from the statutory auditor that the loan was used for the purpose for which it was availed
Funding working capitalA detailed assessment of historical and projected working-capital utilisation and requirements
Acquisition or downstream investment in a subsidiaryDisclosures about the subsidiary or target company and the manner in which funds will be infused into it

Vague objects are a common reason for SEBI queries. “General corporate purposes” may be listed, but investors and SEBI expect the specific items to carry most of the weight.

Directors and key management personnel

The offer document must describe each director and key management person fully:

  • age, address, occupation, qualifications, nature of directorship or position held, term of directorship or date of appointment (for key management), role in the issuer, director identification number, other directorships, past experience and remuneration for the last fiscal;
  • the number of securities held by directors in the issuer;
  • changes in directors in the last three years with the reasons; and
  • interests of directors in the issuer, including any agreements with the issuer.

Litigation disclosure

Litigation disclosure extends to the issuer, its subsidiaries, its promoters and its directors, and covers all pending:

  1. criminal proceedings;
  2. actions by statutory and regulatory authorities;
  3. claims relating to direct and indirect taxes (in a consolidated manner); and
  4. other litigation considered material under the board’s materiality policy.

Pending litigation involving group companies that may have a material impact on the issuer must also be disclosed. And any disciplinary action — including penalties — imposed by SEBI or the stock exchanges against the promoters in the last five fiscals, including outstanding action, must be disclosed.

Practical note — the materiality policy

Since 2018 the regulations have favoured a materiality-based approach: instead of listing every dispute, the board adopts a policy defining which matters are material, and the document discloses those individually plus aggregated summaries of the rest. The policy must be approved before filing and should be defensible — typically a quantitative threshold (a percentage of net worth, turnover or profit) plus a qualitative test (disputes that could affect the business or reputation).

Financial statements

Periods

The issuer must include audited and consolidated (if relevant) financial statements for each of the three fiscals preceding the IPO and the latest stub period, if required, so that the financial statements are not more than six months old from the date of the offer document and the Issue Opening Date.

Restatement

The historical audited accounts must be restated to ensure consistency of presentation, disclosure and accounting policies across all periods. For example, if a new accounting policy was adopted in the last year, earlier years are recast as if it had always applied.

Pro forma financial statements

Where there has been a material acquisition or divestment — including of a business of the issuer or its subsidiaries, and any deemed disposal — after the latest period for which financial information is disclosed, the offer document must include pro forma financial statements. Materiality is judged against 20% or more of turnover, net worth or profit before tax in the issuer’s latest consolidated financial statements.

Subsidiaries on the website

The separate audited financial statements for the past three full fiscals of the issuer and each material subsidiary (one contributing 10% or more of turnover, net worth or profit before tax in the annual consolidated statements for the respective year) must be made available on the issuer’s website.

Worked example — the six-month staleness test

Polaris Pharma Ltd has audited financials to 31 March 2019. It files its DRHP in August 2019 and expects to open the issue in February 2020.

The financial statements must not be more than six months old at the date of the offer document and at the Issue Opening Date. March 2019 statements go stale on 30 September 2019 — before the planned opening. Polaris must therefore add a stub period (say, six months to 30 September 2019, audited and restated). Statements to 30 September 2019 remain valid up to 31 March 2020, so an issue opening in February 2020 is fine. If delays push the opening beyond 31 March 2020, it needs a further update to 31 December 2019 or later.

Financial staleness is one of the most common causes of timetable slippage — plan audit and restatement work backwards from the target listing window.

When can SEBI reject an offer document?

SEBI may reject an offer document where it has reasonable grounds to believe that the adequacy and quality of disclosures are not satisfactory, or that an investor may not be able to assess the risks associated with the issue. Examples of grounds are vague objects, a complex business model and unidentified promoters. A company whose draft offer document is rejected is barred from accessing the capital markets for at least one year.

Since 2019

Disclosure expectations have grown, for example more granular disclosure of key performance indicators, related-party transactions, and the basis for price band and valuation; and the use of general corporate purposes and unidentified acquisitions as objects has been capped. Check the current Schedule VI-type content requirements in the ICDR Regulations. See Chapter 29.

Chapter summary

  • Offer-document disclosure must be true and adequate; standards are close to global ones, with some Indian extras.
  • Objects must be specific, with lender details and auditor certificates for loan repayments and a detailed assessment for working capital.
  • Litigation, directors, promoters, group companies and approvals are all covered; litigation uses a board materiality policy.
  • Three fiscals plus any stub period, restated, and not more than six months old; pro forma statements for material acquisitions or divestments (20% test).
  • SEBI can reject documents with inadequate disclosure, triggering at least a year’s bar.
